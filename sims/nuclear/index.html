The big question

Why does the chain stop at lead‑206?

⚖Two forces at war

The strong force glues nucleons together but only reaches its nearest neighbours. The electric force pushes every proton apart across the whole nucleus. In a giant nucleus like uranium, repulsion nearly wins — so it keeps shedding pieces.

∿The neutron cushion

Neutrons add glue but no charge, diluting proton repulsion. Heavy nuclei need extra neutrons, so the stable ratio climbs: U‑238 → N/Z ≈ 1.59 down to Pb‑206 → 1.51. Each step steers toward that "valley of stability."

★A magic number: 82

Nucleons fill shells like electrons do. Closed shells sit at the magic numbers 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126. Lead has Z = 82 — a closed, magic proton shell — making it exceptionally tightly bound.

⧉Even loves even

Pb‑206 has even protons (82) and even neutrons (124). Nucleons pair up, and even‑even nuclei gain extra "pairing energy" — one more reason this lead sits so comfortably.

⬛Lead is the end of the road

Beyond bismuth (Z = 83) there are no stable nuclei — repulsion finally wins. Lead is the heaviest element with stable isotopes, so all three natural series end on lead.

ΣThe bookkeeping

From ²³⁸U to ²⁰⁶Pb: 8 α + 6 β⁻. Mass: 238 − 8×4 = 206. Charge: 92 − 8×2 + 6×1 = 82. Exactly stable lead.

The sibling process

β⁺ vs β⁻ vs electron capture

▸β⁻ (too many neutrons)

A neutron becomes a proton: n → p + e⁻ + ν̄. Z rises by 1. This is what drives the uranium chain in Tab ①.

▸β⁺ (too many protons)

A proton becomes a neutron: p → n + e⁺ + ν. Z falls by 1. Needs energy > 1.02 MeV to create the positron.

⤵Electron capture

Same destination, different route: the nucleus grabs an inner electron, p + e⁻ → n + ν. Proton‑rich nuclei often do both.

✦The annihilation signature

Every positron soon meets an electron and both vanish into two 511 keV gamma photons flying exactly opposite. That back‑to‑back pair is what makes PET possible.

🏥Where it is used

F‑18 is bonded to glucose (FDG) and injected. Busy cells — tumours, brain, heart — soak up sugar and glow on the scan. Also used: C‑11, N‑13, O‑15.

⚙Made fresh, nearby

With a 110‑minute half‑life, F‑18 can't be stored. A cyclotron near the hospital makes it the same morning it's used.

In one sentence

Everything rolls toward iron

↗Fusion (light nuclei)

Joining light nuclei climbs the steep left slope toward stronger binding, releasing energy. Powers the Sun and stars, the hydrogen bomb, and experimental reactors like ITER.

⛰Iron: the bottom of the well

Fe‑56 / Ni‑62 are the most tightly bound nuclei. You can't release energy by fusing or splitting them — which is why fusion inside a dying star stops at iron and triggers a supernova.

↙Fission (heavy nuclei)

Splitting a heavy nucleus lets the fragments slide back up toward iron, releasing energy. Powers nuclear reactors and fission weapons — and it's the slow, natural version of this that drives Tab ①.
