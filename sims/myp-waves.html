01What is a wave?

DefinitionA wave is a disturbance that transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter.

Drop a stone into a pond. Ripples spread outwards, but a floating leaf only bobs up and down: it is not carried away. The water particles oscillate (vibrate) about fixed positions and pass the energy on to their neighbours.

Try itA “Mexican wave” in a stadium: each person only stands up and sits down, but the wave travels all the way around.

Example 5.1A water wave has a frequency of 2.0 Hz and a wavelength of 0.75 m. Calculate its speed.
v = f λ = 2.0 × 0.75 => 1.5 m/s

λ = frac{v}{f} = frac{340}{680} => 0.50 m

Common mistakeReading a “wavelength” off a displacement–time graph. The distance between peaks on a time graph is the period, not the wavelength.
Extension · phaseTwo points on a wave are in phase if they move in exactly the same way at the same time (e.g. two crests). Points half a wavelength apart are in antiphase: when one is at a crest the other is at a trough.

07Wavefronts and how waves behave

A wavefront is a line joining points on a wave that are in the same phase, such as all the crests. A ray is an arrow showing the direction the wave travels; it is always at 90° to the wavefronts.

BehaviourWhat happensSee it in…
ReflectionThe wave bounces off a barrier. Angle of incidence = angle of reflection.Light (mirrors), Sound (echoes), Water
RefractionThe wave changes speed when it enters a new medium, so it may change direction. Frequency stays the same.Light (glass, lenses), Sound, Water (depth)

05Refraction of light

DefinitionRefraction is the change in direction of light as it passes from one medium into another, caused by a change in speed.
Light going…SpeedBends
into a denser medium (air → glass)decreasestowards the normal (r < i)
into a less dense medium (glass → air)increasesaway from the normal (r > i)
along the normal (i = 0°)changesdoes not bend
Why does it bend?Imagine a toy car rolling at an angle from a smooth floor onto carpet. The wheel that reaches the carpet first slows down first, so the car swings round towards the normal. The same happens to a wavefront of light.

The frequency stays the same when light refracts (it is set by the source). Because v = fλ, a lower speed means a shorter wavelength.

Definition: refractive indexThe refractive index (n) of a material is the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum to the speed of light in that material:
n = frac{speed of light in a vacuum}{speed of light in the material} = frac{c}{v}
It tells you how many times slower light travels in the material than in a vacuum, and therefore how strongly the material bends light. Equivalently, for light entering the material from air (or a vacuum), n = sin i ÷ sin r (Snell’s law).
  • n has no unit (it is a ratio of two speeds).
  • n is always 1 or more: n = 1 for a vacuum (and almost exactly 1 for air), because nothing travels faster than light in a vacuum.
  • A larger n means light is slower, the material is optically denser, and light bends more.
Example: glass has n = 1.5, so light travels 1.5 times slower in glass than in a vacuum: 3.0 × 10⁸ ÷ 1.5 = 2.0 × 10⁸ m/s.

A material with a higher refractive index is optically denser: light travels more slowly in it and bends more on entering it. Optical density is not the same as mass density: cooking oil (n ≈ 1.47) floats on water, yet it is optically denser than water (n = 1.33).

Common mistake: rainbows are not TIRThe reflection at the back of a raindrop is an ordinary partial reflection, not total internal reflection. The light meets the back of the drop at about 40°, which is less than the critical angle of water (about 49°), so most of the light passes out of the drop and only a few per cent is reflected. That is why rainbows are faint. The sky between the two bows looks darker (Alexander’s band) because no light from either bow is sent there.
Combining prismsA prism always bends light towards its base. Two prisms base to base bend rays towards each other, like a convex (converging) lens; two prisms apex to apex bend rays apart, like a concave (diverging) lens. A real lens behaves like many prisms whose angle changes smoothly, so all the rays meet at one focus. Two identical prisms placed the opposite way round recombine the spectrum into white light.
Why study dispersion? (MYP links)
  • Criterion A (knowing and understanding): dispersion is evidence that white light is a mixture of wavelengths, and that the refractive index (and so the speed of light in glass) depends on wavelength. Expect questions like “Explain why violet light is deviated more than red light.” A full-mark answer links three ideas: different wavelengths → different speeds in glass → different refractive indices → different amounts of bending.
  • Criterion B and C (inquiring, processing): you can investigate how the angle of deviation depends on the angle of incidence (see the Prism lab), plot a graph and find the minimum deviation.
  • Criterion D (reflecting on the impacts of science): dispersion has real consequences. Chromatic aberration makes coloured fringes in cheap lenses, so cameras use combinations of lenses (achromatic doublets). Spectroscopes split starlight to find which elements stars are made of, and how fast they move. In optical fibres, dispersion spreads out light pulses, which limits internet speed.
  • Global context (orientation in space and time; scientific and technical innovation): Newton’s prism experiments (1666) changed how people understood colour, and spectroscopy later revealed the chemistry of the Universe.

10Diffraction of light

DefinitionDiffraction is the spreading out of a wave as it passes through a gap or around the edge of an obstacle.
  • Diffraction is greatest when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength.
  • If the gap is smaller than the wavelength, the waves that get through still spread out as semicircles, but they carry very little energy: most of the wave is reflected by the barrier. This is why the small holes in a microwave oven door block the microwaves (λ ≈ 12 cm) but let light through.
  • The wavelength, frequency and speed do not change when a wave diffracts.
  • Light has a tiny wavelength (≈ 0.0005 mm), so it only spreads noticeably through very narrow slits. Sound (λ ≈ 1 m) diffracts through doorways: you can hear round a corner but not see round it.
  • Diffraction is evidence that light is a wave.
See it yourselfLook at a street light through a fine cloth, or tilt a CD under a lamp: the colours come from light diffracting from the tiny tracks (a diffraction grating).
Extension · single-slit patternLight through a narrow slit forms a bright central band with dimmer bands either side. A narrower slit or a longer wavelength (red rather than blue) makes the pattern wider. Try it in the Diffraction sub-tab.

12Lighting design

Good lighting lets people see clearly and comfortably without wasting energy. Designers use ideas from this chapter.

IdeaWhy it matters
Illuminance (lux)The amount of light falling on each square metre of a surface. Typical targets: corridor 100 lux, classroom 300–500 lux, science lab 500 lux, art room or detailed work 500–750 lux.
UniformityThe lowest illuminance ÷ the average. Values of about 0.6 or more mean no desk is left in a dark patch.
Diffuse reflectionPale, matte walls and ceilings scatter light all round the room, so it feels brighter and more even.
GlareRegular reflection from glossy surfaces (whiteboards, screens, polished desks) sends bright patches into people's eyes. Bare, very bright lamps in the line of sight also cause glare.
Reflectors and diffusersCurved reflectors direct light down onto the work surface; frosted covers (diffusers) spread it and reduce glare.
Distance from the lampLight spreads out, so illuminance falls quickly with distance: doubling the distance gives about one quarter of the illuminance (inverse-square law, Extension).
Energy and daylightLEDs give the most light per watt. Using daylight from windows, with blinds to stop glare, saves energy.

    01What is sound?

    DefinitionSound is a longitudinal mechanical wave produced by a vibrating object. It transfers energy through a medium (solid, liquid or gas) as a series of compressions and rarefactions.
    • Every sound starts with a vibration: guitar strings, vocal cords, a loudspeaker cone, a tuning fork.
    • Sound is mechanical: it needs particles to pass the vibration on, so it cannot travel through a vacuum.
    • Sound is longitudinal: particles vibrate back and forth parallel to the direction the sound travels.
    The bell-jar experimentAn electric bell rings inside a glass jar. As a vacuum pump removes the air, the sound gets quieter and quieter until it can hardly be heard, although you can still see the hammer striking the bell. Light passes through the vacuum, but sound does not. This shows that sound needs a medium.

    02How sound travels

    TermMeaning in a sound wave
    CompressionA region where air particles are pushed close together: higher pressure than normal.
    RarefactionA region where air particles are spread apart: lower pressure than normal.
    Wavelength λThe distance from one compression to the next (or one rarefaction to the next).
    How we hearSound waves make the eardrum vibrate. Three tiny bones (the ossicles) pass the vibrations to the cochlea, where hair cells turn them into electrical signals that travel along the nerve to the brain.

    03Properties and graphs of sound

    A microphone turns pressure changes into a voltage, which an oscilloscope shows as a wave trace. Even though sound is longitudinal, the trace looks like a transverse wave: it is a graph of pressure (or displacement) against time.

    v = f λ

    f

    λ

    T = 4 × 0.5 ms = 2.0 ms = 0.0020 s f = frac{1}{T} = frac{1}{0.0020} => 500 Hz

    Why a piano and a violin sound differentBoth can play the same note (the same frequency) at the same loudness (the same amplitude).     Tone or note: what is the difference?

    DefinitionsA tone is a sound of one single frequency. Its waveform is a smooth sine wave. A tuning fork or a signal generator makes an almost pure tone.
    A note is a musical sound with a definite pitch that has a name (for example C, or A = 440 Hz). It is made of a fundamental frequency, which sets the pitch and the name, plus overtones at 2f, 3f, 4f… The overtones give the note its timbre.
    ToneNote
    Frequencies presentOnly one: fThe fundamental f + overtones 2f, 3f, …
    WaveformSmooth sine waveRepeating, but with a more complex shape
    PitchSet by fSet by the fundamental f (the overtones do not change the pitch)
    Timbre“Plain”, pure soundRich; different for each instrument
    ExampleTuning fork at 440 HzA violin or piano playing A (440 Hz)
    RememberEvery note contains a tone (its fundamental), but a note is more than one tone. Two instruments playing the same note have the same fundamental (same pitch) but different overtones (different timbre). Noise has no fundamental at all, so it is neither a tone nor a note. Try the Tone vs note buttons in the Pitch, loudness & timbre sub-tab.
    Extension · decibelsLoudness is measured in decibels (dB). Every +10 dB means the sound intensity is 10 times greater. Whisper ≈ 30 dB, conversation ≈ 60 dB, busy road ≈ 80 dB, rock concert ≈ 110 dB, pain threshold ≈ 130 dB. Long exposure above about 85 dB can permanently damage the hair cells in the cochlea.

    Musical soundNoise

    The physics and the social viewPhysically, the difference is about the waveform: regular and periodic (music) or irregular (noise). Socially, “noise” simply means unwanted sound.     07Speed of sound

    MediumApproximate speed / m/s
    Air (0 °C)331
    Air (20 °C)343
    Water1 500
    Wood4 000
    Steel5 900
    Vacuum0 (no sound)
    What affects the speed of sound?1. The state of the medium. Generally fastest in solids, slower in liquids, slowest in gases. In solids the particles are closely linked by strong forces, so a vibration passes on quickly.
    2. Temperature. In a gas, warmer particles move faster and pass on the vibration more quickly: in air the speed rises by about 0.6 m/s for every 1 °C.
    3. Density (for the same kind of material). Speed depends on how stiff the material is compared with how dense it is. For gases at the same temperature, a denser gas carries sound more slowly: sound is about 3 × faster in helium than in air, which is why helium makes your voice sound squeaky.
    v ≈ 331 + 0.6 T
    v
    speed of sound in air (m/s)
    T
    air temperature (°C)
    Common mistake“Denser materials always carry sound faster” is not right. Steel is denser than water but much stiffer, so stiffness wins. When you compare gases, or the same material, a higher density gives a lower speed. Pressure on its own does not change the speed of sound in air.
    Example 7.1Calculate the speed of sound in air at 35 °C.
    v = 331 + 0.6 × 35 = 331 + 21 => 352 m/s
    Example 7.2You see lightning and hear thunder 3.0 s later. Calculate how far away the storm is (v = 340 m/s).
    distance = speed × time = 340 × 3.0 => 1 020 m (about 1 km) # Light arrives almost instantly (3 × 10⁸ m/s), so the delay is all due to the sound.

    08Reflection of sound: echoes

    DefinitionAn echo is a reflected sound that is heard separately from the original sound. Sound obeys the law of reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection.

    Hard, smooth surfaces (cliffs, walls, tiles) reflect sound well. Soft, rough or porous surfaces (curtains, carpets, foam, people) absorb sound.

    2d = v × t
    d
    distance to the reflecting surface (m)
    v
    speed of sound (m/s)
    t
    time from making the sound to hearing the echo (s)
    Remember the 2The sound travels to the wall and back, so the total distance is 2d. Forgetting the factor of 2 is the most common error in echo questions.
    Example 8.1A student claps 85 m from a cliff. Calculate the time before the echo is heard (v = 340 m/s).
    t = frac{2d}{v} = frac{2 × 85}{340} => 0.50 s
    Minimum distance for an echoThe ear can only hear two sounds as separate if they are at least about 0.1 s apart. In that time sound travels 340 × 0.1 = 34 m, so the wall must be at least 34 ÷ 2 = 17 m away to hear a distinct echo.
    Conditions for hearing an echo
    1. The reflecting surface must be at least about 17 m away, so the echo arrives at least 0.1 s after the original sound.
    2. The surface should be large compared with the wavelength (otherwise the sound diffracts round it).
    3. The surface should be hard and smooth (rock, brick, concrete) so it reflects rather than absorbs.
    4. The sound must be loud and short (a clap or shout), and the surroundings quiet enough to hear the weaker echo.
    Example 8.2A girl shouts towards a cliff and hears the echo 1.6 s later. Calculate how far away the cliff is (v = 340 m/s).
    2d = v × t = 340 × 1.6 = 544 m => d = 272 m
    Example 8.3A boy stands between two cliffs and claps. He hears the first echo after 0.40 s and the second after 0.60 s. Calculate the distance between the cliffs (v = 340 m/s).
    d_1 = frac{340 × 0.40}{2} = 68 m d_2 = frac{340 × 0.60}{2} = 102 m => distance between the cliffs = 68 + 102 = 170 m

    Echo sounding and SONARShips time ultrasound echoes from the sea bed or fish shoals: d = vt ÷ 2.
    Medical ultrasoundEchoes from boundaries between tissues build an image of a fetus or organ.
    EcholocationBats and dolphins find prey and avoid obstacles in the dark.
    Parking sensorsUltrasound sensors in car bumpers beep faster as the echo time shortens.
    Seismic surveysEchoes from rock layers deep underground locate oil, gas and water.
    Crack detectionAn early echo from inside a metal rail or weld reveals a hidden crack.

    09Reverberation

    DefinitionReverberation is the persistence of a sound in an enclosed space because of many repeated reflections arriving less than 0.1 s apart, so they blend together and the sound seems to “ring on”.
    EchoReverberation
    ReflectionsOne clear reflectionMany overlapping reflections
    Time gapat least 0.1 sless than 0.1 s
    Surface distanceat least 17 m awayUsually a closer, enclosed space
    Heard asA separate repeatA prolonged, “boomy” sound

    Too much reverberation makes speech unclear (an empty hall, a swimming pool); too little makes music sound “dead”. Concert halls are designed with the right reverberation time using:

    • Absorbing materials: curtains, carpets, padded seats, acoustic foam panels, fibreboard ceilings.
    • Shaped or angled walls and ceilings that scatter sound evenly.
    • Audiences: people absorb sound, so a full hall sounds different from an empty one.

    10Refraction and diffraction of sound

    Sound changes speed when it moves into air of a different temperature (or into a different medium), so it changes direction: it refracts. Sound bends towards the region where it travels more slowly (cooler air).

    DaytimeNight-time
    Air near the groundWarm (heated by the ground)Cool (the ground cools quickly)
    Sound bendsUpwards, away from the groundDownwards, back towards the ground
    ResultDistant sounds are harder to hearDistant sounds (trains, traffic) carry much further
    Other examplesSound also refracts on entering water from air, and wind blowing in one direction bends sound towards the ground downwind. Sound travelling into water speeds up, so it bends away from the normal.

    Diffraction of sound: hearing round corners

    DiffractionDiffraction is the spreading out of waves when they pass through a gap or round the edge of an obstacle. It is greatest when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength.
    • Speech has wavelengths from about 0.3 m to 3 m (λ = v/f = 340/1000 = 0.34 m; 340/150 = 2.3 m). A doorway is about 1 m wide, so speech diffracts strongly through it and round the edge of a wall.
    • So a person behind the wall can still hear someone talking, even though there is no straight line between them.
    • Light has a wavelength of only about 0.0000005 m, far smaller than a doorway, so it hardly diffracts at all: the person behind the wall cannot see the speaker.
    • Low-pitched sounds (long λ) diffract more than high-pitched sounds (short λ). That is why a voice or music heard round a corner sounds muffled: the bass gets through better than the treble.
    RememberDiffraction changes the direction of the waves only. The wavelength, frequency and speed stay the same. Try the Diffraction of sound sub-tab: drag the listener behind the wall and listen.

    11Ultrasound and sonography

    Ultrasound is sound with a frequency above 20 kHz. Medical scanners use 2–15 MHz. A transducer sends short pulses into the body and detects the echoes. Each time the pulse reaches a boundary between two tissues, part of it reflects. A computer uses the echo times to calculate the depth of each boundary and builds up an image (a sonogram).

    depth = frac{v × t}{2}
    v
    speed of ultrasound in soft tissue ≈ 1 540 m/s
    t
    time for the echo to return
    Example 11.1An echo from a baby's skull returns 0.080 ms after the pulse is sent. Calculate the depth of the skull (v = 1 540 m/s).
    depth = frac{v × t}{2} = frac{1540 × 0.000080}{2} => 0.062 m = 6.2 cm
    Use of ultrasoundHow it works
    Pre-natal scansImages of a fetus; ultrasound is non-ionising, so it is much safer than X-rays.
    Organ scansImages of the heart (echocardiogram), liver, kidneys; detects gallstones and tumours.
    Doppler scansThe frequency shift of echoes from moving blood shows blood-flow speed.
    Kidney stone treatmentHigh-energy ultrasound breaks stones into small pieces (lithotripsy).
    IndustryDetecting cracks in metal and welds; cleaning jewellery and delicate parts.
    Why the gel?Air between the probe and the skin would reflect almost all the ultrasound before it entered the body. A water-based gel removes the air gap so the ultrasound passes into the body.
    Comparison · ECG is not soundAn ECG (electrocardiogram) is often confused with an ultrasound heart scan, but it uses no sound at all. Electrodes on the skin detect the tiny electrical signals made by the heart muscle each time it beats. The trace is a graph of voltage against time.

    Ultrasound (echocardiogram)ECG
    What is detectedReflected sound waves (echoes)Electrical signals from the heart
    Energy sent into the bodyYes: pulses of ultrasoundNo: it only “listens” electrically
    OutputA moving picture of the heart's structureA graph of voltage against time (heart rhythm)
    Used to checkValves, chambers, blood flowHeart rate and rhythm, heart attacks
    Both are waves on a screen, but only one is a sound wave. An ECG trace is a useful graph: you can find the heart rate from the time between beats, just like finding T on an oscilloscope.

    12Echolocation and sonar

    Echolocation: bats, dolphins and some whales send out ultrasound clicks and listen for the echoes. From the time delay they judge the distance to an object; from the strength and direction of the echo, its size and position. Short wavelengths of ultrasound let bats detect insects only a few millimetres across.

    SONAR (SOund Navigation And Ranging): ships send ultrasound pulses down through water and time the echoes from the sea bed or from shoals of fish and submarines.

    Example 12.1A ship's sonar pulse returns from the sea bed after 1.2 s. Sound travels at 1 500 m/s in sea water. Calculate the depth of the sea.
    d = frac{v × t}{2} = frac{1500 × 1.2}{2} => 900 m
    Example 12.2A bat emits a 50 kHz pulse (v = 340 m/s). Calculate the wavelength, and suggest why bats use such high frequencies.
    λ = frac{v}{f} = frac{340}{50 000} => 0.0068 m = 6.8 mm # The wavelength is about the size of an insect, so the insect reflects the sound clearly. A longer wavelength would diffract round it.

    How to study this picture

    01Water waves transfer energy, not water

    Key ideaA wave transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter. A cork floating on a pond bobs up and down as waves pass, but it is not carried along with the waves.
    • Water waves on the surface are treated as transverse waves: the water surface moves up and down, at right angles (perpendicular) to the direction the wave travels.
    • Water waves are mechanical: they need a material (the water) to travel through.
    • The energy of the wave comes from whatever disturbed the water: the wind, a boat, a stone, or an earthquake on the sea floor.
    Transverse waveLongitudinal wave
    VibrationPerpendicular (at 90°) to the direction of travelParallel to the direction of travel
    PatternCrests and troughsCompressions and rarefactions
    ExamplesWater surface waves (model), light and all EM waves, waves on a ropeSound, P-waves in earthquakes, pushes along a slinky
    Extension · water particles really move in circlesLook closely and a real water particle at the surface moves in a small circle: up and forward at a crest, down and back in a trough. So water waves are really a mix of transverse and longitudinal motion. The circles get smaller with depth; in shallow water they flatten into ellipses. Each particle still ends up where it started, so no water is carried along. Turn on “real orbits” in the Graphs & the cork sub-tab.

    Example 3.1A dipper vibrates at 8.0 Hz. On a photo, 10 wavefronts span 15 cm. Calculate the wave speed.
    10 wavefronts have 9 gaps, so λ = frac{15}{9} = 1.67 cm = 0.0167 m v = f × λ = 8.0 × 0.0167 => 0.13 m/s
    Example 3.2An ocean wave has a period of 8.0 s and travels at 12 m/s. Calculate its wavelength.
    f = frac{1}{T} = frac{1}{8.0} = 0.125 Hz λ = frac{v}{f} = frac{12}{0.125} => 96 m
    Exam tip: counting wavefrontsCount the gaps, not the lines. 10 crests in a row are 9 wavelengths apart.

    04Measuring waves in a ripple tank

    • Frequency: read it from the signal generator driving the motor, or count the waves passing a point in 10 s and divide by 10.
    • Wavelength: freeze the pattern with a stroboscope or take a photo with a ruler in view. Measure across several wavelengths (for example 10) and divide, to reduce the uncertainty.
    • Speed: either use v = fλ, or time one wavefront travelling a measured distance: v = distance ÷ time.
    Watch outThe pattern on the screen is magnified, because the light spreads out from the lamp. Put a ruler in the tank (or measure the shadow of a ruler of known length) so you can scale your measurements.
    Speed and depthWater waves travel more slowly in shallow water. Friction with the bottom slows them down. In a ripple tank, a 1 cm depth gives a speed of about 0.3 m/s; in the deep ocean, waves can travel faster than a jet plane.

    05Reflection of water waves

    Law of reflectionWhen water waves hit a barrier, they bounce back. The angle of incidence = angle of reflection, both measured between the ray and the normal (a line at 90° to the barrier). Equivalently, the angle between the wavefronts and the barrier is the same before and after.
    • The wavelength, frequency and speed stay the same, because the wave is still in the same water at the same depth.
    • Only the direction changes.
    • Circular waves from a point reflect as if they came from an image point behind the barrier, just like light in a plane mirror.
    • A concave (curved-in) barrier focuses plane waves to a point, the focus, just like a concave mirror focuses light.
    Where you see itSea walls reflect waves back out to sea. Engineers often build sloping or stepped walls, or piles of rocks, that absorb some of the energy rather than reflect it, because strong reflected waves can scour the beach in front of the wall.

    06Refraction of water waves

    RefractionRefraction is the change in direction of a wave when it changes speed, because it moves into a different medium. For water waves, moving from deep water into shallow water is like moving into a new medium.
    Deep → shallow waterWhat happensWhy
    SpeedDecreasesFriction with the bottom slows the wave
    FrequencyStays the sameSet by the source (the dipper); waves cannot appear or vanish at the boundary
    WavelengthDecreases: wavefronts get closer togetherλ = v/f: smaller v with the same f
    DirectionBends towards the normal (if the waves arrive at an angle)The part of each wavefront in shallow water slows down first, so the wavefront swings round
    Head-on: no bendingIf the waves hit the boundary along the normal (wavefronts parallel to the boundary), the whole wavefront slows down at the same moment. The wavelength still gets shorter, but the direction does not change.
    frac{sin i}{sin r} = frac{v_{1}}{v_{2}} = frac{λ_{1}}{λ_{2}} # the same rule as Snell’s law for light
    Example 6.1Waves of frequency 10 Hz travel at 0.30 m/s in deep water. In shallow water their wavelength is 1.8 cm. Calculate the wavelength in deep water and the speed in shallow water.
    λ_{deep} = frac{v}{f} = frac{0.30}{10} = 0.030 m = 3.0 cm v_{shallow} = f × λ = 10 × 0.018 => 0.18 m/s # f stays 10 Hz in both
    Real coastsAs ocean waves approach a beach, the water gets shallower, so they slow down and turn until they arrive almost parallel to the shore, whatever direction they came from. Around a headland the waves bend in towards it, concentrating energy there: headlands are eroded while sand collects in the calmer bays. Try the “coast” mode in the Refraction sub-tab.

    07Diffraction of water waves

    DiffractionDiffraction is the spreading out of waves when they pass through a gap or move past the edge of an obstacle.
    • Diffraction is greatest when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength: the waves spread out into almost semicircles.
    • If the gap is much wider than the wavelength, the waves go straight through with only a little spreading at the edges, leaving a calm “shadow” on either side.
    • If the gap is smaller than the wavelength, the waves still spread out as semicircles, but very little energy gets through, so they are weak.
    • The wavelength, frequency and speed do not change when waves diffract. Only the direction (and the spreading) changes.
    Why it mattersHarbours are protected by walls with narrow entrances. Waves diffracting through the gap and round the ends of the walls can still reach the boats, so engineers design the size and shape of the entrance to keep the water calm. Diffraction is also why you can hear round a corner (sound λ ≈ 1 m, similar to a doorway) but cannot see round it (light λ ≈ 0.0000005 m).

    08Interference (Extension)

    Extension · superpositionWhen two sets of waves overlap, their displacements add. Where a crest meets a crest, the water rises twice as high: constructive interference. Where a crest meets a trough, they cancel and the water stays flat: destructive interference. Two dippers vibrating together make a pattern of lines of calm water (nodal lines) between lines of large waves.
    constructive: path difference = 0, λ, 2λ, … destructive: path difference = frac{1}{2}λ, 1frac{1}{2}λ, …
    This pattern is strong evidence that something is a wave: light shows exactly the same pattern in Young’s double-slit experiment.

    09Ocean waves and tsunamis

    • Most ocean waves are made by the wind transferring energy to the water surface. Stronger winds blowing for longer over a longer distance make bigger waves.
    • A tsunami is a series of waves usually caused by an earthquake that suddenly lifts or drops the sea floor. Landslides and volcanic eruptions can also cause them.
    • In the deep ocean a tsunami has a huge wavelength (100–200 km), a small amplitude (often less than 1 m), and travels at up to about 200 m/s (700 km/h). Ships at sea hardly notice it.
    • As it reaches shallow water near the coast, it slows down and its wavelength gets shorter, so the energy is squeezed into a smaller space and the wave grows much taller, sometimes more than 10 m.
    Extension · wave speed and depthWhen the wavelength is much longer than the depth (as for tsunamis), the speed depends only on the depth d:
    v = sqrt{g d} # g = 9.8 m/s², d = depth in m at 4000 m: v = sqrt{9.8 × 4000} ≈ 198 m/s at 10 m: v = sqrt{9.8 × 10} ≈ 9.9 m/s
    Fairness and developmentIn December 2004 an earthquake off Sumatra caused a tsunami that killed about 230 000 people in 14 countries. The waves took up to two hours to reach Sri Lanka and India, but there was no warning system in the Indian Ocean then, so most people had no time to escape. An Indian Ocean warning system was set up afterwards. Today, sensors on the sea floor and buoys detect tsunamis and send warnings by satellite. The people most at risk are often those in low-lying coastal communities, fishing villages and informal housing, who may have fewer resources for warning systems, sea defences or rebuilding. Explore this in the Tsunamis sub-tab.

    10All waves behave alike

    Reflection, refraction and diffraction follow the same rules for every type of wave. This is why the ripple tank is such a useful model: what you see with water waves tells you what sound and light do.
