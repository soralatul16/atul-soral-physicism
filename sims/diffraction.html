8.2 · Waves spread through gaps

Diffraction

Diffraction is the spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap or around an obstacle. How much it spreads depends on the width of the gap compared with the wavelength — the effect is greatest when the gap is about one wavelength wide.

AS Level · Core

When a wave meets a gap or the edge of an obstacle, it bends and spreads into the region beyond. This spreading is called diffraction. How much it spreads depends on the gap width a compared with the wavelength λ — drag both sliders and watch.

Ripple tank — plane waves meet a gap

Straight (plane) wavefronts travel in from the left towards a barrier with a single gap. Narrow the gap towards the wavelength and the circular spreading becomes dramatic.

When the gap is much wider than the wavelength, the wave passes almost straight through with only slight spreading at the edges.

Diffraction is the spreading of a wave into the region beyond a gap or obstacle as it passes the edge.

Each point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary circular wavelets (Huygens' idea). At a gap, the wavelets near the edges have no neighbours to cancel their sideways spreading — so the wave curves into the "shadow" region.

The rule

  • a >> λ — little diffraction; a near-straight beam with slightly curved edges.
  • a ≈ λ — maximum diffraction; near-semicircular wavefronts fill the space beyond.
  • a < λ — still wide spreading, but little energy gets through.

Wavelength, frequency and speed do not change on diffraction — only the shape and direction of the wavefronts.

Water waves — the ripple tank

The classic demonstration: plane water waves from a vibrating bar meet a barrier with an adjustable gap. With a wide gap the waves pass almost straight through; as the gap is narrowed towards the wavelength, the waves spread into clear circular arcs beyond the barrier. This is the one you must be able to describe in the exam.

Sound

Sound diffracts around doorways and walls because its wavelength (a few cm to a few m) is comparable to everyday openings — so you hear someone in the next room even when you cannot see them.

Light

Light's wavelength is tiny (∼400–700 nm), so ordinary gaps are far too wide to show spreading. A very narrow slit (comparable to the wavelength) is needed — the basis of the single- and double-slit experiments in 8.3.

Microwaves

Microwaves (λ ≈ 3 cm) diffract through a few-centimetre gap between metal plates — easily shown with a transmitter and receiver on the bench.

Radio

Long-wave radio (λ up to hundreds of metres) diffracts around hills and buildings, giving reception in valleys; short-wavelength TV and mobile signals diffract far less.

Practice · Exam-style
Plane water waves of wavelength 2 cm approach a gap in a barrier. The gap width is slowly reduced from 10 cm to 2 cm.
(a) State what is meant by diffraction. [1]
Diffraction is the spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap (or around an obstacle/edge) into the region beyond.
(b) Describe how the appearance of the waves beyond the gap changes as the gap is narrowed to 2 cm. [2]
At 10 cm the waves pass almost straight through with only slight curving at the edges. As the gap narrows towards 2 cm (≈ one wavelength) the waves spread much more, emerging as near-semicircular arcs that fill the space beyond the gap.
(c) State what happens to the wavelength and the speed of the waves as they pass through the gap. [1]
Both the wavelength and the speed stay the same — diffraction changes only the shape and direction of the wavefronts, not the frequency, wavelength or speed.
✓ Nice — that is exactly the ripple-tank description examiners look for.
AS Level · Core

Diffraction at this level is qualitative — no formula is required. Marks come from correct reasoning about the gap-to-wavelength ratio and from precise description. Work through these the way an examiner marks them.

Worked Example 1
A singer stands behind a stage pillar 0.4 m wide. A member of the audience can still clearly hear the singing but cannot see the singer. Explain this using diffraction. Take the speed of sound as 340 m s−1 and a typical singing frequency of 340 Hz.

Wavelength of the sound:

λ = v / f = 340 / 340 = 1.0 m

The sound wavelength (1.0 m) is larger than the pillar (0.4 m). The obstacle is small compared with the wavelength, so the sound diffracts strongly around it and reaches the listener — they hear it clearly.

Light has a wavelength of only ∼5 × 10−7 m, which is enormously smaller than the pillar. The pillar is a huge obstacle compared with the light's wavelength, so light diffracts negligibly — it travels in straight lines, casting a shadow, and the singer cannot be seen.

Mark points: calculate λ of sound; compare λ with obstacle size for both sound and light; conclude strong diffraction for sound, negligible for light.
Worked Example 2
In a ripple tank, plane waves of wavelength 1.5 cm are diffracted at a gap. State and explain the gap width that would give the greatest spreading of the waves beyond the gap.

Maximum diffraction (greatest spreading) occurs when the gap width is approximately equal to the wavelength.

gap ≈ λ ≈ 1.5 cm

At this width the gap behaves almost like a single point source, so the emerging wavefronts are nearly semicircular and spread through the widest angle. A much wider gap would let the waves pass through as a near-straight beam; a much narrower gap would spread the waves but transmit very little energy.

Worked Example 3
Long-wave radio (λ ≈ 1500 m) and VHF television (λ ≈ 2 m) are both broadcast towards a town hidden behind a range of hills about 300 m high. Explain which signal gives better reception in the town.

The hills act as an obstacle/edge roughly 300 m in size.

For long-wave radio, λ (1500 m) is larger than the hills, so the waves diffract strongly around and over them and reach the town — good reception.

For VHF television, λ (2 m) is far smaller than the hills, so diffraction is negligible; the signal is largely blocked and casts a "radio shadow" — poor reception.

Conclusion: long-wave radio gives better reception because its wavelength is comparable to (or larger than) the obstacle, so it diffracts far more.
AS Level · Core

The commonest ways candidates lose diffraction marks. Fix these now.

"Diffraction changes the wavelength of the wave."
Wavelength, frequency and speed are all unchanged.

Diffraction only alters the shape and direction of the wavefronts as they spread. The source still sets the frequency, and the medium still fixes the speed, so λ = v/f is unchanged.

"A wider gap gives more diffraction because more wave gets through."
A narrower gap (closer to λ) gives more spreading.

More energy passes through a wide gap, but it stays as a near-straight beam. The angular spreading is greatest when the gap is about one wavelength wide, not when it is widest.

"Only light diffracts" (or "only water waves diffract").
All waves diffract — sound, water, light, microwaves, radio.

Whether the spreading is noticeable depends on the gap/obstacle size compared with that wave's wavelength. The physics is identical for every wave.

"We can't see round corners, so light doesn't diffract at all."
Light does diffract — but its wavelength is tiny.

Everyday gaps are millions of wavelengths wide, so light's spreading is far too small to notice. Through a slit comparable to λ, light's diffraction becomes clearly visible.

"Diffraction and refraction are the same bending."
They are different causes of bending.

Refraction is bending due to a change of speed when entering a new medium. Diffraction is spreading at a gap or edge with no change of medium or speed.

"The gap just lets a straight slice of the wave through."
The edges of the gap make the wave curve outwards.

Secondary wavelets at the gap edges have no neighbours to cancel their sideways spread, so the wavefront curves into the region beyond — this is the heart of diffraction.
