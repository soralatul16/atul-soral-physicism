01What is energy?

Energy is a quantity that tells us what can happen in a system. Energy is never used up. When something happens, energy is transferred from one store to another.

UnitEnergy is measured in joules (J). 1 kJ = 1000 J and 1 MJ = 1 000 000 J. Lifting an apple (about 1 N) through 1 m transfers about 1 J.

To describe any event, ask three questions: Where is the energy at the start? How does it move? Where is it at the end?

02The seven energy stores

The syllabus asks you to state that energy may be stored as:

StoreWhat has energy in this store?Examples
KineticAny moving object. More mass or more speed means more energy.A moving car, a flying ball, wind
Gravitational potentialAn object raised in a gravitational field. Higher means more energy.Water behind a dam, a book on a shelf
ChemicalSubstances that can release energy in chemical reactions.Food, fuels, batteries, explosives
Elastic (strain)An object that is stretched, squashed, bent or twisted.A stretched spring, a drawn bow, a bungee cord
NuclearThe nucleus of an atom. Released by fission or fusion.Uranium fuel, hydrogen fusing in the Sun
ElectrostaticCharges that attract or repel each other.A charged balloon, a thundercloud
Internal (thermal)The particles of a substance (their kinetic and potential energy). A hotter object has more.Hot tea, warm brakes, the air in a room
Common mistakeLight, sound and electricity are not stores. They are ways of transferring energy. The energy they carry ends up in a store, often the internal (thermal) store of whatever absorbs it.

03Energy transfer pathways

Energy moves between stores in four ways. You need to describe examples of each.

PathwayHow it worksExamples
Mechanical work
by forces		A force moves an object through a distance.Lifting a box, a car engine pushing the car, friction slowing a sledge
Electrical work
by electric currents		A current transfers energy around a circuit.A battery running a motor, mains supply to a kettle
HeatingEnergy moves from a hotter object to a cooler one (conduction, convection, radiation).A flame heating a pan, a hot drink cooling down
WavesElectromagnetic waves (light, infrared, microwaves), sound waves and other waves carry energy.Sunlight warming the ground, a loudspeaker, seismic waves

04Describing energy transfers

Use this sentence frame to get full marks:

Answer frameEnergy is transferred from the [store] of [object] to the [store] of [object] by [pathway]. Some energy is wasted to the thermal store of the surroundings.

Worked descriptions

A battery-powered torch.

chemical (battery)electrical worklamplight + heatingthermal (surroundings)

Energy is transferred from the chemical store of the battery by an electric current to the lamp. The lamp transfers energy by light, which is useful, and by heating, which is wasted. In the end all the energy is in the thermal store of the surroundings.

An archer firing an arrow.

elastic (bow)mechanical workkinetic (arrow)

The bent bow pushes on the arrow as it springs back. The force does work on the arrow, so energy moves from the elastic store of the bow to the kinetic store of the arrow. A little is wasted as sound and heating.

A car braking.

kinetic (car)work done by frictionthermal (brakes)heatingthermal (surroundings)

A hydroelectric power station has several stages: GPE of water → kinetic of falling water → kinetic of turbine → electrical work → homes. Every stage wastes some energy, mostly to the thermal store of the surroundings.

The Sun warming the Earth. Fusion in the Sun's core releases energy from its nuclear store. Electromagnetic waves carry it to Earth, where it increases the thermal store of the ground.

05Conservation of energy

Principle of conservation of energyEnergy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only be transferred from one store to another. The total energy of a closed system stays constant.
total energy in = useful energy out + wasted energy out

Wasted energy and dissipation

Wasted energy is not destroyed. It spreads out (dissipates) into the surroundings, usually to the thermal store of the air. It is then too spread out to be useful. Friction, air resistance, electrical resistance and sound are the usual causes.

Example 5.1A motor is supplied with 500 J. It lifts a load, gaining 300 J of GPE, and produces 30 J of sound. How much energy is transferred by heating?
# energy in = sum of energy out 500 = 300 + 30 + E_{thermal} E_{thermal} = 500 − 300 − 30 => 170 J
Exam tipWhen asked to explain why something slows down or stops, name the force (friction or air resistance), say it does work, and say where the energy goes: “to the thermal store of the surroundings”.

06Kinetic energy

E_k = frac{1}{2} m v^2
Ek
kinetic energy, in joules (J)
m
mass, in kilograms (kg)
v
speed, in metres per second (m/s)

Because speed is squared, doubling the speed makes Ek four times bigger, and tripling it makes Ek nine times bigger. Doubling the mass only doubles Ek.

Rearranged forms
v = sqrt{frac{2 E_k}{m}}
m = frac{2 E_k}{v^2}
Example 6.1Calculate the kinetic energy of a 1200 kg car moving at 15 m/s.
E_k = frac{1}{2} m v^2 = frac{1}{2} × 1200 × 15^2 = frac{1}{2} × 1200 × 225 => 135 000 J (135 kJ)
Example 6.2A 60 g tennis ball has 27 J of kinetic energy. Calculate its speed.
# First convert the mass: 60 g = 0.060 kg v = sqrt{frac{2 E_k}{m}} = sqrt{frac{2 × 27}{0.060}} = sqrt{900} => 30 m/s
Example 6.3A 900 kg car speeds up from 10 m/s to 20 m/s. Calculate the increase in its kinetic energy.
# Before: E_{k1} = frac{1}{2} × 900 × 10^2 = 45 000 J # After: E_{k2} = frac{1}{2} × 900 × 20^2 = 180 000 J # Increase: ΔE_k = 180 000 − 45 000 => 135 000 J # Twice the speed gives four times the energy.
Common mistakesSquaring (½mv) instead of only v. Using grams instead of kilograms. Using km/h instead of m/s (divide km/h by 3.6). Working out the change in speed first and then squaring it: you must find each Ek separately and subtract.

07Change in gravitational potential energy

ΔE_p = m g Δh
ΔEp
change in GPE, in joules (J)
m
mass, in kilograms (kg)
g
gravitational field strength, 9.8 N/kg on Earth
Δh
change in height, in metres (m)
Rearranged forms
Δh = frac{ΔE_p}{m g}
m = frac{ΔE_p}{g Δh}

Only the change in height matters, not the path taken. Climbing a ramp or a ladder to the same height gives the same gain in GPE.

Example 7.1A 2.5 kg bag is lifted onto a shelf 1.8 m higher. Calculate the gain in GPE.
ΔE_p = m g Δh = 2.5 × 9.8 × 1.8 => 44.1 J
Example 7.2A 65 kg climber gains 25 480 J of GPE. Calculate how high the climber went.
Δh = frac{ΔE_p}{m g} = frac{25 480}{65 × 9.8} = frac{25 480}{637} => 40 m
Example 7.3An astronaut lifts a 10 kg rock by 1.5 m on the Moon (g = 1.6 N/kg). Calculate the gain in GPE and compare it with Earth.
# On the Moon: ΔE_p = 10 × 1.6 × 1.5 => 24 J # On Earth: ΔE_p = 10 × 9.8 × 1.5 = 147 J # The same lift on Earth gives about six times more GPE.

08Problems linking GPE and kinetic energy

When an object falls freely (with no air resistance), all the GPE it loses goes to its kinetic store:

# GPE lost = kinetic energy gained m g h = frac{1}{2} m v^2 # divide both sides by m (mass cancels): g h = frac{1}{2} v^2 v = sqrt{2 g h}

Mass cancels, so a heavy and a light object dropped from the same height land at the same speed (with no air resistance).

Example 8.1A 0.40 kg ball is dropped from 5.0 m. Air resistance can be ignored. Calculate its speed just before it hits the ground.
# Step 1: GPE lost ΔE_p = 0.40 × 9.8 × 5.0 = 19.6 J # Step 2: by conservation of energy, this all becomes kinetic energy E_k = 19.6 J # Step 3: find the speed v = sqrt{frac{2 × 19.6}{0.40}} = sqrt{98} => 9.9 m/s
Example 8.2The same ball loses 4.6 J to air resistance on the way down. Calculate its landing speed.
E_k = 19.6 − 4.6 = 15.0 J v = sqrt{frac{2 × 15.0}{0.40}} = sqrt{75} => 8.7 m/s
Example 8.3A 0.20 kg ball is thrown straight up at 12 m/s. Calculate the maximum height it reaches (ignore air resistance).
# Kinetic energy at the start: E_k = frac{1}{2} × 0.20 × 12^2 = 14.4 J # At the top it is all GPE: 14.4 = 0.20 × 9.8 × h h = frac{14.4}{0.20 × 9.8} => 7.3 m

09Multi-stage problems

For a complex example (Supplement), follow the energy one stage at a time. The useful output of each stage is the input to the next.

Example 9.1A 600 kg roller-coaster car starts at rest at the top of a 40 m drop. It then climbs a 25 m hill. Between the top and the hill, 60 000 J is transferred to the thermal store by friction. Calculate its speed at the top of the hill.
# Stage 1: GPE lost going from 40 m down to 25 m (Δh = 15 m) ΔE_p = 600 × 9.8 × 15 = 88 200 J # Stage 2: subtract the wasted energy E_k = 88 200 − 60 000 = 28 200 J # Stage 3: find the speed v = sqrt{frac{2 × 28 200}{600}} = sqrt{94} => 9.7 m/s
Example 9.2A nuclear power station. The fuel releases 1000 MJ. The reactor wastes 100 MJ, the turbine wastes 500 MJ and the generator wastes 40 MJ. Determine the electrical energy output.
# Reactor: thermal store of steam = 1000 − 100 = 900 MJ # Turbine: kinetic store of turbine = 900 − 500 = 400 MJ # Generator: electrical output = 400 − 40 => 360 MJ

10Sankey diagrams

A Sankey diagram is a flow diagram drawn to scale. Use these rules to interpret one:

  • The width of each arrow is proportional to the amount of energy.
  • Useful energy carries straight on. Wasted energy usually bends away, downwards.
  • The input width equals the total width of all the outputs (conservation of energy).
  • In a multi-stage diagram, energy is wasted at every stage, so the useful arrow gets narrower each time.
Efficiency (useful for reading Sankey diagrams)
efficiency = frac{useful energy output}{total energy input} × 100% # For the power station above: efficiency = frac{360}{1000} × 100% => 36%
Example 10.1On a Sankey diagram the input arrow is 50 mm wide and stands for 200 J. The useful arrow is 15 mm wide. Calculate the useful energy.
# Find the scale: 1 mm = frac{200 J}{50} = 4 J useful energy = 15 × 4 => 60 J
