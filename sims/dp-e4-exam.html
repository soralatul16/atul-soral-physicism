1 · How the marks actually work

E.4 is the same for SL and HL. Marks are lost through too few decimal places in mass-defect calculations, mixing up the moderator and the control rods, MeV–J conversions, and vague answers such as “the reactor uses neutrons to make energy”.

How a DP markscheme thinks

  • One mark per marking point (MP1, MP2…). “Max 4” means more points are listed than marks available.
  • Error carried forward (ECF): a wrong energy per fission used correctly in a power calculation still earns the later marks.
  • “Show that”: give the steps and one more significant figure than the printed answer.
  • OWTTE: the idea counts, but key terms (“absorbs neutrons”, “slows neutrons”) must be there.
  • In “Discuss” and “Evaluate”, marks need both sides and a judgement.

Command terms you will meet

  • Define: the precise meaning (binding energy, mass defect, critical mass).
  • Distinguish: the difference (spontaneous vs neutron-induced fission).
  • Outline / Describe: brief vs detailed account (energy transfers in a plant).
  • Explain: each “because” is usually a mark (role of the moderator).
  • Calculate / Determine: working, then an answer with a unit.
  • Sketch: the binding-energy curve or a Sankey diagram, with labels.
  • Discuss / Evaluate: benefits and limitations, then a conclusion.

Do and don’t in the exam hall

✔ DO

  • Keep six decimal places of u until the subtraction.
  • Check both A and Z balance in every fission equation, including the neutrons.
  • Convert MeV to J with 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J before using power in watts.
  • Say the moderator slows neutrons and the control rods absorb them.
  • Use real examples in waste and climate answers (Chernobyl, Fukushima, Onkalo).

✘ DON’T

  • Don’t say binding energy is energy “stored in” the nucleus: it is the energy needed to separate it.
  • Don’t say the moderator controls the reaction rate: that is the control rods.
  • Don’t forget the incoming neutron on the left of the equation.
  • Don’t say fission products are safe once the reactor stops: decay heat continues.
  • Don’t confuse critical mass with “the mass that explodes”.

1APaper 1A: multiple choice

About 2 minutes per question. E.4 multiple-choice questions test balancing equations, reading the binding-energy curve, the parts of a reactor, and quick energy and power estimates.

Fast checks

  • Equations: add the top numbers (A) and the bottom numbers (Z) on each side. ¹₀n counts as A = 1, Z = 0.
  • Energy: 1 u ↔ 931.5 MeV; 200 MeV per U-235 fission = 3.2 × 10⁻¹¹ J.
  • Fissions per second = power ÷ energy per fission.
  • Curve: energy is released when the products are higher on the BE/A curve.

Traps the wrong options use

  • Swapping the jobs of moderator, control rods and coolant.
  • “Fission products have too many protons” (they have too many neutrons).
  • Forgetting the incoming neutron when counting released neutrons.
  • Total binding energy vs binding energy per nucleon.
  • “k slightly above 1” for steady operation (it must be exactly 1).

Worked example

²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴⁰₅₄Xe + X + 2 ¹₀n. Identify X.

  1. Mass numbers: 235 + 1 = 140 + A + 2, so A = 94.
  2. Proton numbers: 92 + 0 = 54 + Z + 0, so Z = 38 (strontium).
  3. X = ⁹⁴₃₈Sr. The traps: A = 96 (forgot the incoming neutron) or Z = 36 (subtracted the neutrons from Z).

1BPaper 1B: data-based questions

20 marks, about 40 minutes. E.4 data includes binding-energy tables, reactor start-up readings, neutron energies after collisions and the activity of stored waste. Expect uncertainties, log graphs and “does it agree?”.

Uncertainty in a sum

Q = ΣA(EB/A) for products − ΣA(EB/A) for reactants.

  • Adding or subtracting: add the absolute uncertainties.
  • Each A × (EB/A) term has uncertainty A × Δ(EB/A).
  • Values to 3 s.f. (e.g. 8.29 MeV) carry ±0.005 MeV.

Exponential data

If N = N₀eλt (growth) or A = A₀e−λt (decay), a graph of ln N against t is a straight line with gradient ±λ.

  • A constant ratio between equal time steps shows the data are exponential.
  • Doubling time or half-life = ln 2 / λ.

“Does it agree?”

  1. Calculate the value and its absolute uncertainty.
  2. Write the range, e.g. (184 ± 2) MeV means 182 to 186 MeV.
  3. It agrees only if the other value lies inside that range. Say so.

Worked example: energy from binding energies, with uncertainty

EB/A: U-235 7.59 MeV, Xe-140 8.29 MeV, Sr-94 8.59 MeV. Estimate Q for ²³⁵U + n → ¹⁴⁰Xe + ⁹⁴Sr + 2n, with its uncertainty.

  1. Q = 140 × 8.29 + 94 × 8.59 − 235 × 7.59 = 1160.6 + 807.5 − 1783.7 = 184 MeV (free neutrons have zero binding energy).
  2. ΔQ = (140 + 94 + 235) × 0.005 = 2.3 MeV, so Q = (184 ± 2) MeV.
  3. The value from atomic masses, 184.7 MeV, lies in this range, so the two methods agree.

2Paper 2: structured and extended answers

E.4 Paper 2 questions usually chain: equation → energy per fission → fissions per second → fuel used per year → efficiency. Explanations are marked on the number of correct links.

Weak answer vs top answer

Explain the role of the moderator in a thermal fission reactor. [3]

1 / 3

The moderator controls the reaction so that it does not get out of control.

That is the job of the control rods. Only “part of the reactor” style credit, if any.
3 / 3

Neutrons released by fission are fast (about 2 MeV). The moderator slows them down by elastic collisions with light nuclei such as hydrogen in water or carbon in graphite MP1. Slow (thermal) neutrons are much more likely to be absorbed by U-235 and cause further fission MP2, so the chain reaction can be sustained with low-enriched fuel, and the moderator itself absorbs few neutrons MP3.

What it does, how, and why that matters for the chain reaction.

Discuss the issues involved in the long-term storage of high-level nuclear waste. [4]

1 / 4

Waste is radioactive and dangerous, so it is buried underground.

One point, no time scale, no second side, no judgement.
4 / 4

Spent fuel contains nuclides such as Pu-239 with half-lives of about 24 000 years, so it stays more hazardous than uranium ore for about 100 000 years MP1. A deep geological repository must stop groundwater carrying nuclides into the environment for that whole time, despite earthquakes or climate change MP2. Future generations must be warned, and the waste guarded against misuse, long after today’s languages and governments have changed MP3. On the other hand, the volume is small and well-sited repositories (e.g. Onkalo in Finland) use multiple barriers, so the risk can be made very low, but it cannot be proven to be zero MP4.

Time scale, physical risk, social and ethical issues, and a balanced judgement.

The calculation chain

  1. Δm = m(before) − m(after), in u. Q = Δm × 931.5 MeV.
  2. Q in J = MeV × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³.
  3. Fissions per second = Pthermal / Q.
  4. Mass per second = (fissions per second / NA) × 0.235 kg.
  5. Efficiency = Pelectrical / Pthermal.

Three ways to get Q

  • From masses: Q = (mbefore − mafter)c².
  • From binding energies: Q = ΣEB(products) − ΣEB(reactants).
  • From EB/A: multiply each by its A first. Free neutrons have EB = 0.

7 · Before the exam

Equations for E.4

E = m c² 1 u = 931.5 MeV c⁻² = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg EB = Δm c² Δm = Zmp + Nmn − mnucleus Q = ΣEB(products) − ΣEB(reactants) efficiency = useful power outtotal power in

Constants and numbers to know

  • mp = 1.007276 u · mn = 1.008665 u · me = 0.000549 u
  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, so 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J · NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹
  • ≈ 200 MeV per U-235 fission; ≈ 8 × 10¹³ J per kg of U-235
  • Natural uranium ≈ 0.7% U-235; reactor fuel ≈ 3–5%
  • Fission neutrons ≈ 2 MeV; thermal neutrons ≈ 0.025 eV
  • Peak EB/A ≈ 8.8 MeV near A = 56–62; U-235 ≈ 7.6 MeV

Final checklist

  • I can define binding energy and mass defect, and calculate them.
  • I can sketch the EB/A curve and use it to explain fission and fusion.
  • I can balance a fission equation and distinguish spontaneous from induced fission.
  • I can calculate Q in three ways and say where the energy goes.
  • I can explain chain reactions, k and critical mass.
  • I can explain the moderator, control rods, coolant, heat exchanger and shielding.
  • I can do power, fuel-use and efficiency calculations, and draw a Sankey diagram.
  • I can discuss fission products, waste storage and fission’s role in climate change.
