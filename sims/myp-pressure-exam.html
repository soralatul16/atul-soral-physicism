1 · How the marks actually work

Each criterion is scored on a level from 0 to 8, strand by strand. In this unit, most lost marks come from unit conversions (cm² → m², g → kg) and from explanations that skip the "why", such as why pressure increases with depth or why upthrust exists.

Command terms in this unit

  • Define: the exact meaning, with formula and unit. "Pressure is the force acting normally per unit area."
  • State: the law or condition, with no reasoning (Pascal's law, Archimedes' principle).
  • Explain: link cause → physics → effect (area ↓, so pressure ↑, so it pierces).
  • Draw: force diagrams with labelled arrows from the centre, drawn to relative size.
  • Calculate: equation, then conversion, then substitution, then answer with unit.
  • Evaluate / Discuss: benefits and limitations, then a justified conclusion.

Do and don't in the exam hall

✔ DO

  • Convert first: 1 cm² = 10⁻⁴ m², 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m², 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³, 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³.
  • Use weight in newtons (W = mg), not mass, for force.
  • Say "upthrust = weight of fluid displaced" word for word.
  • For floating: upthrust = weight, not "upthrust is bigger".
  • For hydraulics: use areas; if you're given diameters, square the ratio.
  • Add atmospheric pressure when the question asks for total pressure.

✘ DON'T

  • Don't say an object "loses mass" in water. It loses apparent weight.
  • Don't say upthrust increases with depth once the object is fully submerged.
  • Don't say heavy objects sink. Compare densities instead.
  • Don't think the shape or width of a container changes liquid pressure. Only depth does.
  • Don't say Bernoulli means "fast air pulls". Explain the pressure difference.
  • Don't give relative density a unit.

AKnowing & Understanding

Every checklist item, plus atmospheric pressure, manometers, barometers and Boyle's law. (g = 9.8 N/kg unless the question gives 10 N/kg.)

Density & relative density

ρ = mVkg/m³ (or g/cm³)
RD = density of substancedensity of waterno unit
RD = weight in airloss of weight in water

Water: 1000 kg/m³ = 1.0 g/cm³. An RD of 2.7 means 2.7 times as dense as water (aluminium).

Pressure in solids

p = FA1 Pa = 1 N/m²
  • Pressure is the force acting normally (at 90°) per unit area.
  • Small area → high pressure: knives, nails, needles, stilettos.
  • Large area → low pressure: tank tracks, skis, snowshoes, wide tyres, camels' feet.

Liquid pressure

p = h ρ gh = depth in m
ptotal = patm + hρgpatm ≈ 101 kPa
  • It increases with depth and density only, not with the container's shape or volume.
  • It acts equally in all directions at a given depth, because particles collide randomly.
  • Dams are thicker at the base, where the pressure is greatest.

Pascal's law & hydraulics

Pascal's law: pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally to every part of the fluid.

F₁A₁ = F₂A₂
A₁ d₁ = A₂ d₂the same volume of fluid moves

Lifts, brakes and presses multiply force, but the effort moves further. Liquids must be incompressible: air in brakes is dangerous.

Upthrust & Archimedes

Archimedes' principle: a body wholly or partly immersed in a fluid experiences an upthrust equal to the weight of fluid displaced.

U = ρfluid Vdisplaced g
apparent weight = W − U

Why upthrust exists: the pressure on the bottom face (deeper) is greater than on the top face, which gives a resultant upward force.

Floating, sinking, hovering

Applications: ships (hollow, low average density), submarines (ballast tanks), hydrometers, hot-air balloons, Plimsoll lines.

Atmospheric & gas pressure

  • Air pressure ≈ 101 kPa at sea level; it falls with altitude.
  • Barometer: 760 mm of mercury balances the atmosphere (p = hρg).
  • Manometer: the height difference h gives the excess gas pressure, hρg.
  • Straws, suction cups and syringes rely on atmospheric pressure pushing.
p₁V₁ = p₂V₂Boyle's law (fixed mass, constant T)

Bernoulli's principle

As the speed of a fluid increases, its pressure decreases.

lift force = Δp × A
  • Wings: faster air above, lower pressure above, giving lift.
  • Spray bottles: fast air over the tube lowers the pressure, and atmospheric pressure pushes liquid up.
  • Storms: fast wind over a roof gives low pressure above, so the roof lifts.
  • Train platforms: fast air near the train gives low pressure, so people are pushed towards it.

Weak answer vs top-band answer

Question: Explain why a steel ship floats although steel is denser than water. [3]

1 / 3

Because the ship is big and the water pushes it up.

Size isn't the reason; it doesn't mention density, displacement or the balance of forces.
3 / 3

The hull is hollow and full of air, so the ship's average density is less than water ✓. It displaces a large volume of water, and by Archimedes' principle the upthrust equals the weight of water displaced ✓. It sinks until upthrust = weight, then floats in equilibrium ✓.

Density, Archimedes, and the force balance.

BInquiring & Designing

Be ready to plan: factors affecting floatation, the relative density of a solid or liquid, or how liquid pressure changes with depth.

B i · Research question

Top band

How does the depth below the surface (0.10–0.50 m) affect the pressure measured by a pressure sensor in tap water at 20 °C?

Then explain it: the deeper the sensor, the greater the weight of water above it per unit area (p = hρg).

B ii · Hypothesis

Top band

If the depth doubles, then the water pressure will double (p ∝ h), giving a straight line through the origin with gradient ρg ≈ 9.8 kPa/m, because p = hρg and ρ and g are constant.

B iii · Variables & sufficient data

TypeVariableHow

CVLiquid & temperatureSame water; density depends on both
CVStill waterWait for ripples to stop and the reading to settle

B iv · Method for RD of a solid

  1. Hang the object from a newton-meter (±0.1 N); record its weight in air, W₁.
  2. Lower it fully into water, not touching the sides; record W₂.
  3. Repeat 3 times; take the means.
  4. RD = W₁ / (W₁ − W₂).

Safety

  • Wipe spills (slip hazard).
  • Handle glassware carefully.
  • Keep electrical sensors away from water.

CProcessing & Evaluating

Show a sample calculation for each processed column, choose axes that give a straight line, and link the gradient to the physics.

C i · Present & process

  • Units in the headers; consistent decimal places.
  • Processed columns: mean, upthrust (W₁ − W₂), RD, p = F/A.
  • Write one full sample calculation.
  • Graph: title, labelled axes with units, sensible scale, line of best fit.

C ii · Interpret

Top band

Pressure rose from 0.98 kPa at 0.10 m to 4.90 kPa at 0.50 m; doubling h doubled p, so p ∝ h. The gradient is 9.8 kPa/m = ρg, giving ρ = 1000 kg/m³, the density of water.

DReflecting on the impacts of science

The statement of inquiry links physics to personal and cultural expression: dance, sport, festivals and design. Criterion D asks you to weigh the consequences.

The four strands

  • D i: explain how the science solves a problem (hydraulic brakes, Plimsoll line).
  • D ii: discuss and evaluate implications (economic, social, environmental, ethical, political, cultural).
  • D iii: precise language ("upthrust", not "the water's force").
  • D iv: full, consistent references.

Good D topics for this unit

  • Large dams: power and irrigation against displacement
  • Ocean plastics: float or sink?
  • Ship safety and the Plimsoll line
  • Deep-sea mining submersibles
  • Cyclone-safe roofs (Bernoulli)
  • High heels, pointe shoes and floors
  • Buoyancy aids and drowning prevention

Weak answer vs top-band answer

Question: Evaluate the use of hydraulic brakes in cars. [5]

2 / 5

Hydraulic brakes are strong and make cars safe.

No physics, no limitation, no judgement.
5 / 5

A small pedal force on a small master piston raises the fluid pressure, which is transmitted equally to larger pistons at all four wheels (Pascal's law), multiplying the force ✓. Safety: all wheels brake evenly with little effort ✓. Limitation: air bubbles or leaks make the brakes fail, so the fluid must be checked ✓. Environmental: brake fluid is toxic and must be disposed of safely ✓. On balance, essential and reliable when maintained ✓.

Physics, benefits, risks, and a judgement.
