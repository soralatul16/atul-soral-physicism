01Magnetic flux

DefinitionThe magnetic flux Φ through a flat area A in a uniform field B is
Φ = B A cos θ
θ is the angle between the field and the normal (the line at 90°) to the area. The unit is the weber: 1 Wb = 1 T m².
Flux linkageA coil of N turns has each turn threaded by Φ, so its flux linkage is NΦ (unit Wb, sometimes written Wb-turns). Faraday’s law uses the change in flux linkage.
Example 1.1A square coil of side 4.0 cm with 200 turns is in a field of 0.25 T. The normal to the coil makes 60° with the field. Calculate the flux through one turn and the flux linkage.
Φ = BA cos θ = 0.25 × (0.040)^{2} × cos 60° = 2.0 × 10^{-4} Wb NΦ = 200 × 2.0 × 10^{-4} => 4.0 × 10^{-2} Wb
Watch the angleIf the question gives the angle between B and the plane of the coil, then θ = 90° − that angle (or use sin instead of cos).

02Faraday’s law of induction

The lawA changing magnetic flux linkage induces an emf equal to the rate of change of flux linkage:
ε = -N frac{ΔΦ}{Δt}
The minus sign is Lenz’s law (section 4): the induced emf opposes the change that causes it.

There are three ways to change Φ = BA cos θ, and so three ways to induce an emf:

Key ideaThe emf depends on how fast the flux changes, not on how big the flux is. A huge steady flux gives no emf at all; a small flux that changes quickly gives a large emf. On a graph of NΦ against t, the emf is minus the gradient.
Example 2.1The flux through a 500-turn coil falls steadily from 3.0 mWb to 1.0 mWb in 0.20 s. Calculate the magnitude of the induced emf.
|ε| = N frac{ΔΦ}{Δt} = 500 × frac{(3.0 - 1.0) × 10^{-3}}{0.20} => 5.0 V
Area under an ε–t graphRearranging, NΔΦ = (area under the ε–t graph). When a magnet is dropped through a coil, the two pulses of emf have equal areas (the same flux enters and leaves), even though the second pulse is taller and narrower because the magnet is moving faster. See Experiments → Falling magnet.

03A straight conductor moving through a field

Derivation 1: from the force on the charges

# each free charge moves with the rod, so feels F = qvB along the rod # charge builds up at the ends until the electric force balances it qE = qvB # the field in the rod is E = ε/L frac{qε}{L} = qvB => ε = BvL

Derivation 2: from Faraday’s law

# in time Δt the rod sweeps out an area ΔA = L v Δt ΔΦ = B ΔA = B L v Δt ε = frac{ΔΦ}{Δt} => ε = BvL
Two pictures, one answerFaraday’s law (changing flux) and the magnetic force on moving charges (qvB from D.3) give exactly the same result. This only applies when v, B and L are all at right angles, which is all IB requires quantitatively.
Example 3.1An aircraft with a wingspan of 40 m flies horizontally at 250 m s⁻¹ where the vertical component of the Earth’s field is 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ T. Calculate the emf between the wing tips.
ε = BvL = 4.0 × 10^{-5} × 250 × 40 => 0.40 V

No current flows, because there is no complete circuit through the field: a voltmeter carried on board moves with the wing and has the same emf induced in its leads.

04Lenz’s law and conservation of energy

Lenz’s lawThe direction of the induced emf (and current) is such that it opposes the change that produces it.
  • Push the N pole of a magnet towards a coil: the near end of the coil becomes an N pole and repels the magnet.
  • Pull the N pole away: the near end becomes an S pole and attracts it back.
  • Slide a rod along rails: the force BIL on the induced current acts against the motion.
Why? Energy conservationSuppose the induced current helped the change instead. Then pushing a magnet in would pull it in faster, which would induce a bigger current, which would pull it faster still, producing electrical energy and kinetic energy from nothing. That would break conservation of energy. Because the induced current opposes the change, you must do work to keep the change going, and that work is the electrical energy produced.
# energy check for the sliding rod (circuit resistance R) I = frac{ε}{R} = frac{BvL}{R} F = BIL = frac{B^{2} L^{2} v}{R} mechanical power in = Fv = frac{B^{2} L^{2} v^{2}}{R} electrical power out = εI = frac{B^{2} L^{2} v^{2}}{R} # they are equal: every joule of work becomes a joule of electrical energy
Finding the direction in an exam1. Decide whether the flux through the circuit is increasing or decreasing. 2. The induced current makes its own field opposing that change (against B if Φ is increasing, along B if decreasing). 3. Use the right-hand grip rule to turn that field direction into a current direction. For a moving rod, you can instead use the left-hand rule: the force on the induced current must oppose v. Practise in Lenz’s law → Direction trainer.
Example 4.1The rod in section 3 is 0.50 m long, moves at 2.0 m s⁻¹ in a field of 0.40 T, and the circuit resistance is 0.80 Ω. Determine the current, the force needed to keep the rod moving at constant speed, and the power dissipated.
ε = BvL = 0.40 × 2.0 × 0.50 = 0.40 V I = frac{ε}{R} = frac{0.40}{0.80} = 0.50 A F = BIL = 0.40 × 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.10 N P = Fv = 0.10 × 2.0 = 0.20 W => P = εI = 0.40 × 0.50 = 0.20 W (check)
Terminal velocityIf a rod falls down vertical rails under gravity, the opposing force grows with speed. It reaches a terminal velocity when mg = frac{B^{2}L^{2}v}{R}, i.e. v_{t} = frac{mgR}{B^{2}L^{2}}. Then all the loss of gravitational potential energy becomes electrical energy (and heat in R). Try it in Moving conductor.

05Rectangular coils moving into and out of a field

# while entering, only the leading side (length L) cuts field lines ε = NBvL # time to enter fully, for a coil of width w t = frac{w}{v} # fully inside a uniform field: flux constant, so ε = 0
Example 5.1A 50-turn square coil of side 5.0 cm moves at 0.20 m s⁻¹ into a 0.30 T field. Calculate the emf while it enters and how long it lasts.
ε = NBvL = 50 × 0.30 × 0.20 × 0.050 = 0.15 V t = frac{w}{v} = frac{0.050}{0.20} => 0.25 s
Common trapA coil moving entirely inside a uniform field has no induced emf, even though it is moving. Each side has an emf BvL, but they act in opposite directions round the coil and cancel. The flux is not changing.

06Time-varying magnetic fields

A coil that does not move can still have an emf induced in it if the field through it changes, for example when a nearby electromagnet is switched on or off, or when it carries alternating current.

ε = -N A frac{ΔB}{Δt} (for a fixed coil facing the field)
  • B increasing steadily → constant emf.
  • B constant (however large) → no emf.
  • B sinusoidal → emf sinusoidal, a quarter of a cycle out of phase (maximum emf when B is passing through zero, where it changes fastest).
Example 6.1A 120-turn coil of area 8.0 × 10⁻³ m² faces a field that rises from 0 to 0.60 T in 40 ms. Calculate the average induced emf.
|ε| = NA frac{ΔB}{Δt} = 120 × 8.0 × 10^{-3} × frac{0.60}{0.040} => 14 V

07A coil rotating in a uniform field: the generator

# the angle between B and the normal increases steadily θ = ωt NΦ = NBA cos ωt # emf = −(rate of change of flux linkage) ε = NBAω sin ωt # peak emf ε_{0} = NBAω = 2π f NBA
Where is the emf largest?Coil plane parallel to B (normal at 90° to B): flux is zero but changing fastest, and the long sides of the coil are cutting the field lines at right angles. Coil plane perpendicular to B: flux is maximum but momentarily not changing, and the sides move parallel to the field lines, so ε = 0.
Same answer from ε = BvLEach long side (length L) moves on a circle of radius w/2 with speed v = ω w/2. Its velocity component perpendicular to B is v sin ωt, so each side gives BL(ωw/2) sin ωt. Two sides and N turns: ε = NB(Lw)ω sin ωt = NBAω sin ωt.
Example 7.1A generator coil has 150 turns, each 6.0 cm × 4.0 cm, and rotates at 50 revolutions per second in a field of 0.12 T. Calculate the peak emf.
ω = 2π f = 2π × 50 = 314 rad s^{-1} ε_{0} = NBAω = 150 × 0.12 × (0.060 × 0.040) × 314 => 13.6 V
How to increase the power output (guiding question 2)The peak emf NBAω increases with: more turns N, a stronger field B (stronger magnets, or electromagnets with iron cores), a larger coil area A, and faster rotation ω. The power delivered to a load R is ε²/R, so doubling the emf quadruples the power. Real power stations also reduce losses (low-resistance windings, laminated iron to cut eddy currents) and use large, efficient turbines.
Slip rings and commutatorsWith slip rings the output is alternating (a.c.). With a split-ring commutator the connections swap every half-turn, so the output is always in the same direction: a varying d.c. Only the shape of the output changes, not the physics of induction.

08The effect of changing the frequency of rotation

If the coil rotates twice as fast (f doubled):

  • the peak emf doubles (ε₀ = 2πfNBA ∝ f), because the flux changes twice as fast;
  • the period halves: the emf goes through twice as many cycles each second.

09Self-induction (qualitative) and eddy currents

Self-inductionWhen the current in a coil changes, the coil’s own magnetic flux changes, so an emf is induced in the coil itself. By Lenz’s law this back emf opposes the change in current: it slows the growth of the current when a circuit is switched on, and tries to keep the current flowing when it is switched off.
  • A lamp in series with a large coil lights up a little later than one in series with a resistor.
  • Breaking the circuit of a large coil can make a spark at the switch: the rapid collapse of the flux induces a very large emf.
  • Only a qualitative description is required: inductance and RL circuits are not in the syllabus.
Eddy currentsA changing flux through a solid piece of metal induces circulating currents inside it. By Lenz’s law they oppose the change, so they cause magnetic braking (a magnet falls slowly through a copper pipe) and they heat the metal (induction cooking). In transformers and motors they waste energy, so the iron cores are made of thin insulated sheets (laminations). Explore in Lenz’s law → Falling magnet in a tube.

10Linking questions

A.3 · B.4 · Energy productionHow is the efficiency of electricity generation dependent on the source of energy?

Most power stations turn a turbine connected to a generator. The generator itself is very efficient (often about 98%), so the overall efficiency depends mainly on how the turbine is driven. Thermal stations (coal, gas, nuclear) heat water to make steam, and a heat engine cannot be more efficient than the Carnot limit (B.4): typically 33–40%, or about 60% in combined-cycle gas plants. Hydroelectric and wind turbines are driven directly by moving water or air, with no heat engine, so hydroelectric generation can reach about 90% (wind turbines are limited by how much kinetic energy can be extracted from the air, at most about 59%). Solar photovoltaic cells do not use induction at all.

Nature of science · rates of changeFaraday’s law of induction includes a rate of change. Which other areas of physics relate to rates of change?

Velocity (rate of change of displacement) and acceleration (rate of change of velocity), A.1; force as the rate of change of momentum (F = Δp/Δt), A.2; power as the rate of doing work (P = ΔW/Δt), A.3; current as the rate of flow of charge (I = ΔQ/Δt), B.5; radioactive decay rate (activity, A = −dN/dt), E.3; the rate of thermal energy transfer (conduction, Q/Δt), B.1. In each case the “rate” is the gradient of a graph against time, and the area under the rate–time graph gives the total change.

11Nature of science and history

Faraday, 1831Oersted (1820) showed that a current produces a magnetic field. Faraday spent years looking for the reverse effect, expecting a steady magnet to produce a steady current. He found that an emf appears only while the field is changing: a key discovery that came from careful experiment, not from theory. Faraday had little formal mathematics; his idea of “lines of force” was later turned into equations by Maxwell.
Guiding question 3 · Induction and industrializationBefore induction, electricity came only from batteries: expensive and weak. Generators (1860s–1880s) made it possible to produce electricity on a large scale from steam, falling water or wind. This led to electric lighting, electric motors in factories, electric trams and railways, and national power grids (with transformers, which also work by induction). Factories no longer had to be built next to a water wheel or a steam engine. See the timeline in Applications.

Common exam mistakes
  • Thinking a large flux means a large emf: it is the rate of change that matters.
  • Using θ between B and the plane instead of the normal.
  • Forgetting N, or putting N in twice.
  • Saying the emf is maximum when the coil faces the field (it is zero then).
  • When f doubles, sketching only twice as many cycles and forgetting the doubled peak (or vice versa).
  • Thinking a coil moving inside a uniform field has an emf.
  • Explaining Lenz’s law without mentioning energy: the question often asks why it must be true.
  • Unit slips: cm² → × 10⁻⁴ m², mWb → × 10⁻³ Wb, ms → × 10⁻³ s, rpm → ÷ 60 for f.

14One-page summary

D.4 Induction (HL) · one-page summary

Flux

  • Φ = BA cos θ (θ from the normal)
  • Unit Wb = T m²
  • Flux linkage NΦ
  • Facing B: max. Edge-on: zero

Faraday’s law

  • ε = −N ΔΦ/Δt
  • emf = −gradient of NΦ–t graph
  • NΔΦ = area under ε–t graph
  • Change B, A or θ
  • Steady flux → no emf

Moving conductor

  • ε = BvL (all at 90°)
  • From qvB = qE, or ΔΦ = BLvΔt
  • I = BvL/R, F = B²L²v/R (opposes v)
  • Fv = εI: work in = electrical energy out
  • Falling rod: vt = mgR/(B²L²)

Lenz’s law

  • Induced current opposes the change
  • Consequence of energy conservation
  • N pole in → near end N (repels)
  • N pole out → near end S (attracts)
  • Eddy currents: braking and heating

Coils and fields

  • Entering: ε = NBvL, lasts w/v
  • Fully inside: ε = 0
  • Leaving: equal and opposite
  • Fixed coil: ε = NA ΔB/Δt

Generator

  • NΦ = NBA cos ωt
  • ε = NBAω sin ωt, ε₀ = 2πfNBA
  • ε max when flux is zero (plane ∥ B)
  • Double f: double ε₀ and halve T
  • More power: ↑N, B, A, ω (P ∝ ε²)

Top 8 mistakes to avoid

  1. Big flux ≠ big emf: it is the rate of change.
  2. θ is measured from the normal.
  3. The emf is zero when the coil faces the field.
  4. Doubling f doubles the peak AND halves the period.
  5. A coil moving inside a uniform field has no emf.
  6. Lenz’s law: always mention energy conservation.
  7. Convert cm², mWb, ms and rpm.
  8. Self-induction: qualitative only.

The method

Step 1Is the flux through the circuit increasing or decreasing?
Step 2The induced current makes its own field to oppose that change: against B if Φ is increasing, in the same direction as B if Φ is decreasing.
Step 3Right-hand grip rule: curl your fingers the way the current goes round the loop and your thumb gives the direction of its field.

Exam answer

“If the induced current were in the opposite direction, the force on it would be in the direction of motion. The rod would accelerate, increasing the induced current and the force further, so kinetic energy and electrical energy would both increase with no work done. This would violate the conservation of energy, so the induced current must oppose the change (Lenz’s law).”

Questions

1. Why is the second pulse opposite in sign to the first?
2. Why is the second pulse taller and narrower?
3. Compare the areas. What does the area represent, and why does it not depend on h?
4. Why is peak emf proportional to √h?
Answers
1. Entering, the flux through the coil increases; leaving, it decreases, so ΔΦ/Δt changes sign (Lenz’s law: the coil first repels, then attracts the magnet).
2. The magnet is moving faster when it leaves (it has accelerated under gravity), so the flux changes faster: bigger emf for a shorter time.
3. Area under ε–t = NΔΦ, the total change in flux linkage. The same flux enters and leaves whatever the speed, so the areas are equal and independent of h.
4. The speed at the coil is v = √(2gh), and the peak emf ∝ rate of change of flux ∝ v, so εpeak ∝ √h.
