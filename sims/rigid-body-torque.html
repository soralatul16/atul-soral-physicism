A.4 · Higher Level

Torque & the turning effect

Why does a long spanner beat a short one, and why do we push a door at its edge, never the hinge? Set the force, slide it along the arm, and change its angle — then watch the turning-effect gauge respond to τ = Fr sinθ.

HL · Higher Level
The big idea

Torque measures how hard a force twists an object around a pivot. Push a spanner far from the bolt and it turns with ease; grip it right next to the bolt and you strain for nothing. Three things decide the twist: how hard you push (F), how far from the axis you push (r), and the angle you push at (θ). Put together, they give τ = Fr sinθ.

Torque is the exact rotational echo of force. A resultant force changes how fast something travels in a straight line; a resultant torque changes how fast it spins. Get comfortable here and the whole of rotational mechanics opens up.

A force on a rigid arm

Drag the red dot along the arm to change r. Watch the turning-effect gauge at the top — it swells and shrinks the instant you change the force, the distance, or the angle.

Derivation · τ = Fr sinθ

Resolve the force into two parts and keep only the one that actually turns the body.

Step 1 · resolve the force
F∥ = F cosθ,F⊥ = F sinθ
Split F into a component along the arm (F cosθ) and a component perpendicular to it (F sinθ).
Step 2 · the parallel part does nothing
τ(from F∥) = 0
The line of action of F cosθ runs straight through the axis, so it has no turning effect — it only pushes or pulls along the arm.
Step 3 · torque from the perpendicular part
τ = F⊥ · r = (F sinθ) r
Only F sinθ turns the body. Torque = perpendicular force × its distance r from the axis.
Step 4 · result
τ = Fr sinθ
Unit N m. Greatest when θ = 90° (push square-on) and zero when the force lines up with the arm (θ = 0° or 180°).

The working

τ = Fr sinθunit N m
Question
A spanner of length 0.25 m turns a bolt. A force of 40 N is applied at its far end.
(a) Calculate the torque when the force is applied perpendicular to the spanner. [2]
τ = Fr sinθ = (40)(0.25) sin90° =10 N m
(b) The same force is now applied at 30° to the spanner. Calculate the new torque. [2]
τ = (40)(0.25) sin30° = (40)(0.25)(0.5) =5.0 N m
Half as much — the turning component F sinθ has halved.
(c) State how the force should be applied to get the maximum torque. [1]
Apply it perpendicular to the spanner (θ = 90°) and as far from the axis as possible (largest r).
HL · Higher Level
Rotational mass

Mass measures how hard it is to get something moving in a line. The moment of inertia I measures how hard it is to get something turning. Shove a loaded merry-go-round and it barely stirs — that stubbornness is I, and it is exactly what the torque in τ = Iα has to overcome.

What sets I apart from plain mass is where the mass sits. Push mass outward and it resists turning far more, because every piece contributes m r² — distance squared. Keep the mass close to the axis and the same kilograms spin up with ease. That is why, kilo for kilo, a hoop is stubborn while a sphere is nimble.

Compare the shapes

Pick a shape to watch it spin about its axis — drag the size slider and the shape grows or shrinks with it. The bars compare I for every shape at the same mass and size, so you can see how much the leading coefficient changes.

Where I comes from · I = Σ m r²

Just the idea for now — the full derivation of each shape's formula comes in Part 3.

Step 1 · a single point mass
I = m r²
One particle of mass m, a distance r from the axis, has moment of inertia m r². The farther out it sits, the more it resists being spun.
Step 2 · add up the whole body
I = Σ mi ri²
Picture an extended body as countless tiny masses and add each one's m r². That total is the body's moment of inertia.
Step 3 · why shape matters
I ∝ (mass) × (distance from axis)²
Because of the r², mass far from the axis dominates. Same mass, rearranged, gives a completely different I.
Step 4 · the standard shapes
ring M R²,disc ½M R²,sphere ⅖M R²
The ring keeps all its mass at R (largest I); the sphere bunches mass near the centre (smallest). These booklet formulas are derived and applied in full in Part 3.

The working

I = Σ m r²unit kg m²
Question
Two wheels have the same mass and the same radius. One is a thin ring, the other a solid disc.
(a) Which has the larger moment of inertia, and why? [2]
The ring: I = MR² against ½MR² for the disc. All of the ring's mass sits out at radius R, whereas the disc carries much of its mass closer to the axis where r² is small.
(b) A rod of mass M and length L is spun first about its centre, then about one end. State I for each and say which is larger. [2]
Centre: I = 1⁄12 ML². End: I = ⅓ ML². The end axis gives the larger value (four times as big) because more of the rod's mass now lies far from the axis.
(c) Using I = Σ m r², explain why moving mass outward increases the moment of inertia. [1]
Each mass contributes m r², which grows with the square of its distance. Moving mass to larger r raises every r² term, so the total I increases.
HL · Higher Level
A sharper way to see it

There is a second, often quicker, way to find torque. Instead of splitting the force into components, draw the whole line the force acts along and ask one question: how far does that line pass from the axis? That shortest (perpendicular) distance is the lever arm d, and the torque is simply τ = F d, where d = r sinθ.

Here is the elegant part — slide the force back and forth along its own line and the turning effect never changes, because the perpendicular distance stays fixed. The twist is strongest when that line sweeps farthest from the axis.

The perpendicular distance

The dashed line is the force's line of action; the gold segment is the lever arm d. The lower graph is τ against θ.

Derivation · τ = F d = Fr sinθ

First see exactly where d = r sinθ comes from, then use it to get the torque.

Step 1 · build a right triangle
hypotenuse = r,angle = θ
The arm (length r) runs from the axis to where the force acts. Draw the force's line of action and drop a perpendicular onto it from the axis. These lines form a right-angled triangle: the arm is the hypotenuse, and θ sits at the point where the force is applied.
Step 2 · why d = r sinθ
sinθ = oppositehypotenuse = dr ⇒ d = r sinθ
The lever arm d is the side opposite θ. By the definition of sine, sinθ = d / r, so d = r sinθ. This is the real reason a sine appears in the torque formula.
Step 3 · torque = force × lever arm
τ = F d
Torque is the full force multiplied by the perpendicular distance to its line of action.
Step 4 · the two views agree
τ = F(r sinθ) = Fr sinθ
Identical to the result from resolving the force. It is largest at θ = 90° (d = r) and zero when the line of action runs through the axis (d = 0).

The working

τ = F d,d = r sinθ
A door is 0.80 m wide with its hinge along one edge.
Question
(a) A 20 N push is applied perpendicular to the door at its outer edge. Find the torque about the hinge. [2]
τ = F d = (20)(0.80) =16 N m
Here d = r = 0.80 m because the push is perpendicular.
(b) The same push is applied at 50° to the door. Find the lever arm and the torque. [2]
d = r sinθ = 0.80 sin50° ≈ 0.61 m
τ = F d = (20)(0.61) ≈12 N m
(c) Explain why we push a door at its outer edge rather than near the hinge. [1]
The outer edge gives the largest r, hence the largest lever arm d and the greatest torque for the same force.
HL · Higher Level
The balance rule

A rigid body is in rotational equilibrium when the resultant torque about any axis is zero: Στ = 0. Pair that with ΣF = 0 and nothing accelerates — the body neither drifts nor spins up.

This single rule is the principle of moments. It is what keeps a see-saw level, a shelf bracket from rotating off the wall, and a loaded beam resting quietly on its supports. The clever move in exam problems: take moments about a point where an unknown force acts, and that force drops out of the maths entirely.

Balancing a beam

Change the masses and distances with the sliders, or just drag a weight along the beam. The beam tilts toward the larger moment and settles level when Στ = 0.

Derivation · the principle of moments

Apply Στ = 0 about the pivot and watch g cancel.

Step 1 · equilibrium condition
Στ = 0
In rotational equilibrium the clockwise and counter-clockwise torques about the pivot must be equal.
Step 2 · balance the two sides
τccw = τcw
The left mass turns the beam counter-clockwise; the right mass turns it clockwise. Set them equal.
Step 3 · write each torque
m1 g d1 = m2 g d2
Each torque is weight × distance: (mg)(d). The forces act perpendicular to the beam, so no sinθ is needed.
Step 4 · the principle of moments
m1 d1 = m2 d2
g cancels. Translational equilibrium ΣF = 0 then gives the upward pivot reaction R = (m1 + m2)g.

The working

m1 d1 = m2 d2
Question
A see-saw pivots at its centre. A child of mass 30 kg sits 1.5 m from the pivot (take g = 9.81 m s⁻²).
(a) Calculate the torque this child produces about the pivot. [2]
τ = m g d = (30)(9.81)(1.5) ≈4.4 × 10² N m
(441 N m, counter-clockwise.)
(b) A 45 kg child sits on the other side and balances the see-saw. How far from the pivot must they sit? [2]
m1 d1 = m2 d2 ⇒ d2 = (30)(1.5)45 =1.0 m
(c) State the upward reaction force exerted by the pivot. [1]
R = (m₁ + m₂)g = (30 + 45)(9.81) ≈ 7.4 × 10² N (736 N) upward.
HL · Higher Level
When the twists don't cancel

Let the two torques go unequal and something new happens: there is a resultant torque, and the wheel no longer turns steadily — it speeds up. An unbalanced torque produces angular acceleration, exactly as an unbalanced force produces linear acceleration.

This is Newton's laws rewritten for rotation. Balanced torques → the spin stays constant (or the body stays still). Unbalanced torque → the spin changes, and how fast it changes depends not only on the torque but on how the mass is spread about the axis — the moment of inertia you will meet in Part 3.

Two torques on a wheel

While the torques are unequal the wheel keeps gaining angular speed. Make them equal and the speed holds steady.

From resultant torque to angular acceleration

The logic that carries into Part 3, where τ = Iα is derived in full.

Step 1 · balanced case
Στ = 0 ⇒ α = 0
Equal and opposite torques give no resultant: the body stays at rest or spins at a constant rate.
Step 2 · unbalanced case
τnet ≠ 0 ⇒ α ≠ 0
A resultant torque changes the rotation — it produces an angular acceleration α in the direction (sense) of that resultant.
Step 3 · the proportionality
τnet ∝ α
Experiment shows α is proportional to the resultant torque for a given body — larger net torque, larger angular acceleration.
Step 4 · Newton's second law for rotation
τnet = I α
The constant of proportionality is the moment of inertia I from Tab 2 — a measure of how the mass is spread about the axis. The full derivation of τ = Iα comes in Part 3.

The working

τnet = τ1 − τ2
Question
A wheel is free to rotate about a fixed axle. Two forces are applied at its rim.
(a) The two forces produce equal and opposite torques. Describe the motion of the wheel. [1]
The resultant torque is zero, so there is no angular acceleration: the wheel stays at rest, or keeps turning at constant angular speed.
(b) One torque is now increased. Describe what happens and in which sense. [2]
There is a resultant torque, so the wheel angularly accelerates in the sense of the larger torque — its angular speed increases in that direction.
(c) Besides the resultant torque, what property of the wheel determines the size of its angular acceleration? [1]
Its moment of inertia — how the wheel's mass is distributed about the axle (τ = Iα).
HL · Higher Level

Five problems to test yourself on — torque at an angle, the lever-arm trick, and balancing a loaded beam. Work each one through before you reveal the full solution.

Worked Example · Torque at an angle
A force of 25 N acts at the end of a 0.40 m rod. Find the torque about the far end (i) perpendicular to the rod, (ii) at 35° to it.
(i) τ = Fr sinθ = (25)(0.40) sin90° =10 N m
(ii) τ = (25)(0.40) sin35° ≈5.7 N m

The off-axis push loses the factor sin35° ≈ 0.57 of its turning effect.

Worked Example · Lever arm
A spanner 0.30 m long is pulled with 60 N at 70° to its length. Find the lever arm and the torque on the bolt.
d = r sinθ = 0.30 sin70° ≈ 0.28 m
τ = F d = (60)(0.28) ≈17 N m

Equivalently τ = (60)(0.30) sin70° ≈ 16.9 N m.

Worked Example · Balancing masses
On a see-saw, a 4.0 kg mass sits 0.60 m from the pivot. An unknown mass 0.40 m from the pivot on the other side balances it. Find the mass.
m1 d1 = m2 d2 ⇒ m2 = (4.0)(0.60)0.40 =6.0 kg

g cancels on both sides, so only the masses and distances matter.

Worked Example · Two supports
A light horizontal plank 2.0 m long rests on supports at each end. A 10 kg mass sits 0.50 m from the left support. Find the force on each support (g = 9.81 m s⁻²).

Take moments about the left support (its force then has zero lever arm):

RR(2.0) = (10)(9.81)(0.50) ⇒ RR = 49.052.0 ≈25 N
ΣF = 0: RL = (10)(9.81) − 24.5 ≈74 N

The nearer support carries more of the load, as expected.

Worked Example · Force needed
A bolt needs a torque of 30 N m to turn. Using a 0.50 m spanner, what force is needed if applied (i) perpendicular, (ii) at 40° to the spanner?
(i) F = τr sinθ = 30(0.50) sin90° =60 N
(ii) F = 30(0.50) sin40° ≈93 N

Pulling off-square wastes effort — you need far more force for the same turning effect.

HL · Higher Level

The traps that quietly cost marks. Each card sets the tempting wrong idea beside what is really going on.

"Torque is just another word for force."

Torque is the turning effect of a force and depends on the force, its distance from the axis, and its angle (τ = Fr sinθ). Its unit is the newton metre, not the newton.

τ = Fr sinθ, unit N m.
"A bigger force always gives a bigger torque."

Not if it acts close to the axis or along the arm. A small force with a long lever arm can out-turn a large force applied near the pivot.

Lever arm matters as much as force.
"Torque is greatest when the force points along the arm."

That gives zero torque (θ = 0°, the line of action passes through the axis). Torque is greatest when the force is perpendicular to the arm (θ = 90°).

Maximum at 90°, zero at 0°.
"If the net force is zero, the body is in equilibrium."

You need both ΣF = 0 and Στ = 0. Two equal, opposite, non-aligned forces (a couple) give zero net force yet still turn the body.

Equilibrium needs ΣF = 0 and Στ = 0.
"You must always take moments about the pivot."

Moments can be taken about any point. Choosing an axis through an unknown force removes it from the equation and simplifies the algebra.

Any axis works — pick a smart one.
"A newton metre is the same as a joule."

Both reduce to kg m² s⁻², but torque (N m) is a turning effect while energy (J) is work done. They are kept distinct, and torque is never written in joules.

Torque in N m, energy in J.
