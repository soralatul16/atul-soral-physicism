The hydrogen atom, Bohr's picture

An electron (charge −e, mass m) circles a proton (charge +e) at radius r and speed v.

The constants in play
k
8.99 × 109 N m2 C−2
Coulomb constant, k = 1/4πε0. Sets how strong the electric force is between charges.
e
1.60 × 10−19 C
Elementary charge, the size of the charge on the proton (+) and electron (−).
m
9.11 × 10−31 kg
Mass of the electron, the particle doing the orbiting.
h
6.63 × 10−34 J s
Planck constant. It sets the scale of all quantum effects and appears in the quantisation rule.
n
1, 2, 3, …
Principal quantum number, a whole number that labels which allowed orbit the electron is in.

Derivation · radius of the allowed orbits

Goal: show that only certain radii are allowed, and find r.

Step 1 · The force
The electric pull is the centripetal force:
mv2r=ke2r2
The inward Coulomb attraction between +e and −e supplies exactly the force needed to keep the electron on its circle.
Step 2 · Solve for speed
Cancel one r, then rearrange:
mv2=ke2r→v=ke2 / mr
Keep this expression for v handy, we substitute it in Step 4.
Step 3 · Bohr's quantum rule
Angular momentum is quantised:
mvr=nh2π
This is the new physics Bohr added by hand: only whole-number multiples of h/2π are allowed. Everything quantised follows from this one line.
Step 4 · Substitute v
Put v = √(ke2/mr) into mvr:
ke2mr=nh2π
Because mr·√(ke2/mr) = √(ke2mr).
Step 5 · Square & rearrange
Square both sides, solve for r:
r=n2h24π2ke2m
The radius can only take values proportional to n2. Orbits are quantised, the electron cannot spiral inward.
Step 6 · The ground state
Put n = 1 and the constants in:
r1=5.3 × 10−11 m
This is the Bohr radius, the size of a hydrogen atom in its lowest state. Larger n gives bigger, less tightly bound orbits.

Energy of a bound electron

Total energy = kinetic energy + electric potential energy. Watch the energy ladder build.

The constants in play
eV
1 eV = 1.60 × 10−19 J
Electronvolt, a convenient tiny energy unit for atoms. Divide joules by this to read energies in eV.
13.6
13.6 eV
The ionisation energy of hydrogen, the energy needed to free the ground-state electron (E1 = −13.6 eV).
k, e
as on the orbit page
Same Coulomb constant and elementary charge reused here inside the energy expression.
n
1, 2, 3, …
Principal quantum number. Because E ∝ −1/n2, it fixes which rung of the energy ladder the electron sits on.

Derivation · the energy levels of hydrogen

Goal: get Etotal = −13.6/n2 eV.

Step 1 · Add the energies
Etotal=½mv2+(−ke2r)
Kinetic energy of the electron plus the electric potential energy of the +e/−e pair (negative because they attract).
Step 2 · Reuse the force result
From the orbit page, mv2 = ke2/r, so:
½mv2=½ke2r
The kinetic energy is exactly half the size of the potential energy, a signature of any inverse-square orbit.
Step 3 · Combine
Etotal=½ke2r−ke2r=−½ke2r
A clean, compact result: the total energy is negative and equals minus one-half the potential energy magnitude.
Step 4 · Ground state in joules
Put r = 5.3×10−11 m:
Etotal=−2.2 × 10−18 J
Divide by 1.6×10−19 J/eV and you get −13.6 eV. The numbers match experiment.
Step 5 · Substitute the quantised r
Insert r = n2h2/(4π2ke2m):
E=−2π2k2e4mn2h2=−13.6n2 eV
All the constants collapse into 13.6 eV. Energy is quantised because r was quantised, the whole ladder comes from Bohr's one rule.
Step 6 · Read the ladder
n=1: −13.6 · n=2: −3.40 · n=3: −1.51 eV
Levels crowd together as n grows and reach 0 at n→∞ (ionisation). Jumps between levels give hydrogen's spectral lines.
💡 Tip: each step draws another rung on the energy ladder.

How big, and how dense, is a nucleus?

Nucleons pack like tiny spheres. Add more, and the ball just grows, its density stays fixed.

▶ Try it live · change the nucleon number A
The constants in play
R0
1.20 × 10−15 m
Fermi radius, an empirical constant roughly the size of a single nucleon. It sets the scale of every nucleus.
A
whole number (dimensionless)
Nucleon number, the total count of protons + neutrons. Larger A means a bigger, heavier nucleus.
u
1.661 × 10−27 kg
Atomic mass unit, close to the mass of one nucleon, so the nuclear mass is about A×u.
fm
1 fm = 10−15 m
Femtometre (or "fermi"), the natural length scale for nuclear sizes.

Derivation · radius and density of nuclei

Goal: R = R0A1/3 and show density is the same for all nuclei.

Step 1 · Radius law
Scattering experiments show R grows with the cube root of A:
R=R0A1/3
Cube root, because volume ∝ A and volume ∝ R3. Doubling the nucleons does not double the radius.
Step 2 · Worked example, gold
R = (1.20×10−15) × 1971/3
= 6.98 × 10−15 m  ≈ 7.0 fm
A gold nucleus (A = 197) is only about 7 femtometres across, tiny even by atomic standards.
Step 3 · Set up density
Use ρ = m/V with m ≈ Au and a spherical volume:
ρ=Au43π(R0A1/3)3
Mass is roughly A nucleons, each about one atomic mass unit u.
Step 4 · The A cancels
(A1/3)3 = A, so A cancels top and bottom:
ρ=3u4πR03
No A left. Nuclear density does not depend on how many nucleons there are, or on the radius.
Step 5 · Put the numbers in
ρ=3(1.661×10−27)4π(1.20×10−15)3=2.3×1017 kg m−3
Every nucleus, hydrogen to uranium, has about this same staggering density.
Step 6 · Why it matters
Atoms are ~105× bigger than nuclei, so:
1017(105)3≈102 kg m−3
That matches everyday matter. Only neutron stars and black holes reach true nuclear density.
💡 Tip: steps 1–2 grow the nucleus so you can see the cube-root law.

Two forces between nucleons

Coulomb repulsion reaches far; the strong force is short-range but wins up close, until it turns repulsive.

The scales in play
< 1.5
below ~1.5 fm
At these separations the strong force is large and attractive, it dominates over the electric repulsion and binds nucleons together.
> 3
beyond ~3 fm
The strong force has faded to zero, so only the long-range Coulomb repulsion is left and Rutherford's law holds.
k, e
Coulomb constant & charge
k = 8.99×109 N m2 C−2, e = 1.60×10−19 C, they set the electric repulsion F = ke2/r2.

Reading Figure E1.19

Why Rutherford scattering breaks down at high energy.

Step 1 · The Coulomb curve
Two protons always repel electrically:
FCoulomb=ke2r2
Positive (repulsive) everywhere and it decays smoothly. This is all Rutherford assumed, and it explains normal scattering.
Step 2 · Add the strong force
A second curve appears: the strong nuclear force.
It acts only between nucleons that are very close. Beyond a few femtometres it is essentially zero, which is why we never notice it at atomic scales.
Step 3 · Beyond 3 fm
For separations > ~3×10−15 m:
Electric repulsion dominates. Scattering here follows Coulomb's law exactly, so ordinary Rutherford analysis works fine.
Step 4 · Closer than 1.5 fm
The strong force becomes attractive and large.
A very energetic alpha particle can push this close. Now it feels attraction as well as repulsion, so deviations from Rutherford's prediction appear, this is the experimental evidence for the strong force.
Step 5 · Even closer
At the smallest separations the strong force turns repulsive again.
That repulsive core stops nucleons collapsing into each other and sets the near-constant density we found on the previous page.

Common misconceptions, cleared up

The traps that cost marks. Each card pairs the wrong idea with the fix.

✕

"The electron should spiral into the nucleus."

✓

Classically an accelerating charge radiates and would spiral in. Bohr's quantisation of angular momentum (mvr = nh/2π) allows only fixed orbits, so the ground state is stable, that is the whole point of the model.

✕

"Negative energy means something is wrong."

✓

Zero energy is defined as a free electron infinitely far away. Negative simply means bound, you must add energy to remove the electron. The deeper (more negative) the level, the more tightly bound.

✕

"n is the radius, or the energy."

✓

n is just a quantum number (1, 2, 3…). Radius scales as r ∝ n2 and energy as E ∝ −1/n2. They are related to n, but are not n.

✕

"The photon energy equals the upper level's energy."

✓

The photon carries the difference between two levels: Ephoton = |Ehi − Elo| = hf. The same two levels give the same photon whether emitted or absorbed.

✕

"Emission and absorption spectra are different wavelengths."

✓

They occur at the same wavelengths. Emission shows bright lines (photons given out); absorption shows dark lines at those same positions on a continuous spectrum.

✕

"A bigger nucleus is denser."

✓

The A cancels in the density derivation: ρ = 3u/(4πR03). Every nucleus has roughly the same density (~2.3×1017 kg m−3), regardless of size.

✕

"Angular momentum can be any value."

✓

In the Bohr model it is quantised in units of h/2π (= ℏ). Only whole-number multiples are allowed, that restriction is what forces discrete orbits and energies.

✕

"The strong force is always attractive."

✓

It is attractive around 1–1.5 fm but becomes repulsive at very short range and ~zero beyond ~3 fm. That short range plus a repulsive core is why nuclear matter does not collapse.

✕

"R = R0A1/3 means radius doubles when A doubles."

✓

It is a cube-root law. To double the radius you need 8× the nucleons. Volume, not radius, is proportional to A.

✕

"k is Boltzmann's constant."

✓

Here k is the Coulomb constant, k = 1/(4πε0) ≈ 8.99×109 N m2 C−2. Watch which k a formula means.

Check your understanding

Try each one before revealing the answer. Use the spectrum tab to check the numbers.

Q1 · Bohr

State the two physical ideas Bohr combined to derive the radii of the electron orbits in hydrogen.

(1) Classical physics: the Coulomb attraction provides the centripetal force, mv²/r = ke²/r². (2) Quantum idea: angular momentum is quantised, mvr = nh/2π. Combining them gives r = n²h²/(4π²ke²m).
Q2 · Energy

Show that the total energy of the hydrogen atom is E = −½ke²/r.

E = KE + PE = ½mv² − ke²/r. From the force equation mv² = ke²/r, so ½mv² = ½ke²/r. Then E = ½ke²/r − ke²/r = −½ke²/r.
Q3 · Spectrum

An electron falls from n = 3 to n = 2 in hydrogen. Which series is this line in, and roughly what colour?

nlo = 2 → the Balmer series (visible). n = 3 → 2 is the Hα line at 656 nm, which is red. Check it on the spectrum tab.
Q4 · Photon

Calculate the energy (in eV) of the photon emitted when an electron falls from n = 4 to n = 1.

ΔE = 13.6(1/1² − 1/4²) = 13.6(1 − 0.0625) = 13.6 × 0.9375 = 12.75 eV. This is a Lyman-series line (UV).
Q5 · Nucleus

Use R = R₀A^(1/3) to estimate the radius of a carbon-12 nucleus (R₀ = 1.20 × 10⁻¹⁵ m).

R = 1.20 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 12^(1/3) = 1.20 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 2.289 = 2.75 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
Q6 · Density

Explain why all nuclei have approximately the same density.

Mass ∝ A, and volume = (4/3)πR³ ∝ (A^(1/3))³ = A, so ρ = mass/volume ∝ A/A, independent of A. Hence ρ = 3u/(4πR₀³) ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³ for every nucleus.
