7.1 · Energy travels, matter does not

Progressive Waves

A progressive wave carries energy away from a source through a medium, while the particles of the medium only oscillate about fixed equilibrium positions. This section builds every term you need, the wave equation, the CRO, and intensity — to full Cambridge AS rigour.

AS · Standard

Flick a rope, push a slinky, or dip a finger in a ripple tank and a disturbance travels outwards. The disturbance moves on, but each piece of the medium just vibrates in place — a progressive wave transfers energy, never matter.

Watch a wave travel — and one particle stay put

The red particle is tracked. Notice it only oscillates about its own fixed point — it never moves along with the wave. The green arrow shows the direction energy is carried.

Two kinds of progressive wave

In a transverse wave the oscillations are at right angles to the direction of energy transfer — a wave on a rope or string, water-surface ripples, and all electromagnetic waves.

In a longitudinal wave the oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer. Pushing and pulling a slinky, or a sound wave in air, produces regions where the medium is squeezed together (compressions) and pulled apart (rarefactions).

In every case the particles have the same period as the source and pass energy on to their neighbours. The source does work; the medium carries that energy forward as kinetic and potential energy of its oscillating particles.

Question
A long rope is fixed at one end. A student moves the free end up and down steadily, sending a continuous wave along the rope.
(a) State what is transferred along the rope, and what is not. [2]
Energy is transferred along the rope (away from the hand). Matter is not transferred — each part of the rope only oscillates about its equilibrium position.
(b) State and explain whether the wave on the rope is transverse or longitudinal. [2]
Transverse. The rope particles oscillate up and down, at right angles to the horizontal direction in which the wave (and its energy) travels.
(c) The student now moves the end faster, with the same size of movement. State the effect on the frequency and on the amplitude of the wave. [2]
Frequency increases (more oscillations per second). Amplitude is unchanged, because the size of the hand's movement sets the maximum displacement and that has not changed.

Every wave quantity has a precise meaning. Drag the controls to see displacement, amplitude and wavelength, then separate the two markers to read the phase difference between two points.

Anatomy of a wave

Definitions & readouts

Δφ = 2π · dλ

The exact wording the examiner wants

Displacement — the distance of a particle from its equilibrium position, in a stated direction. Amplitude — the maximum displacement of a particle from its equilibrium position. Wavelength λ — the minimum distance between two points on a wave oscillating in phase (e.g. crest to crest).

Period T — the time for one complete oscillation. Frequency f — the number of complete oscillations per unit time, with f = 1/T. Speed v — the distance travelled by the wave profile per unit time.

Phase difference — a measure of how far one oscillation leads or lags another, expressed as an angle (radians or degrees), not a distance. Two points a distance d apart differ in phase by Δφ = 2πd/λ. They are in phase when d = nλ (Δφ = 0, 2π, …) and in antiphase when d = (n + ½)λ (Δφ = π, 3π, …).

Question
The diagram of a transverse wave has wavelength 0.60 m. Two points P and Q on the wave are 0.15 m apart.
(a) Define amplitude. [1]
The maximum displacement of a particle from its equilibrium (rest) position.
(b) Calculate the phase difference between P and Q, in radians. [2]
Δφ = 2π · dλ=2π × 0.150.60=π/2 rad
Equivalent to 90°, i.e. one quarter of a cycle.
(c) State the smallest extra distance Q could be moved from P so that the two points oscillate in antiphase. [2]
Antiphase needs d = λ/2 = 0.30 m. P and Q are already 0.15 m apart, so Q must move a further 0.15 m.
AS · Standard

A cathode-ray oscilloscope plots voltage (vertical) against time (horizontal). Two settings decode the trace: the y-gain (volts per division) gives amplitude from the height, and the time-base (time per division) gives the period — and hence the frequency — from the width of one cycle.

Read the trace

y-gain sets the vertical scale (volts/division). Time-base sets the horizontal scale (time/division). The screen is 10 divisions wide and 8 high.

Decoding the signal

T = ndiv × tbase,  f = 1T
On a CRO the time-base is set to 5.0 ms per division and the y-gain to 2.0 V per division. A sinusoidal trace completes one full cycle across 4.0 divisions and reaches a maximum height of 3.0 divisions above the centre line.
Question
(a) Determine the period of the signal. [2]
T = 4.0 × 5.0 ms=20 ms = 0.020 s
(b) Hence calculate the frequency. [1]
f = 1T=10.020=50 Hz
(c) Determine the amplitude and the peak-to-peak voltage of the signal. [2]
Amplitude = 3.0 div × 2.0 V/div = 6.0 V. Peak-to-peak = 2 × 6.0 = 12 V (the trace spans 6 divisions top to bottom).
AS · Standard

In exactly one period the wave profile advances exactly one wavelength. That single fact, with f = 1/T, gives the most-used equation in waves: v = fλ.

Set f and λ, watch v

The crest marked in red advances one whole wavelength every period. A higher f or a longer λ both make the profile travel faster.

The speed

v = fλ

Derivation · why v = fλ

Reveal one line of reasoning at a time, straight from the definitions of speed, frequency and wavelength.

Step 1 · one period, one wavelength
In a time of one period T, the wave moves forward by one wavelength λ.
By definition T is the time for one complete oscillation, and in that time the repeating profile shifts along by exactly λ.
Step 2 · speed is distance over time
v = distancetime=λT
Using the definition of speed with distance λ travelled in time T.
Step 3 · substitute f = 1/T
v = λ × 1T=λf
Frequency is the reciprocal of the period, so 1/T is simply f.
Step 4 · the wave equation
v = fλ
True for every progressive wave — mechanical or electromagnetic.
Question
A sound wave in air travels at 340 m s⁻¹ and has a frequency of 170 Hz.
(a) Calculate the wavelength of the sound. [2]
λ = vf=340170=2.0 m
(b) The wave passes into a warmer region where its speed rises to 350 m s⁻¹. The frequency is unchanged. State and explain the effect on the wavelength. [2]
Wavelength increases. Frequency is fixed by the source, so with f constant and v larger in λ = v/f, the wavelength must grow (to 350/170 ≈ 2.06 m).
(c) State the period of the wave. [1]
T = 1f=1170≈5.9 × 10⁻³ s
AS · Standard

A progressive wave carries energy outward from its source. How concentrated that energy is at a point is the intensity — the power passing through unit area. For a point source it falls as an inverse square, and for any wave it is proportional to the square of the amplitude.

Energy spreading from a point source

All the power crosses the surface of a sphere of area 4πr². Double the distance and the same power is spread over four times the area, so the intensity falls to a quarter.

Intensity at distance r

I = P4πr²⇒I ∝ 1r²

Turn up the amplitude

The upper wave shows the chosen amplitude; the bar shows the resulting intensity. Because I ∝ A², doubling the amplitude makes the wave four times as intense.

The square law

I ∝ A²

Why amplitude matters so much

The energy stored in an oscillating particle is proportional to the square of its amplitude, so the energy delivered per second — the power — and therefore the intensity are proportional to A². A loudspeaker driven to twice the amplitude is four times as intense; a wave of half the amplitude carries only a quarter of the energy.

Combining the two results, for a point source the amplitude itself falls as 1/r: since I ∝ 1/r² and I ∝ A², it follows that A ∝ 1/r.

Question
A small lamp emits light uniformly in all directions with a power of 25 W.
(a) Calculate the intensity of the light at a distance of 2.0 m from the lamp. [3]
I = P4πr²=254π(2.0)²≈0.50 W m⁻²
(b) State the intensity at a distance of 6.0 m, without a full recalculation. [2]
Distance tripled ⇒ intensity falls by 3² = 9. So I = 0.50 / 9 ≈ 0.055 W m⁻².
(c) At 6.0 m, how does the amplitude of the light wave compare with its amplitude at 2.0 m? [2]
Since I ∝ A² and the intensity is one-ninth, the amplitude is √(1/9) = one-third of its value at 2.0 m (consistent with A ∝ 1/r).

Worked Example · Using v = fλ
Ripples on water have a wavelength of 2.5 cm and travel at 0.30 m s⁻¹. Find their frequency and period.
f = vλ=0.300.025=12 Hz
T = 1f=112≈0.083 s

Watch the units: the wavelength must be in metres (2.5 cm = 0.025 m) before dividing.

Worked Example · Phase difference
Two points on a progressive wave of wavelength 0.80 m are separated by 0.60 m along the direction of travel. Find their phase difference in radians and degrees.
Δφ = 2π · dλ=2π × 0.600.80=3π/2 rad

That is 270°, or three-quarters of a cycle. Equivalently the points are π/2 rad (90°) out of phase in the opposite sense.

Worked Example · Reading a CRO
A CRO has its time-base at 2.0 ms per division. A signal shows 2.5 complete cycles across the full 10-division screen. Find the frequency.

Divisions per cycle = 10 / 2.5 = 4.0 div.

T = 4.0 × 2.0 ms = 8.0 ms⇒f = 18.0 × 10⁻³=125 Hz

Read the period off the horizontal axis first, then invert for frequency.

Worked Example · Inverse-square intensity
A point source radiates 90 W uniformly. Find the intensity at 3.0 m, and the distance at which the intensity would be four times greater.
I = 904π(3.0)²≈0.80 W m⁻²

Four times the intensity means half the distance (because I ∝ 1/r²): r = 3.0 / 2 = 1.5 m.

Worked Example · Amplitude and energy
The amplitude of a wave is reduced to 40% of its original value. What fraction of the original intensity remains?
II₀ = A²A₀² = (0.40)²=0.16

Only 16% of the intensity remains — the square law makes intensity very sensitive to amplitude.

AS · Standard

The traps that cost marks on waves questions. Each card pairs the wrong idea with the physics an examiner wants.

"The particles travel along with the wave."

Each particle only oscillates about its own fixed equilibrium position. The profile and its energy move on; the matter stays put.

Energy is transferred, not matter.
"Phase difference is measured in metres."

Phase difference is an angle — radians or degrees — found from Δφ = 2πd/λ. The separation d is a distance, but the phase difference is not.

Phase difference is an angle.
"When a wave speeds up, its frequency increases."

Frequency is fixed by the source. On entering a new medium v and λ change together through v = fλ, but f stays the same.

The source sets f; it does not change.
"Doubling the amplitude doubles the intensity."

Intensity depends on the square of the amplitude. Doubling A multiplies the intensity by four.

I ∝ A², so ×2 amplitude ⇒ ×4 intensity.
"The time-base controls the height of the trace."

The time-base sets the horizontal (time) scale, giving the period. The y-gain sets the vertical scale, giving the amplitude.

Time-base → period; y-gain → amplitude.
"A displacement-distance graph is the same as a displacement-time graph."

A displacement-distance graph is a snapshot of the whole wave and reads off λ. A displacement-time graph tracks one particle and reads off T.

Space graph → λ; time graph → T.
