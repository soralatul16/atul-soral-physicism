1 · How the marks actually work

MYP papers are marked strand by strand, and each criterion is scored on a level from 0 to 8. You reach the top band by showing the skill each strand names, using the word in the question that tells you what to do. Knowing more facts won't get you there on its own.

Command terms set the depth

The command term (the instruction word, such as "state" or "explain") tells you how much to write. Answer at that depth: write less and you lose marks, write more and you waste time.

  • State: one fact or value, no reason. "State the law of reflection."
  • Outline: a brief account in 1–2 sentences.
  • Describe: what happens, in detail, without explaining why.
  • Explain: what happens and why. Use "because" or "therefore" and a physics principle.
  • Calculate: show the equation, the substitution and the answer with its unit.
  • Suggest: apply ideas to an unfamiliar situation. More than one answer can be right.
  • Evaluate / Discuss: give strengths and weaknesses, then a conclusion.
  • Justify: give the evidence or reasoning that supports your answer.

What a top-band (7–8) answer looks like

A 7–8
Explain scientific knowledge, apply it to unfamiliar problems, and analyse and evaluate information to reach a judgment.
B 7–8
Explain the question, give a testable hypothesis with scientific reasoning, explain how variables are changed and measured, and design a complete, safe, logical method.
C 7–8
Correctly present and transform data, accurately interpret it with reasoning, and evaluate the hypothesis and method and explain improvements.
D 7–8
Explain how science is applied to a specific problem, discuss and evaluate its implications, apply scientific language consistently, and document sources completely.

Lower bands use weaker words: "state", "outline", "recall". Use the top-band verbs in your answers.

Do and don't in the exam hall

✔ DO

  • Check the marks in brackets. [3] means three separate points.
  • Draw ray diagrams with a ruler, add arrows on every ray, draw a dashed normal, and label i and r between the ray and the normal.
  • Write the equation before you substitute: n = sin i / sin r.
  • Give answers to 3 significant figures, with a unit (n has none).
  • Use the terms denser medium, normal, critical angle, total internal reflection.
  • Explain the "why": light slows down in glass, so it bends towards the normal.
  • Refer to the data with numbers in Criterion C ("from 10° to 60°, r rose from 6.7° to 35.3°").

✘ DON'T

  • Don't measure angles from the surface of the block. Always measure from the normal.
  • Don't say mirrors "refract". Mirrors reflect, lenses and blocks refract.
  • Don't plot i against r to find n. It curves. Plot sin i against sin r, which gives a straight line with gradient n.
  • Don't write "the light bends because it changes medium" without saying its speed changes.
  • Don't give a hypothesis with no reason, like "As i increases, r increases." Add the Snell's law reasoning.
  • Don't write "human error" in an evaluation. Name the actual error (parallax, a thick ray, ±1° protractor).
  • Don't leave a calculation unfinished. A correct method still earns marks.

AKnowing & Understanding

An exam summary of the core ideas, with simulations and model answers. For the full chapter notes, open Waves → Light.

Reflection vs refraction

  • Reflection: light bounces off a surface and stays in the same medium. Angle of incidence = angle of reflection.
  • Refraction: light changes direction as it crosses into a different medium, because its speed changes.
  • Into a denser medium (air → glass), light slows down and bends towards the normal. Into a less dense medium, it speeds up and bends away from the normal.
  • A ray along the normal (i = 0°) passes straight through without bending, but its speed still changes.

Refractive index: definition

The refractive index n of a material is how many times slower light travels in it than in a vacuum (air ≈ vacuum).

n = cvc = speed in vacuum, v = speed in the material
n = sin isin rlight going from air into the material
n₁ sin i = n₂ sin rany two materials
  • n has no unit and is always ≥ 1. Water 1.33, glass ≈ 1.50, diamond 2.42.
  • A higher n means light travels more slowly in the material and bends more.

Critical angle & TIR

The critical angle c is the angle of incidence in the denser medium that gives an angle of refraction of 90°.

sin c = 1nso c = sin⁻¹(1/n)

Both conditions for TIR

  1. Light travels from a denser to a less dense medium (glass → air).
  2. The angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle.

Examiners expect both conditions. If you give only one, you get half the marks.

Real & apparent depth

A pool looks shallower than it is. Light from the bottom bends away from the normal as it leaves the water, and your brain traces the rays back in straight lines to a point that is too high.

n = real depthapparent depth

Example: a 2.0 m deep pool with n = 1.33 looks 2.0 / 1.33 = 1.50 m deep.

Slab, prism, semicircular block

  • Glass slab: the ray bends towards the normal going in and away coming out. The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but shifted sideways (lateral displacement).
  • Triangular prism: the ray bends towards the base at both faces, so it is deviated. White light disperses into a spectrum because each colour has a slightly different n, and violet bends the most.
  • Semicircular block: aim the ray at the centre of the flat face through the curved side. It enters along a radius, which is the normal, so it doesn't bend there. Only the flat face refracts it, which makes this block ideal for finding the critical angle.

TIR in use

  • Optical fibre: the glass core has a higher n than the cladding, so light hits the boundary above c and reflects all the way along, even round bends. Used in internet cables and endoscopes.
  • Periscope / binoculars: 45° prisms. Glass has c ≈ 42°, and 45° > 42°, so the light is totally reflected. Prisms reflect almost 100% of the light, more than mirrors, and don't tarnish.
  • Diamond: n = 2.42, so c ≈ 24°. Light enters and undergoes TIR many times before leaving through the top, which makes it sparkle.

Lenses & mirrors: sign convention

Use the convention your teacher taught and state it in your answer. With the real-is-positive convention:

1f = 1u + 1vlens / mirror equation
m = vu = image heightobject heightmagnification
  • Converging lens and concave mirror: f is positive. Diverging lens and convex mirror: f is negative.
  • A negative v means a virtual image (on the same side as the object for a lens).
  • Object beyond 2F gives a real, inverted, smaller image. Object between F and the lens gives a virtual, upright, magnified image (a magnifying glass).

Weak answer vs top-band answer

Question: Explain why a ray of light bends towards the normal when it enters a glass block from air. [3]

1 / 3

The light bends because glass is a different material to air.

No mention of speed, no reason, and no link between speed and direction.
3 / 3

Glass is optically denser than air, so light slows down as it enters ✓. One side of the wavefront slows down before the other, which turns the ray ✓. Because the speed decreases, the ray bends towards the normal, so r < i (n = sin i / sin r > 1) ✓.

Three marks for three linked points: cause, mechanism, effect.

BInquiring & Designing

The exam gives you a scenario, usually a glass block, prism or semicircular block with a ray box, and asks you to plan an investigation. Below is a model plan to memorise and adapt.

B i · Research question

Name the IV, the DV, the system and the range.

Top band

How does the angle of incidence (10°–70°) affect the angle of refraction of light passing from air into a rectangular glass block?

Then explain the question in 1–2 lines of physics: light changes speed at the boundary, so the angle of refraction depends on the angle of incidence (Snell's law).

B ii · Hypothesis

Use the structure If… then… because…, and give a scientific reason.

Top band

If the angle of incidence increases, then the angle of refraction will also increase, but it will always be smaller than i. sin i / sin r should stay constant at about 1.5, because Snell's law states that n = sin i / sin r is constant for a given material, and light slows down in glass and bends towards the normal.

B iii · Variables

TypeVariableHow
IVAngle of incidenceSet with a protractor at 10°, 20° … 70° (at least 5 values)
DVAngle of refractionMark the ray with pins or pencil dots, join with a ruler, measure from the normal
CVSame glass blockn depends on the material
CVSame colour of lightn changes with wavelength (dispersion)
CVSame point of entry and block positionDraw round the block so it stays in place
CVDim roomSo the ray can be seen clearly

For each control variable, say why it matters and how you will keep it constant. That's what earns 7–8.

B iv · Method

  1. Place the block on A3 paper, draw round it, and draw a normal at the midpoint of one long side.
  2. Use a protractor to draw incident rays at 10°, 20°, 30°, 40°, 50°, 60° and 70° to the normal.
  3. Shine a thin ray from the ray box (single slit) along the 10° line.
  4. Mark the emergent ray with two crosses far apart, remove the block, and join the entry and exit points to draw the refracted ray.
  5. Measure r from the normal with the protractor (±1°).
  6. Repeat each angle 3 times on fresh lines and calculate the mean r.
  7. Calculate sin i and sin r, then plot sin i (y) against sin r (x).

Safety

  • The ray box bulb gets hot: leave it to cool and don't touch the hood.
  • Laser: Class 2 only, never look into the beam, and keep reflective surfaces away from eye level.
  • Glass block edges can chip: handle carefully and report breakages.

Sufficient data is a top-band phrase: "7 values of i across 10°–70° with 3 repeats each gives enough points to draw a reliable line of best fit." Use it.

CProcessing & Evaluating

Every strand has its own marks: table, transformed data, graph, interpretation, hypothesis, method evaluation and improvements. Use the plotter below to see the difference between the two graphs.

C i · Table & graph

  • Put units in the column headers, not in the cells: Angle of incidence i / °.
  • Give all values to the same number of decimal places. Show the mean column.
  • Give sin values to 3 decimal places.
  • Graph: sin i on the y-axis, sin r on the x-axis. Label both axes, use a sensible scale, plot points as small crosses, and draw one straight line of best fit through the origin.

C ii · Interpret

Describe the trend, quote numbers, then give the physics reason.

Top band

The graph of sin i against sin r is a straight line through the origin, so sin i is directly proportional to sin r. The gradient is (0.866 − 0.174)/(0.578 − 0.117) ≈ 1.50, so n = 1.50, which matches the expected value for glass. This shows light slows down to about c/1.5 in glass.

C iii · Hypothesis

Say whether it is supported and quote the data. Don't write "proved".

"The hypothesis is supported: r increased with i in every trial, and sin i / sin r stayed between 1.48 and 1.51, so it was constant within experimental error."

DReflecting on the impacts of science

Criterion D asks you to connect the physics of light to a real problem and weigh its consequences. Most marks come from explaining the science behind an application and then giving a balanced, evaluated discussion.

The four strands

  • D i: explain how the science is applied to solve a specific problem, e.g. how TIR in optical fibres delivers rural broadband.
  • D ii: discuss and evaluate the implications: moral, ethical, social, economic, political, cultural, environmental.
  • D iii: apply scientific language accurately, e.g. "refracts", "critical angle", "total internal reflection".
  • D iv: document sources fully and consistently (author, title, date, URL, date accessed).

Top-band structure (S-I-E-C)

  1. Science: explain the physics behind the application in 2–3 linked sentences.
  2. Implications: give at least 3 different types, each with a specific example.
  3. Evaluate: for each, a benefit and a limitation. Who gains and who loses?
  4. Conclude: a justified judgement ("On balance… because…").

Use the mnemonic MESSPEC to cover the implication types: Moral, Economic, Social, Scientific, Political, Environmental, Cultural.

Do and don't for D

✔ DO

  • Name a specific case (BharatNet, JWST, LiDAR), not just "technology".
  • Use numbers where possible: cost, distance, number of people.
  • Cite every source, including images.

✘ DON'T

  • List advantages with no physics.
  • Write a one-sided essay.
  • Cite "Google" or "Wikipedia" alone.

Weak answer vs top-band answer

Question: Discuss the implications of using optical fibres to bring internet to rural villages. [6]

2 / 6

Optical fibres are fast and good. Villages can use the internet for school. But it costs a lot of money.

No physics, vague benefits, one limitation and no conclusion.
6 / 6

Data travels as light pulses that stay in the glass core by total internal reflection, because the cladding has a lower refractive index, so signals cross tens of kilometres with little loss ✓. Educational: students reach online lessons ✓. Health: village clinics can consult city doctors by telemedicine ✓. Economic: laying cable costs a lot, and cuts by road works need expensive repairs ✓. Social: a connection alone doesn't close the digital divide without devices and digital skills ✓. On balance fibre is worth it, because it lasts for decades and carries far more data than copper, provided training is funded too ✓.

Science, three or more implication types, benefits and limits, and a justified conclusion.
