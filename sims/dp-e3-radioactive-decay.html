01The nucleus and isotopes

A nucleus is made of protons and neutrons, together called nucleons. It is tiny (about 10⁻¹⁵ m across, 100 000 times smaller than the atom) but contains almost all of the atom’s mass.

Nuclide notation^{A}_{Z}X   A = nucleon (mass) number = protons + neutrons · Z = proton (atomic) number · N = A − Z = neutron number. A nuclide is one particular kind of nucleus, with a definite Z and N.
IsotopesIsotopes of an element have the same number of protons (same Z, so the same element and the same chemistry) but a different number of neutrons (different N and A). For example ^{12}_{6}C, ^{13}_{6}C and ^{14}_{6}C all have 6 protons, with 6, 7 and 8 neutrons. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable; carbon-14 is radioactive.

02Mass defect and binding energy

The mass of a nucleus is always less than the total mass of its separate protons and neutrons. The difference is the mass defect.

DefinitionsMass defect Δm = (mass of the separate nucleons) − (mass of the nucleus).
Binding energy EB = Δm c²: the energy needed to separate a nucleus completely into its individual protons and neutrons. It is also the energy released when the nucleus forms from separate nucleons.
Binding energy is not stored in the nucleusA nucleus with a large binding energy is more tightly bound and has less energy than its separate parts. You must supply the binding energy to pull it apart.

Tables usually give atomic masses (nucleus plus electrons). Using the mass of a hydrogen atom (1.007825 u) for each proton makes the electron masses cancel:

Δm = Z m_{H} + N m_{n} - m_{atom}
Example 2.1The atomic mass of helium-4 is 4.002602 u. Calculate its mass defect in u and kg, and its binding energy in MeV.
Δm = 2 × 1.007825 + 2 × 1.008665 - 4.002602 = 0.030378 u = 0.030378 × 1.661 × 10^{-27} => Δm = 5.05 × 10^{-29} kg E_{B} = 0.030378 × 931.5 => E_{B} = 28.3 MeV # per nucleon: 28.3 ÷ 4 = 7.07 MeV
Example 2.2The atomic mass of iron-56 (26 protons) is 55.934936 u. Determine its binding energy per nucleon.
Δm = 26 × 1.007825 + 30 × 1.008665 - 55.934936 = 0.528464 u E_{B} = 0.528464 × 931.5 = 492.3 MeV => frac{E_{B}}{A} = frac{492.3}{56} = 8.79 MeV
E = mc² in nuclear reactionsIn any nuclear reaction or decay, if the total mass of the products is less than the mass before, the missing mass Δm appears as energy Q = Δm c² (usually as kinetic energy of the products and γ-ray energy). Q = Δm (in u) × 931.5 MeV.

03Binding energy per nucleon

The binding energy per nucleon, EB/A, tells us how tightly each nucleon is held, on average. A higher value means a more stable nucleus.

Interpreting the curve
  • EB/A rises steeply for light nuclei; helium-4 is unusually stable.
  • The peak is near iron-56 / nickel-62 (about 8.8 MeV per nucleon): the most stable nuclei.
  • Joining light nuclei (fusion) or splitting heavy nuclei (fission) moves towards the peak: the products are more tightly bound, so energy is released.
  • Energy released = (total binding energy of the products) − (total binding energy before).
HL · Why is EB/A nearly constant above A ≈ 60?If every nucleon attracted every other one, the total binding energy would grow like A², and EB/A would keep rising with A. Instead it stays roughly constant. This shows the strong force is short-range: each nucleon only attracts its nearest neighbours, so adding more nucleons adds about the same binding energy each. The slow fall for very heavy nuclei is due to the long-range electric repulsion between all the protons, which grows faster than the strong-force binding.

04The strong nuclear force

The strong nuclear forceAn attractive force between nucleons (proton–proton, proton–neutron and neutron–neutron alike) that is much stronger than the electric force at short distances but has a very short range, about 3 × 10⁻¹⁵ m (3 fm). Below about 0.5 fm it becomes strongly repulsive, which stops the nucleons merging.
Linking questionWould a nucleus be able to exist if only gravitational and electric forces were considered?
Example 4.1Compare the electric and gravitational forces between two protons 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m apart.
F_{E} = frac{k e^{2}}{r^{2}} = frac{8.99 × 10^{9} × (1.60 × 10^{-19})^{2}}{(1.0 × 10^{-15})^{2}} => F_{E} = 230 N F_{G} = frac{G m_{p}^{2}}{r^{2}} = frac{6.67 × 10^{-11} × (1.67 × 10^{-27})^{2}}{(1.0 × 10^{-15})^{2}} => F_{G} = 1.9 × 10^{-34} N => frac{F_{E}}{F_{G}} = 1.2 × 10^{36}

The electric repulsion between protons is 10³⁶ times stronger than their gravitational attraction. With only these two forces, every nucleus with more than one proton would fly apart. A third, attractive force must exist: the strong nuclear force.

HL · Evidence for the strong nuclear force
  • Nuclei exist: up to 92 or more protons stay together in spite of huge electric repulsion, so a stronger attractive force must act.
  • Neutrons are bound: the deuteron (one proton and one neutron) is bound although the neutron has no charge, so the force does not depend on charge.
  • Short range: EB/A is about constant above A ≈ 60, and the nuclear density is the same for all nuclei (the radius R = R₀A1/3), so each nucleon is bound only to its neighbours.
  • Scattering: high-energy α particles and protons fired at nuclei stop following the Coulomb (electric) prediction when they come within a few fm, showing a new force acts at that range.
Linking questionHow does equilibrium within a star compare to stability within the nucleus of an atom?

In a star, two long-range effects balance: gravity pulls inwards and gas and radiation pressure push outwards. If one changes, the star expands or contracts until a new balance is reached. In a nucleus, the short-range strong attraction between neighbouring nucleons competes with the long-range electric repulsion between all the protons. A nucleus is stable when no change (decay) could lower its energy: the right balance of neutrons to protons (section 6) and a high binding energy per nucleon. A star can adjust smoothly; a nucleus can only change in steps, by emitting particles.

05Radioactive decay: α, β⁻, β⁺ and γ

Random and spontaneousRadioactive decay is random: it is impossible to predict when a particular nucleus will decay, only the probability that it will decay in a given time. It is spontaneous: it is not affected by temperature, pressure, chemical combination or anything else outside the nucleus. Each nucleus has the same chance of decaying in the next second, however long it has existed.

In every decay, nucleon number A and charge (proton number Z) are conserved, as well as energy and momentum.

Neutrinos and antineutrinosEvery β⁻ decay emits an antineutrino ν̄ and every β⁺ decay emits a neutrino ν. They have no charge and almost no mass, and they interact so weakly that most pass through the whole Earth. They carry away part of the energy and momentum of the decay.
Example 5.1Atomic masses: Ra-226 226.025410 u, Rn-222 222.017578 u, He-4 4.002602 u. Calculate the energy released in the α decay of radium-226.
Δm = 226.025410 - 222.017578 - 4.002602 = 0.005230 u Q = 0.005230 × 931.5 => Q = 4.87 MeV

Most of this is kinetic energy of the α particle (4.78 MeV); the radon nucleus recoils with the rest, because momentum is conserved.

Example 5.2Thorium-232 (Z = 90) decays by α, then β⁻, then β⁻. Determine the final nuclide.
after α: A = 232 - 4 = 228, Z = 90 - 2 = 88 (radium) after β⁻: A = 228, Z = 89 (actinium) after β⁻: A = 228, Z = 90 => final nuclide = thorium-228

An α followed by two β⁻ decays gives an isotope of the original element.

06Nuclear stability and the neutron–proton ratio HL

Plotting every known nuclide on a chart of neutron number N against proton number Z shows a narrow band of stability.

The role of N/Z
  • For light nuclei the stable ratio is N/Z ≈ 1 (e.g. C-12, O-16).
  • Heavier nuclei need extra neutrons (N/Z up to about 1.5 for lead-208): neutrons add strong-force attraction without adding electric repulsion.
  • Above the band (neutron-rich): β⁻ decay turns a neutron into a proton, moving down and right towards the band.
  • Below the band (proton-rich): β⁺ decay (or electron capture) turns a proton into a neutron.
  • Very heavy nuclei (A above about 200, Z above 83): α decay removes 2p and 2n at once. No nuclide with Z above 83 is stable.
How large unstable nuclei become stable: decay chainsUranium-238 reaches stable lead-206 through 14 decays: 8 α and 6 β⁻. The α decays reduce A by 32 (238 → 206) and Z by 16; the 6 β⁻ decays raise Z by 6, so Z goes 92 → 82. Each step moves the nucleus closer to the band of stability. Explore the whole chain in Decay types → N–Z chart.

07Nuclear energy levels and decay spectra HL

Like electrons in atoms, a nucleus can only exist in certain discrete energy levels. The evidence comes from the energies of the radiation emitted.

α and γ spectra: discrete lines → discrete nuclear energy levelsThe α particles from one nuclide have only a few definite energies. Americium-241 emits α particles of 5.486 MeV (85%), 5.443 MeV (13%) and 5.388 MeV (2%). Each leaves the daughter nucleus (neptunium-237) in a different energy level, which then emits a γ-ray of a definite energy to drop to a lower level (for example 59.5 keV). The γ-ray energies match the gaps between the α energies.
Linking questionAre there differences between the photons emitted as a result of atomic versus nuclear transitions?

Both are photons, travelling at c with energy E = hf, and both come in discrete energies because both atoms and nuclei have discrete energy levels. The difference is the energy: electron transitions in atoms give photons of a few eV (visible, ultraviolet) up to keV (X-rays), while nuclear transitions give γ-ray photons of keV to several MeV, about a million times more energetic, because nucleons are bound far more strongly than electrons.

The β spectrum is continuous: evidence for the neutrinoIf β⁻ decay were just nucleus → nucleus + electron, momentum and energy conservation would give every electron the same energy, as in α decay. In fact β electrons have every energy from zero up to a maximum Emax (equal to Q). The missing energy is carried away by a third particle, the antineutrino, which shares the energy in a different proportion in each decay.
Linking question (NOS)How did conservation lead to experimental evidence of the neutrino?

In 1930 the continuous β spectrum seemed to break the law of conservation of energy. Rather than give up conservation, Wolfgang Pauli proposed a new, neutral, almost massless particle that carried away the missing energy and momentum. Enrico Fermi named it the neutrino (“little neutral one”) and built a theory of β decay around it. Because neutrinos interact so weakly, it took 26 years to detect one: in 1956 Clyde Cowan and Frederick Reines placed tanks of water and detectors next to a nuclear reactor, which produces about 10²⁰ antineutrinos per second, and saw the reactions they caused. Faith in a conservation law predicted a particle decades before it could be observed.

08Penetration and ionising ability

Radiation ionises atoms it passes: it knocks electrons out of them. Each ionisation uses some of its energy, so the more strongly a radiation ionises, the sooner it runs out of energy and the less penetrating it is.

Inside the body α is the most dangerous (all its energy is deposited in a tiny volume of tissue); outside the body it is the least dangerous, because it cannot pass the dead outer layer of the skin.

09Activity, count rate and background radiation

DefinitionsActivity A: the number of nuclei that decay per second. Unit: the becquerel, 1 Bq = 1 decay per second.
Count rate: the number of decays per second (or per minute) recorded by a detector such as a Geiger–Müller tube. It is always less than the activity, because radiation goes off in all directions and the detector does not record every particle that enters it.
Background radiationA detector always records some counts even with no source present: background radiation. Sources: radon gas from rocks and soil (about half), rocks and building materials, food and drink (potassium-40, carbon-14), cosmic rays from space, and medical and industrial uses. A school GM tube typically records about 15–30 counts per minute of background.
Corrected count rate = measured count rate − background count rate. Measure the background for a long time with no source present, then subtract it from every reading.
Example 9.1A background count of 180 counts is recorded in 10 minutes. With a source in place, 1240 counts are recorded in 2 minutes. Calculate the corrected count rate.
background = frac{180}{10} = 18 counts per minute measured = frac{1240}{2} = 620 counts per minute => corrected count rate = 620 - 18 = 602 counts per minute
Randomness in countsBecause decay is random, repeated counts in equal times vary. Counting for longer (more counts) makes a reading more reliable: the uncertainty in a count of n is about √n, so 10 000 counts are about 1% uncertain, but 100 counts are about 10% uncertain.

10Half-life

Half-life T½The time taken for half the nuclei of a radioactive nuclide in a sample to decay. Equivalently, the time for the activity (or the corrected count rate) to fall to half. It is constant for a given nuclide and ranges from fractions of a second to billions of years.

Example 10.1Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8 days. A sample has an activity of 6400 Bq. Determine its activity after 32 days, and the fraction of the nuclei that have decayed.
number of half-lives = frac{32}{8} = 4 activity = 6400 × (frac{1}{2})^{4} = frac{6400}{16} => activity = 400 Bq => fraction decayed = 1 - frac{1}{16} = frac{15}{16}
Determining a half-life experimentally
  1. Measure the background count rate with no source present.
  2. Record the count rate from the source at regular intervals (for example protactinium-234m, T½ ≈ 70 s).
  3. Subtract the background from every reading.
  4. Plot corrected count rate against time and draw a smooth curve of best fit.
  5. Read off the time for the count rate to fall to half, from several starting points, and average them.
  6. HL Better: plot ln(count rate) against time. It is a straight line of gradient −λ, so T½ = ln 2 / λ.
For a very long half-life (such as uranium-238), the activity hardly changes during an experiment: instead measure the activity A and the number of nuclei N (from the mass), and use λ = A/N.

11The radioactive decay law HL

Each nucleus has the same probability of decaying per unit time, called the decay constant λ (unit s⁻¹). So the number decaying per second is proportional to the number of nuclei present:

A = - frac{ΔN}{Δt} = λ N N = N_{0} e^{-λ t} A = λ N_{0} e^{-λ t} = A_{0} e^{-λ t} T_{½} = frac{ln 2}{λ}
What λ meansλ is the probability that a nucleus decays in unit time only if λt is very small. The exact probability of decaying within a time t is 1 − e−λt, which is close to λt only when λt ≪ 1. For example, with λt = 0.01 the probability is 0.00995 ≈ 0.01; but with λt = 1 it is 0.63, not 1.
Example 11.1Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8.02 days. Calculate its decay constant, and the activity of 1.0 μg of iodine-131 (molar mass 131 g mol⁻¹).
λ = frac{ln 2}{8.02 × 86 400} = 1.00 × 10^{-6} s^{-1} N = frac{1.0 × 10^{-6} g}{131 g mol^{-1}} × 6.02 × 10^{23} = 4.60 × 10^{15} A = λ N = 1.00 × 10^{-6} × 4.60 × 10^{15} => A = 4.6 × 10^{9} Bq
Example 11.2Living wood has a carbon-14 activity of 0.255 Bq per gram of carbon. An ancient wooden tool has 0.080 Bq per gram. T½ of carbon-14 = 5730 years. Estimate the age of the tool.
λ = frac{ln 2}{5730} = 1.21 × 10^{-4} y^{-1} A = A_{0} e^{-λ t} ⇒ t = frac{1}{λ} ln frac{A_{0}}{A} t = frac{1}{1.21 × 10^{-4}} × ln frac{0.255}{0.080} => t = 9600 years

Check: 0.080/0.255 ≈ 0.31, between 1/2 and 1/4, so the age is between 1 and 2 half-lives (5730 and 11 460 years).

Linking question (NOS)Which areas of physics involve exponential change?

Exponential change happens whenever the rate of change of a quantity is proportional to the quantity itself. Examples: radioactive decay; the absorption of γ-rays and X-rays in matter (each thickness removes the same fraction); the cooling of a hot object when the rate of heat loss is proportional to the temperature difference; the amplitude of a damped oscillation; the growth of neutrons in a supercritical chain reaction. In each case there is a constant “halving time” (or doubling time) and a graph of ln(quantity) against time is a straight line.

Common exam mistakes
  • Saying binding energy is energy “stored in” or “released by” the nucleus when it splits.
  • Forgetting the electrons: use atomic masses consistently (mH for protons).
  • Not subtracting background before finding a half-life.
  • Saying a sample has “gone” after two half-lives: a quarter is left.
  • Confusing activity (decays per second of the source) with count rate (what the detector records).
  • Forgetting the (anti)neutrino in β equations, or getting ν and ν̄ the wrong way round.
  • Writing a β⁻ particle as ^{0}_{1}e instead of ^{0}_{-1}e.
  • Mixing units in λ: if T½ is in days, λ is in day⁻¹.
  • Saying γ-rays are “stopped” by lead: they are only reduced.

14One-page summary

E.3 Radioactive decay (SL & HL) · one-page summary

The nucleus

  • A = Z + N
  • Isotopes: same Z, different N
  • 1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 931.5 MeV c⁻²
  • Strong force: attractive, range ≈ 3 fm, repulsive < 0.5 fm

Binding energy

  • Δm = ZmH + Nmn − matom
  • EB = Δmc² (energy to pull apart)
  • Peak EB/A ≈ 8.8 MeV at Fe-56
  • Fusion and fission move towards the peak
  • HL: EB/A ≈ constant above A ≈ 60

Decay

  • Random and spontaneous
  • α: A − 4, Z − 2
  • β⁻: n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ (Z + 1)
  • β⁺: p → n + e⁺ + ν (Z − 1)
  • γ: excited → lower energy state

Radiation

  • α: strongly ionising, paper stops it
  • β: medium, a few mm Al
  • γ: weakly ionising, reduced by cm of Pb
  • Subtract background count rate
  • Count rate < activity

Half-life

  • Time for N (or A) to halve
  • After n half-lives: (1/2)n remains
  • Find T½ from a corrected count-rate graph
  • Isotope choice: radiation type + half-life

HL

  • N/Z: above band β⁻, below β⁺, heavy α
  • Discrete α and γ → nuclear levels
  • Continuous β → neutrino
  • N = N₀e−λt, A = λN
  • T½ = ln 2 / λ; λ ≈ probability only if λt ≪ 1

Top 8 mistakes to avoid

  1. Binding energy must be supplied, not released, to split a nucleus.
  2. Use atomic masses consistently.
  3. Subtract background first.
  4. Two half-lives leave a quarter.
  5. Activity ≠ count rate.
  6. β⁻ with ν̄, β⁺ with ν.
  7. Keep λ and t in the same time unit.
  8. γ is reduced, never fully stopped.
