Drop something and two forces decide its fate: its weight pulling it down, and the air (or fluid) pushing back as it moves. How those two compare, moment to moment, is the whole story of terminal velocity.

Free fall

An object is in free fall when gravity is the only force acting on it — no air resistance at all. Its acceleration is constant, equal to the gravitational field strength g (about 9.8 m·s⁻² on Earth, 1.6 m·s⁻² on the Moon). In a true vacuum, a feather and a hammer fall side by side and speed up forever until they land — there is no terminal velocity, because nothing ever pushes back. Try the Moon scenario to see this.

Air resistance (drag)

As soon as an object moves through a fluid, the fluid pushes back with a drag force that opposes the motion. Crucially, drag grows with speed — for everyday falling objects it rises roughly with the square of the speed:

Fd=12CdρAv2
Bigger area A, denser fluid ρ, blunter shape Cd, or faster speed v → more drag.

The journey to terminal velocity

1

The moment of release

Speed is zero, so drag is zero. The only force is weight, so the net force is largest and the acceleration is greatest — equal to g.

2

Speeding up

As the object gains speed, drag grows. The net downward force shrinks, so the acceleration gets smaller — the object still speeds up, but more and more gently.

3

Terminal velocity

Eventually drag grows until it exactly balances the weight. The net force is zero, so the acceleration is zero. The object keeps falling at a constant maximum speed — the terminal velocity.

The terminal velocity itself

At terminal velocity the forces balance: weight = drag. Setting mg = ½ Cd ρ A vt2 and rearranging gives:

vt=2mgCdρA
Heavier objects fall faster; larger area, denser fluid or blunter shapes fall slower.
Upthrust. In a dense fluid like water there is also an upward upthrust (buoyancy) equal to the weight of fluid displaced. It reduces the effective downward force, so the balance becomes weight = drag + upthrust. In air it is tiny and usually ignored; in water it matters — switch on the upthrust arrow in the steel-ball scenario.

Why a parachute works

A parachute suddenly multiplies the area A and the drag coefficient C_d. Drag leaps far above the weight, so the net force points upward and the skydiver decelerates — quickly settling to a new, much lower terminal velocity that is safe to land at. Deploy the parachute mid-fall and watch the velocity–time graph drop to a new plateau.

Everyday terminal velocities

🪂

Skydiver

Belly-to-earth ≈ 55 m·s⁻¹; under a parachute ≈ 5–6 m·s⁻¹.

🌧️

Raindrop

A few m·s⁻¹ — small mass, so drag balances weight quickly.

🪶

Feather

Very low — large area for a tiny weight.

🌕

On the Moon

No atmosphere → no drag → no terminal velocity: free fall all the way.

Q1 · Describe

Describe how the acceleration of a falling skydiver changes from the moment of release until terminal velocity.

At release, speed is zero so there is no air resistance; the only force is weight, giving the maximum acceleration (equal to g). As speed increases, air resistance increases, so the resultant downward force decreases and the acceleration decreases. When air resistance grows to equal the weight, the resultant force is zero, the acceleration is zero, and the skydiver falls at a constant terminal velocity.
Q2 · Explain

Two identical balls are dropped, one on Earth and one on the Moon. Explain why only the one on Earth reaches a terminal velocity.

Terminal velocity requires a resistive force that grows with speed until it balances the weight. The Moon has no atmosphere, so there is no air resistance — the net force stays equal to the weight and the ball keeps accelerating at g (about 1.6 m·s⁻²) until it lands. On Earth, air resistance builds up until it balances the weight, giving a constant terminal velocity.
Q3 · Predict

A skydiver opens a parachute. Using the idea of forces, explain what happens to the velocity, and sketch the shape of the velocity–time graph.

Opening the parachute greatly increases the area and drag, so the air resistance becomes much larger than the weight. The resultant force now acts upward, so the skydiver decelerates. As they slow down, drag decreases until it again equals the weight, reaching a new, lower terminal velocity. On the velocity–time graph the line rises to the first terminal velocity, drops steeply when the parachute opens, then levels off at the lower terminal velocity.
Q4 · Apply

Why does a heavier object generally have a higher terminal velocity than a lighter object of the same size and shape?

Both experience the same drag at a given speed (same size and shape), but the heavier object has a larger weight. A larger drag force — and therefore a higher speed — is needed before drag balances the weight. From vt = √(2mg / CdρA), vt increases with mass. Test it: increase the mass slider and watch the terminal-velocity line rise.
Q5 · Analyse

Using the simulator, describe the shape of the velocity–time graph before terminal velocity and explain what the gradient represents.

The graph is a curve that rises steeply at first and then bends over, becoming a horizontal line at terminal velocity. The gradient of the velocity–time graph is the acceleration: it starts at its largest value (g) and decreases to zero as the curve flattens, because the resultant force falls to zero when drag balances weight.
Q6 · Water

A steel ball is dropped into water. State the three forces acting on it as it sinks, and write the condition for terminal velocity.

The forces are: weight (down), upthrust/buoyancy (up), and drag/water resistance (up). Terminal velocity is reached when the upward forces balance the downward weight: weight = upthrust + drag, so the resultant force and acceleration are zero.
