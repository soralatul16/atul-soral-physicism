1 · How the marks actually work

E.1 marks are lost by stating Rutherford’s conclusions without the observations that led to them, by using one energy level instead of the gap between two, by mixing eV and J, and, at HL, by forgetting the charge 2e of the α particle in closest-approach calculations. How a DP markscheme thinks

  • One mark per marking point (MP1, MP2…). “Max 4” means more points are listed than marks available.
  • Observation → conclusion: for Rutherford, each conclusion only scores when it is paired with the observation that supports it.
  • Error carried forward (ECF): a wrong ΔE used correctly in λ = hc/ΔE still earns the later mark.
  • “Show that”: give the steps and one more significant figure than the printed answer.
  • Spectra answers need the words photon, energy level and difference, not just “electrons jump”.

Command terms you will meet

  • Describe: the Geiger–Marsden apparatus, or how a spectrum is produced.
  • Outline: observations and conclusions, briefly.
  • Explain: link each line in a spectrum to a transition between two levels.
  • Calculate / Determine: working, then an answer with a unit.
  • Deduce: which transition, or which elements, from given data.
  • Compare: emission and absorption spectra, referring to both.

Do and don’t in the exam hall

✔ DO

  • Give the fraction: “about 1 in 8000 were deflected through more than 90°”.
  • Use ΔE = Eupper − Elower; both values are negative.
  • Use λ(nm) ≈ 1240 / ΔE(eV) to check your answer quickly.
  • HL: use charge 2e for the α particle and Ze for the nucleus.
  • HL: cube-root A before multiplying by R₀ = 1.20 fm.

✘ DON’T

  • Don’t say α particles “bounced off” the nucleus: they are repelled before touching it.
  • Don’t say most α particles were deflected backwards: very few were.
  • Don’t say an atom absorbs “any photon with enough energy”: below ionisation it must match a gap exactly.
  • Don’t confuse an absorption spectrum (dark lines) with an emission spectrum (bright lines).
  • HL: don’t treat the closest approach as the nuclear radius: it is an upper limit.

1APaper 1A: multiple choice

About 2 minutes per question. E.1 multiple-choice questions test which observation shows what in Rutherford scattering, counting nucleons, and quick energy-level arithmetic.

Fast methods

  • Photon wavelength: λ(nm) ≈ 1240 / ΔE(eV).
  • Number of lines from n levels: n(n − 1)/2.
  • Longest wavelength = smallest gap; shortest wavelength = largest gap.
  • HL radius ratio: R₁/R₂ = (A₁/A₂)1/3.
  • HL closest approach: d ∝ Z / Ek.

Traps the wrong options use

  • “Most α particles bounced back.”
  • Using one level’s energy (e.g. 3.7 eV) instead of the gap.
  • Adding two level energies instead of subtracting.
  • “A photon of any energy above the gap is absorbed.” Only a colliding electron can give part of its energy.
  • HL: radius ∝ A instead of A1/3.

Worked example

An atom has energy levels at −10.4, −5.5, −3.7 and −1.6 eV. What is the wavelength of the photon emitted when an electron falls from −3.7 eV to −10.4 eV?

  1. ΔE = −3.7 − (−10.4) = 6.7 eV.
  2. λ ≈ 1240 / 6.7 = 185 nm (ultraviolet).
  3. The traps: 335 nm (used 3.7 eV), 119 nm (used 10.4 eV), 88 nm (added the levels to get 14.1 eV).

1BPaper 1B: data-based questions

20 marks, about 40 minutes. E.1 data comes from Geiger and Marsden’s counts at different angles, from measured spectral lines compared with reference wavelengths, and at HL from nuclear radii against nucleon number.

Test a relationship

  • If N ∝ 1/sin⁴(θ/2), then N sin⁴(θ/2) should be the same for every row.
  • Calculate it for at least three rows spread across the table, then comment on how close they are.
  • HL: if R = R₀A1/3, a graph of R against A1/3 is a straight line through the origin with gradient R₀.

Matching spectra

  1. List each reference line and look for it in the measured spectrum.
  2. An element is present only if all of its strong lines are there.
  3. Say clearly which elements have no matching lines.

Uncertainty

Where the data change rapidly (sin⁴(θ/2) at small angles), a small uncertainty in the measured angle gives a large percentage uncertainty in the result. Say this when a point fits the pattern poorly.

Worked example: testing Rutherford’s prediction

Geiger and Marsden counted 477 α particles at 60° and 51.9 at 120°. Do these fit N ∝ 1/sin⁴(θ/2)?

  1. Predicted ratio N(60°)/N(120°) = sin⁴60° / sin⁴30° = 0.5625 / 0.0625 = 9.0.
  2. Measured ratio = 477 / 51.9 = 9.2.
  3. These agree to about 2%, which supports the nuclear model.

2Paper 2: structured and extended answers

E.1 Paper 2 questions pair a description (the experiment, or how a spectrum forms) with a short calculation from an energy-level diagram. At HL, expect closest approach, R = R₀A1/3 and Bohr-model calculations.

Weak answer vs top answer

Outline the observations of the Geiger–Marsden experiment and the conclusions Rutherford drew from them. [4]

1 / 4

Alpha particles were fired at gold and some bounced back, so the atom has a nucleus.

“Some bounced back” has no fraction and no angle, and only one conclusion is given, with nothing about empty space, charge or mass.
4 / 4

Most α particles passed straight through the foil or were deflected through very small angles MP1, so most of the atom is empty space MP2. A very small fraction, about 1 in 8000, were deflected through more than 90° MP3, so the positive charge and nearly all the mass must be concentrated in a tiny nucleus that repels the α particles strongly MP4.

Two observations, each paired with the conclusion it supports.

Explain how atomic line spectra provide evidence for discrete energy levels in atoms. [3]

1 / 3

Electrons jump between shells and give off light of different colours.

No mention of photon energy, of the gap between two levels, or of why only certain lines appear.
3 / 3

Each line has a single wavelength, so its photons all have one energy, E = hc/λ MP1. A photon is emitted when an electron moves from a higher to a lower level, and its energy equals the difference between them MP2. Only certain lines are seen, so only certain energy differences exist: the electron’s energy can take only discrete values MP3.

Line → photon energy → difference between levels → discrete.

Energy-level chain

  1. ΔE = Eupper − Elower (in eV).
  2. × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ to get joules.
  3. λ = hc / ΔE, or f = ΔE / h.
  4. Check the region: 400–700 nm is visible.

HL nucleus chain

  1. Ek in MeV × 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ = joules.
  2. Closest approach: d = k(2e)(Ze) / Ek.
  3. Radius: R = R₀A1/3, with R₀ = 1.20 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
  4. Bohr: En = −13.6/n² eV, then the energy-level chain.

7 · Before the exam

Equations for E.1

E = hf = hc / λ ΔE = Eupper − Elower R = R₀A1/3 HL Ek = kq₁q₂ / d HL E = −13.6 / n² eV HL mvr = nh / 2π HL

Constants and numbers to know

  • h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s · c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ · e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
  • hc ≈ 1240 eV nm, so a 2 eV photon is about 620 nm
  • atom ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m, nucleus ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m
  • R₀ = 1.20 × 10⁻¹⁵ m and nuclear density ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³ HL
  • k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²; α charge = +2e HL
  • hydrogen: −13.6, −3.40, −1.51, −0.85 eV for n = 1 to 4 HL

Final checklist

  • I can describe the Geiger–Marsden experiment and pair each observation with its conclusion.
  • I can read nuclide notation and find protons, neutrons and electrons.
  • I can explain how emission and absorption spectra form and how they identify elements.
  • I can explain why line spectra show discrete energy levels and use ΔE = hf = hc/λ.
  • HL: I can use R = R₀A1/3 and show that nuclear density is constant.
  • HL: I can calculate the distance of closest approach and explain deviations at high energy.
  • HL: I can use the Bohr model and state its limitations.
