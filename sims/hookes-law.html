Conclusion

Stretch a spring gently and it springs back; the harder you pull, the more it stretches — and, up to a point, exactly in proportion. That proportionality is Hooke's law.

Hooke's law

For a spring (or wire) loaded within its limit of proportionality, the extension is directly proportional to the force applied:

F=kx
F is the force (N), x is the extension (m), and k is the spring constant (N·m⁻¹).

The spring constant k measures stiffness: a large k means a stiff spring that extends little for a given force. On a force–extension graph, Hooke's law gives a straight line through the origin, and its gradient is k.

Extension, not length

What matters is the extension — how much longer the spring has become — not its total length. Extension x = (stretched length) − (natural length). If you plotted force against total length you would get the same straight line, just shifted along by the natural length.

Limit of proportionality & elastic limit

1

Proportional region

Below the limit of proportionality, F ∝ x — the graph is a straight line and the spring returns to its original length when unloaded.

2

Past the limit of proportionality

The line starts to curve: the spring extends more for each extra newton, so F is no longer proportional to x.

3

Past the elastic limit

The spring is permanently deformed (plastic behaviour) — it no longer returns to its original length when the load is removed.

Elastic potential energy

Work done stretching the spring is stored as elastic potential energy. It equals the area under the force–extension graph. Within the proportional region that area is a triangle:

E=12kx2
Equivalently E = ½Fx, since F = kx. Double the extension → four times the stored energy.
What about rubber? A rubber band does not obey Hooke's law — its force–extension graph is a curve from the start, and loading and unloading follow different paths. Hooke's law applies to springs and wires within their limit of proportionality, not to all elastic materials.
Q1 · State

State Hooke's law in words and as an equation, and define each symbol.

Hooke's law: the extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force applied, provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded. As an equation, F = kx, where F is the applied force (N), x is the extension (m) and k is the spring constant (N·m⁻¹).
Q2 · Calculate

A spring extends by 0.20 m when a force of 5.0 N is applied. Find its spring constant, then check with the simulator.

Rearrange k = F/x = 5.0 / 0.20 = 25 N·m⁻¹.
Q3 · Graph

On the force–extension graph you plot in the simulator, what does the gradient represent, and what does the area under the line represent?

The gradient of a force–extension graph is the spring constant k (since F = kx). The area under the line is the work done on the spring, which is stored as elastic potential energy, E = ½kx².
Q4 · Energy

A spring with k = 40 N·m⁻¹ is stretched by 0.15 m. Calculate the elastic potential energy stored.

Use E = ½kx² = ½ × 40 × (0.15)² = ½ × 40 × 0.0225 = 0.45 J.
Q5 · Explain

Explain the difference between the limit of proportionality and the elastic limit.

The limit of proportionality is the point beyond which extension is no longer proportional to force (the graph stops being a straight line). The elastic limit is the point beyond which the spring is permanently deformed and no longer returns to its original length when the load is removed. Load the spring past each in the simulator to see the line bend, then the permanent set.
Q6 · Compare

Two springs are stretched by the same force. Spring A extends more than spring B. Which has the larger spring constant, and why?

Spring B has the larger spring constant. From k = F/x, for the same force F the spring with the smaller extension x has the larger k — it is stiffer. Compare the soft and stiff spring presets to confirm.
