Conclusion

Push a door near its hinge and it barely moves; push near the handle and it swings easily. Same force, different turning effect — because the turning effect depends on distance from the pivot. That turning effect is the moment of a force.

The moment of a force

The moment of a force is a measure of its turning effect about a pivot. Everyday examples: a spanner loosening a nut, a see-saw, a wheelbarrow, a door handle placed far from the hinges, and scissors or a crowbar. In every case a force acts at a distance from a pivot to produce rotation.

moment=F×d
F is the force (N), d is the perpendicular distance from the pivot (m), and the moment is measured in newton-metres (N·m).

The larger the force, or the greater the perpendicular distance from the pivot, the larger the moment. That is why a longer spanner turns a stiff nut more easily.

The principle of moments

When a beam is balanced on a pivot, it is in rotational equilibrium. The principle of moments states that, for a body in equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about any pivot equals the sum of the anticlockwise moments about that pivot:

∑Manticlockwise=∑Mclockwise
If several forces act on one side, add their moments together before comparing the two sides.

Conditions for equilibrium

1

No resultant force

The forces up and down (and left and right) balance, so the object does not accelerate in any direction.

2

No resultant moment

The clockwise and anticlockwise moments about any point are equal, so the object does not start to rotate.

3

Both together

An object is in equilibrium only when there is no resultant force and no resultant moment. Meet just one condition and it can still move or spin.

More than one force each side

Real beams often carry several loads on each side of the pivot. Each load contributes its own moment, F×d, using its own distance from the pivot. To test for balance, add up every anticlockwise moment and every clockwise moment separately, then compare the two totals. Add a second load to each side in the simulator to see this in action.

Demonstrating no resultant moment

Balance a uniform metre rule on a pivot at its centre so it rests level with no load. Hang a known weight on one side and adjust a second weight on the other side until the rule is level again. Measure each weight and its distance from the pivot, then check that (weight×distance) on one side equals (weight×distance) on the other. Repeat for different distances: each time the two products match, showing there is no resultant moment when the beam is in equilibrium.

Watch the pivot. Moments are always taken about a chosen pivot, and distance must be measured perpendicular to the line of the force. Taking moments about the pivot is convenient because the force at the pivot itself has zero moment — its distance from the pivot is zero.
Q1 · State

Define the moment of a force in words and as an equation, define each symbol, and give one everyday example.

The moment of a force is a measure of its turning effect about a pivot. As an equation, moment = F × d, where F is the force (N) and d is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of the force (m); the moment is in N·m. Example: using a long spanner to turn a stiff nut — a larger distance gives a larger turning effect for the same force.
Q2 · Calculate

A force of 20 N is applied at a perpendicular distance of 0.50 m from a pivot. Calculate the moment, then check with the simulator.

Use moment = F × d = 20 × 0.50 = 10 N·m.
Q3 · Principle

A beam is balanced on a central pivot. A 4.0 N weight sits 3.0 m to the left. What force placed 2.0 m to the right will keep the beam balanced?

For balance, clockwise moment = anticlockwise moment. Anticlockwise = 4.0 × 3.0 = 12 N·m. So F × 2.0 = 12, giving F = 6.0 N. Set this up in the simulator and confirm the beam balances.
Q4 · Equilibrium

State the two conditions that must both be met for an object to be in equilibrium.

(1) There is no resultant force acting on the object. (2) There is no resultant moment about any point. Both conditions must hold at the same time.
Q5 · Multiple forces

On the left of a pivot, a 2.0 N weight is 1.0 m away and a 3.0 N weight is 0.40 m away. On the right, a single 4.0 N weight balances the beam. How far from the pivot is it?

Total anticlockwise moment = (2.0 × 1.0) + (3.0 × 0.40) = 2.0 + 1.2 = 3.2 N·m. For balance, 4.0 × d = 3.2, so d = 0.80 m. Recreate it with two loads on the left in the simulator.
Q6 · Explain

Explain why a door handle is placed far from the hinges rather than close to them.

The hinges act as the pivot. Placing the handle far from the hinges gives a large perpendicular distance d, so even a small force F produces a large moment (F × d) and turns the door easily. A handle near the hinges would need a much larger force to produce the same turning effect.
