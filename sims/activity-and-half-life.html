Theme E.3 · Decay is random

Activity & Half-Life

Single nuclei decay at random, yet a whole population thins out on a perfectly predictable curve. This part turns that paradox into numbers: count rate, activity in becquerel, and the half-life that ties them together.

You can never say when a particular nucleus will decay — only the chance it decays in the next second. Watch a population of unstable nuclei and see order rise out of pure randomness.

Population of 196 nuclei

Each glowing square is one unstable nucleus. Every tick, every surviving nucleus rolls the same dice — the chance of decay never changes and never "remembers" how long it has waited.

What the dice produce

Two words that mean two different things

Random means we cannot predict which nucleus decays next, or exactly when any single one will. Spontaneous means the decay is not triggered by anything outside the nucleus — heating the sample, cooling it, squeezing it, or bonding the atom into a different chemical compound changes nothing.

The jagged early counts on the graph are randomness showing through. Push the population higher and the curve smooths into the clean exponential you will measure in the lab — large numbers wash out the luck of individual rolls.

A Geiger–Müller tube clicks each time ionising radiation passes through it. But some clicks come from the source and some come from the world around us — the real measurement is what is left after you subtract the background.

GM tube & count rate

Grey ticks are background counts (cosmic rays, rocks, air). Coloured ticks come from your source. The tube cannot tell them apart.

Reading the counter

Count rate is not activity

Count rate is how many clicks the tube registers each second (counts per second, cps). Activity is how many nuclei actually decay each second in the whole source. The tube only sees the tiny cone of radiation that happens to pass through its window, so the count rate is always far smaller than the activity.

Before any serious measurement you record the background count rate with the source removed, then subtract it from every later reading. That subtracted value is the corrected count rate — the honest signal from your source.

Activity is simply the number of decays per second. Its unit, the becquerel, is one of the smallest named units in physics — one single decay each second.

Only a fraction reaches the detector

The geometry calculation

fraction = X / (4πr²)

Why distance matters so much

The source throws its radiation out equally in every direction, spreading it over the surface of an imaginary sphere of area 4πr². A detector of window area X catches only the fraction X / (4πr²) of that sphere.

Because the sphere's area grows with r², doubling the distance cuts the fraction to a quarter. This is the inverse-square fall-off, and it is pure geometry — it assumes nothing is absorbed on the way and that the source emits evenly in all directions.

The half-life is the time for the activity (or the number of undecayed nuclei) to fall to half of its value. After each half-life, whatever is left halves again — so the curve never quite reaches zero.

Interactive decay curve

Halving, step by step

The whole-number shortcut

After n whole half-lives the fraction remaining is simply (½)ⁿ. One half-life leaves 1/2, two leave 1/4, three leave 1/8, and so on. This is all you need whenever the elapsed time is a neat multiple of the half-life.

remaining fraction after n half-lives = (1/2)ⁿ  →  A = A₀ × (1/2)^(t / T½)

For times that are not a whole number of half-lives you need the exponential law — that is the job of Part 4. For now, master reading the curve and the halving table beside it.

Four original problems, each solved the way you should set out your working in an exam. Try each one before revealing the solution.

Worked Example · Half-life counting
A fresh sample of a radioisotope has an activity of 9600 Bq. Its half-life is 7.5 hours. What is the activity after 30 hours, and what fraction of the original nuclei is still undecayed?

Number of half-lives: 30 ÷ 7.5 = 4 half-lives.

A = 9600 × (1/2)⁴ = 9600 × 1/16 = 600 Bq

The fraction of nuclei still undecayed equals the fraction of activity remaining, since activity is proportional to the number present: (1/2)⁴ = 1/16 ≈ 6.25%.

Worked Example · Finding the half-life from data
A detector near a source reads a corrected count rate of 1280 cps. Exactly 18 minutes later it reads 160 cps. Determine the half-life of the source.

Find how many times the rate has halved: 1280 → 640 → 320 → 160, that is 3 halvings.

3 × T½ = 18 min  →  T½ = 18 / 3 = 6 minutes

Check: 1280 × (1/2)³ = 1280 / 8 = 160 cps. ✓ The word "corrected" matters — the background has already been subtracted, otherwise the ratios would not land on clean halvings.

Worked Example · Background correction
With the source removed, a GM tube records 48 counts in 2 minutes. With the source in place it records 1848 counts in the same 2 minutes. Find the corrected count rate due to the source alone, in counts per second.

Background rate: 48 counts / 120 s = 0.40 cps.

Total rate with source: 1848 / 120 = 15.4 cps.

corrected rate = 15.4 − 0.40 = 15.0 cps

Always subtract the background as a rate, not as raw counts, unless both were measured over exactly the same time (as here, where you could also do (1848 − 48)/120 = 15.0 cps).

Worked Example · Detector geometry
A small source of activity 2.0 × 10⁵ Bq emits equally in all directions. A detector of window area 3.0 cm² is placed 20 cm away. Assuming no absorption, estimate how many of the source's decays are detected each second.

Surface area of the sphere at r = 20 cm: 4πr² = 4π(20)² = 5027 cm².

fraction = X / (4πr²) = 3.0 / 5027 = 5.97 × 10⁻⁴

Detected per second = fraction × activity = 5.97 × 10⁻⁴ × 2.0 × 10⁵ ≈ 119 counts s⁻¹.

So out of 200 000 decays every second, only about 120 reach this detector — a vivid reminder that count rate is a small, geometry-dependent slice of the true activity.

The traps that cost marks in exams. Each card pairs the tempting wrong idea with the correct physics.

"After one half-life everything has decayed."

After one half-life exactly half remains. After two, a quarter; after three, an eighth. The sample thins out but never fully disappears in a finite number of half-lives.

One half-life halves the sample — it does not empty it.
"Count rate and activity are the same thing."

Activity counts every decay in the source each second. Count rate is only the handful of those that strike the detector window. Count rate is always far below activity.

Count rate is a geometric fraction of the activity.
"Heating or compressing a source speeds up its decay."

Radioactive decay is spontaneous — it depends only on the nucleus. Temperature, pressure, and chemical bonding leave the half-life completely unchanged.

Nothing outside the nucleus alters the decay rate.
"An old nucleus is more likely to decay soon."

Decay has no memory. A nucleus that has survived a million years has exactly the same chance of decaying in the next second as a brand-new one.

The decay probability per second is constant.
"You can ignore background radiation."

Background from cosmic rays, rocks, and the air is always present. For weak sources it is a large part of the reading and must be measured and subtracted.

Always subtract background to get the corrected rate.
"1 becquerel is a strong, dangerous amount."

1 Bq is just one decay per second — tiny. Real sources are measured in kBq, MBq, or GBq. The becquerel is a very small unit.

1 Bq = 1 decay per second; real sources are far larger.
