Wave–Particle Duality

Part 2 of 2. If light can behave like particles, can particles behave like waves? Explore de Broglie's matter waves, watch electrons diffract, and collide photons with electrons in Compton scattering.

Every moving particle has a wavelength

de Broglie (1924): a particle of momentum p has a wavelength λ = h/p. Pick a particle, set its speed, and watch the wave.

What the numbers tell you

Wavelength is inversely proportional to momentum, so only tiny momenta give a wave big enough to notice.

λ=hp=hmv

An electron at a few million m/s has a wavelength around 10⁻¹⁰ m — the same size as the spacing between atoms in a crystal. That is exactly why electrons can be diffracted (next tab).

A tennis ball has a wavelength around 10⁻³⁴ m — a hundred trillion trillion times smaller than a nucleus. No experiment could ever resolve it, so everyday objects show no wave behaviour.

The rule of thumb: matter waves matter only when the momentum is extremely small.

Electrons diffract like waves

Accelerate electrons through a graphite film and rings appear on the screen — interference of electron waves. This confirmed de Broglie's idea (Davisson–Germer, 1927).

Faster electrons → smaller rings

A bigger voltage gives more momentum, a shorter wavelength, and therefore a smaller diffraction angle.

eV = ½mv²⇒p = √(2meV)⇒λ = h/p

The rings satisfy a diffraction condition like nλ = 2d sinθ, where d is the spacing between layers of carbon atoms. Measuring the ring size gives λ, and λ agrees with h/p — matter really does have a wavelength.

Increase the voltage and watch: the rings shrink, because a shorter wavelength diffracts through a smaller angle.

Compton scattering

Fire an X-ray photon at a stationary electron. The photon bounces off with less energy — a longer wavelength — exactly like a collision between two particles. Drag the angle.

The wavelength shift

The shift depends only on the angle, not on the incident wavelength or energy — a striking prediction that only the particle model explains.

Δλ = λf − λi = hmec(1 − cos θ)

The constant h/mec = 2.43 pm is the Compton wavelength. The largest possible shift is twice this, at θ = 180° (a direct backward bounce).

Because the photon carries momentum p = h/λ, energy and momentum are conserved in the collision — the electron recoils with the momentum the photon loses. This is stronger evidence for the particle nature of light than the photoelectric effect, which only needs energy conservation.

Two natures, one reality

Light and matter each show wave behaviour in some experiments and particle behaviour in others. Neither picture alone is complete.

Evidence for waves

  • Light: interference and diffraction (Young's slits, gratings).
  • Matter: electron diffraction rings (Davisson–Germer).
  • Superposition — only waves add and cancel.

Evidence for particles

  • Light: the photoelectric effect (threshold frequency).
  • Light: Compton scattering (photon–electron collisions).
  • Energy and momentum delivered in discrete quanta.

Wave–particle duality: every particle has wave properties (λ = h/p) and every electromagnetic wave has particle properties (E = hf, p = h/λ). Which one you observe depends on the experiment you do.

Nature of science

The photoelectric effect and Compton scattering forced physicists to accept that a purely wave theory of light was incomplete — a rare case where a well-established theory was shown to be insufficient by experiment.

We are faced with a new kind of difficulty. We have two contradictory pictures of reality; separately neither of them fully explains the phenomena of light, but together they do.— Albert Einstein & Leopold Infeld

Why "duality" and not just "waves" or "particles"?

The photon and the electron are neither a classical wave nor a classical particle. They are quantum objects whose behaviour our everyday language can only capture in two incomplete pictures. The mathematics of quantum mechanics describes them completely; the words "wave" and "particle" are just the shadows it casts on our intuition.

A testable hypothesis becomes accepted physics

de Broglie proposed matter waves as a hypothesis in 1924 with no direct evidence. Within three years, electron diffraction confirmed it. That is the scientific method in miniature: a bold prediction, then an experiment that could have proved it wrong — but didn't.

Worked examples

Fresh problems — attempt each first, then reveal. h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J s, mₑ = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg, e = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C, h/mₑc = 2.43×10⁻¹² m.

Worked example 1

An electron travels at 3.0×10⁶ m s⁻¹. Calculate (a) its momentum and (b) its de Broglie wavelength.

(a)   p = mv = 9.11×10⁻³¹ × 3.0×10⁶ = 2.73×10⁻²⁴ kg m s⁻¹
(b)   λ=hp=6.63×10⁻³⁴2.73×10⁻²⁴=2.43×10⁻¹⁰ m

Comparable to atomic spacing (~10⁻¹⁰ m), so this electron will diffract through a crystal.

Worked example 2

Through what potential difference must an electron, starting from rest, be accelerated so that its de Broglie wavelength is 1.0×10⁻¹⁰ m?

p = h/λ = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ / 1.0×10⁻¹⁰ = 6.63×10⁻²⁴ kg m s⁻¹
Ek=p²2m=(6.63×10⁻²⁴)²2 × 9.11×10⁻³¹=2.41×10⁻¹⁷ J
V = Ek/e = 2.41×10⁻¹⁷ / 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ = 151 V
Worked example 3

An X-ray photon of wavelength 72 pm is scattered through 90° by an electron. Find (a) the change in wavelength and (b) the scattered wavelength, and (c) the percentage change.

(a)   Δλ = (h/mₑc)(1 − cos θ) = 2.43×10⁻¹² × (1 − cos 90°) = 2.43×10⁻¹² × 1 = 2.43 pm
(b)   λf = 72 + 2.43 = 74.4 pm
(c)   2.43 / 72 × 100% = 3.4%
Worked example 4

A proton and an electron travel at the same speed. Which has the longer de Broglie wavelength, and by what factor?

λ = h/(mv). At the same speed, wavelength is inversely proportional to mass.

λe/λp = mp/me = 1.673×10⁻²⁷ / 9.11×10⁻³¹ ≈ 1836

The electron has the longer wavelength — about 1836 times longer than the proton's.

Common misconceptions, cleared up

The traps in wave–particle duality. Each card pairs the wrong idea with the fix.

✕

"A quantum object is a wave one moment and a particle the next."

✓

It carries both aspects at once. The experiment you choose decides which one you observe — it does not flip back and forth in time.

✕

"de Broglie waves are just electromagnetic waves."

✓

No. A matter wave is not made of oscillating E and B fields. It describes where the particle is likely to be found — a probability wave, not light.

✕

"Faster or heavier particles have longer wavelengths."

✓

The opposite: λ = h/p, so more momentum gives a shorter wavelength. Big, fast objects have vanishingly small wavelengths.

✕

"A thrown ball has a wavelength we could measure."

✓

Its wavelength is around 10⁻³⁴ m — unimaginably smaller than an atom. Wave behaviour only shows up for objects with tiny momenta, like electrons.

✕

"The Compton shift depends on the incident wavelength."

✓

It depends only on the scattering angle θ: Δλ = (h/mₑc)(1 − cos θ). The same angle always gives the same shift, whatever the incoming X-ray.

✕

"Compton scattering and the photoelectric effect prove the same thing."

✓

Both support the particle nature of light, but Compton also conserves momentum (photon p = h/λ) in a collision — stronger evidence than the photoelectric effect, which needs only energy conservation.

✕

"Raising the accelerating voltage makes the diffraction rings larger."

✓

Higher voltage → faster electrons → more momentum → shorter wavelength → smaller diffraction angle. The rings get smaller.

Check your understanding

Original questions — attempt each before revealing. h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J s, h/mₑc = 2.43 pm.

Q1 · Calculation

A neutron (mass 1.675×10⁻²⁷ kg) moves at 2.5×10³ m s⁻¹. Calculate its de Broglie wavelength.

p = mv = 1.675×10⁻²⁷ × 2.5×10³ = 4.19×10⁻²⁴. Then λ = h/p = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ / 4.19×10⁻²⁴ = 1.58×10⁻¹⁰ m.
Q2 · Explanation

Explain why a moving car shows no measurable wave properties.

Its momentum is enormous, so λ = h/p is around 10⁻³⁸ m — far smaller than any object or slit it could interact with. There is nothing in nature fine enough to diffract such a wavelength, so no wave behaviour is ever seen.
Q3 · Reasoning

An electron and a proton have the same kinetic energy. Which has the longer de Broglie wavelength?

Since p = √(2mEk), the heavier particle has the larger momentum and therefore the shorter wavelength. The electron (smaller mass) has the longer de Broglie wavelength.
Q4 · Compton

State the maximum possible Compton wavelength shift and the angle at which it occurs.

Δλ is greatest when (1 − cos θ) is greatest, i.e. cos θ = −1 at θ = 180°. Then Δλ_max = 2h/mₑc = 2 × 2.43 = 4.85 pm (a direct back-scatter).
Q5 · Diffraction

In an electron-diffraction tube the accelerating voltage is quadrupled. By what factor does the electron wavelength change?

Since λ = h/√(2meV), we have λ ∝ V−1/2. Quadrupling V multiplies λ by 1/√4 = ½ — the wavelength halves, so the rings shrink.
Q6 · Duality

Give one piece of evidence for the wave nature and one for the particle nature of light, then state the principle of wave–particle duality.

Wave: interference/diffraction of light. Particle: the photoelectric effect or Compton scattering. Duality: all particles have wave properties (λ = h/p) and all electromagnetic waves have particle properties (E = hf, p = h/λ); which is observed depends on the experiment.
