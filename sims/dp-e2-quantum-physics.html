01Photons: energy in packets

Interference and diffraction show that light behaves as a wave. But when light gives energy to matter, it does so in separate packets called photons. Each photon carries an amount of energy that depends only on its frequency.

Photon energyE = h f = frac{h c}{λ}   where h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s is the Planck constant. A photon is a quantum (smallest possible amount) of electromagnetic energy. Brighter light of the same colour means more photons per second, not bigger photons.
The electronvolt1 eV is the energy gained by one electron accelerated through 1 V: 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. To change J into eV, divide by 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹. A handy shortcut: E (in eV) ≈ frac{1240}{λ (in nm)}.
Example 1.1Calculate the energy of a photon of green light of wavelength 500 nm, in J and in eV.
E = frac{h c}{λ} = frac{6.63 × 10^{-34} × 3.00 × 10^{8}}{500 × 10^{-9}} = 3.98 × 10^{-19} J => E = frac{3.98 × 10^{-19}}{1.60 × 10^{-19}} = 2.49 eV
Example 1.2A laser pointer emits 5.0 mW of red light of wavelength 650 nm. Calculate the number of photons it emits per second.
E = frac{6.63 × 10^{-34} × 3.00 × 10^{8}}{650 × 10^{-9}} = 3.06 × 10^{-19} J N = frac{P}{E} = frac{5.0 × 10^{-3}}{3.06 × 10^{-19}} => N = 1.6 × 10^{16} photons per second

02The photoelectric effect: what is observed

When electromagnetic radiation of high enough frequency falls on a clean metal surface, electrons are emitted from the surface. These are called photoelectrons. A negatively charged zinc plate on an electroscope loses its charge in ultraviolet light, but not in visible light, however bright.

In a photocell the emitted electrons cross a vacuum to a collector, so a current flows in the circuit and is measured by a microammeter. This is how light can create an electric current (guiding question 1). Experiments show four key results:

#Observation
1There is a threshold frequency f₀ for each metal. Below it no electrons are emitted, however intense the light.
2Above f₀, electrons are emitted instantly (within about 10⁻⁹ s), even in very dim light.
3The maximum kinetic energy of the electrons depends on the frequency and increases linearly with it. It does not depend on the intensity.
4The number of electrons emitted per second (the photocurrent) is proportional to the intensity.
Why “maximum” kinetic energy?Every photon gives the same energy hf, but electrons deeper in the metal lose some energy in collisions on the way out. Only the least tightly bound electrons at the surface escape with the most energy, so the photoelectrons have a range of kinetic energies up to Emax.

03Why the wave model fails

GuidanceA discussion of which features of the photoelectric effect cannot be explained using the classical wave theory of light is required.

In the wave model, light energy arrives continuously and is spread evenly over the wavefront. The energy delivered depends on the intensity (amplitude²), not on the frequency. That leads to predictions that are all wrong:

FeatureWave model predictsObserved
Threshold frequencyAny frequency should release electrons if the light is intense enough, or if we wait long enough.No emission below f₀, however intense the light.
Time delayIn dim light an electron needs time (minutes or more) to absorb enough energy to escape.Emission is instantaneous.
Effect of intensity on EmaxBrighter light means bigger waves, so faster electrons.Emax does not depend on intensity.
Effect of frequency on EmaxFrequency should make no difference.Emax increases linearly with f.
Example 3.1Light of intensity 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ W m⁻² falls on a metal of work function 2.3 eV. In the wave model, one electron collects energy from an area of about 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ m² (one atom). Estimate the time it would need to escape.
power received = I A = 1.0 × 10^{-6} × 1.0 × 10^{-19} = 1.0 × 10^{-25} W energy needed = 2.3 × 1.60 × 10^{-19} = 3.7 × 10^{-19} J t = frac{3.7 × 10^{-19}}{1.0 × 10^{-25}} => t = 3.7 × 10^{6} s (about 6 weeks) # observed: emission within about 10^{-9} s
Linking question (NOS)How did the explanation of the photoelectric effect lead to the falsification that light was purely a wave?
FalsificationA scientific theory can never be proved true, but a single reliable experiment that contradicts its predictions can show it is false. The wave theory made clear, testable predictions about the photoelectric effect (a time delay, a dependence on intensity, no threshold). Careful experiments by Lenard (1902) and Millikan (1916) showed all of them to be wrong, while Einstein’s photon model (1905) predicted every result, including the straight-line graph of Emax against f with gradient h. The idea that light is only a wave was falsified. The wave model was not thrown away: it still explains interference and diffraction, which led to the idea of wave–particle duality.

04Einstein’s explanation: Emax = hf − Φ

Einstein (1905) proposed that light arrives as photons and that one photon gives all of its energy to one electron. Some of this energy is used to escape from the metal; what is left is the electron’s kinetic energy.

Einstein’s photoelectric equationE_{max} = h f - Φ
Work function Φ: the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the surface of the metal.
Threshold frequency f₀: the minimum frequency of radiation that releases electrons, given by h f_{0} = Φ, so f_{0} = frac{Φ}{h} and the threshold (longest) wavelength is λ_{0} = frac{h c}{Φ}.
MetalΦ / eVf₀ / 10¹⁴ Hzλ₀ / nmWorks with visible light?

The photon model explains every observation: below f₀ a single photon does not have enough energy (and electrons cannot save up energy from several photons); emission is instant because one photon is absorbed in one event; Emax depends only on hf; more intensity means more photons, so more electrons per second.

Example 4.1Sodium has a work function of 2.28 eV. Calculate its threshold frequency and threshold wavelength.
Φ = 2.28 × 1.60 × 10^{-19} = 3.65 × 10^{-19} J f_{0} = frac{Φ}{h} = frac{3.65 × 10^{-19}}{6.63 × 10^{-34}} => f_{0} = 5.50 × 10^{14} Hz λ_{0} = frac{c}{f_{0}} = frac{3.00 × 10^{8}}{5.50 × 10^{14}} => λ_{0} = 5.45 × 10^{-7} m = 545 nm
Example 4.2Violet light of wavelength 420 nm falls on sodium. Calculate the maximum kinetic energy and the maximum speed of the photoelectrons.
h f = frac{6.63 × 10^{-34} × 3.00 × 10^{8}}{420 × 10^{-9}} = 4.74 × 10^{-19} J = 2.96 eV E_{max} = h f - Φ = 2.96 - 2.28 = 0.68 eV = 0.68 × 1.60 × 10^{-19} = 1.09 × 10^{-19} J v_{max} = sqrt{frac{2 E_{max}}{m_{e}}} = sqrt{frac{2 × 1.09 × 10^{-19}}{9.11 × 10^{-31}}} => v_{max} = 4.9 × 10^{5} m s^{-1}
Common mistakes
  • Mixing units: hf in J with Φ in eV. Convert both to the same unit first.
  • Writing E = hf − Φ for the kinetic energy of every electron: it is the maximum.
  • Thinking a more intense beam gives faster electrons.
  • Forgetting that the threshold wavelength is a maximum: shorter wavelengths work, longer ones do not.
Linking question (NOS)Can the Bohr model help explain the photoelectric effect?
Bohr model and the photoelectric effectPartly. Both use the same idea: energy is absorbed in whole photons, and an electron needs a definite minimum energy to be freed (like the ionisation energy in the Bohr model of hydrogen, 13.6 eV from n = 1). But the Bohr model describes a single isolated atom with sharp energy levels, so it predicts absorption only at particular frequencies. In a metal the outer electrons are shared by all the atoms and have a band of energies; the work function is the energy needed to free the least tightly bound of them. That is why any frequency above f₀ works, not just certain lines, and why the electrons have a range of energies. Models have limits: the Bohr model works well for hydrogen atoms but not for solids.

05Stopping potential and the photocell

To measure Emax, the collector is made negative relative to the emitter. This reverse p.d. repels the photoelectrons. As it is increased, slower electrons are turned back and the current falls. The stopping potential Vs is the smallest reverse p.d. that just stops even the fastest electrons, so that the current falls to zero.

Stopping potentiale V_{s} = E_{max} = frac{1}{2} m_{e} v_{max}^{2}   so e V_{s} = h f - Φ. In eV the numbers are the same: if Vs = 0.68 V then Emax = 0.68 eV.
  • With a large positive collector p.d. every emitted electron is collected: the current reaches a saturation value, set by the number of photons per second (the intensity).
  • At 0 V there is still a current, because the electrons leave with kinetic energy.
  • A and B: same frequency gives the same stopping potential; doubling the intensity doubles the saturation current.
  • C: a higher frequency gives a larger stopping potential. Its saturation current can be higher or lower, depending on how many photons per second arrive.
Example 5.1The stopping potential for a metal of work function 2.25 eV is 1.20 V. Determine the wavelength of the light.
h f = Φ + e V_{s} = 2.25 + 1.20 = 3.45 eV = 3.45 × 1.60 × 10^{-19} = 5.52 × 10^{-19} J λ = frac{h c}{E} = frac{6.63 × 10^{-34} × 3.00 × 10^{8}}{5.52 × 10^{-19}} => λ = 3.6 × 10^{-7} m (360 nm, ultraviolet)

06Graphs: finding h, f₀ and Φ

Rearranging Einstein’s equation gives a straight line. Plotting Emax (or eVs) against f for one metal:

E_{max} = h f - Φ # compare y = m x + c gradient = h f-intercept = f_{0} E-intercept = - Φ
Using a Vs–f graphIf you plot the stopping potential Vs against f, then V_{s} = frac{h}{e} f - frac{Φ}{e}. The gradient is h/e, so h = e × gradient. The intercept on the Vs axis is −Φ/e, which is −Φ in volts. Millikan used this method in 1916 to measure h, and found it agreed with Planck’s value from black-body radiation.
Example 6.1For one metal the stopping potential is 0.89 V at 6.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz and 2.13 V at 9.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz. Determine the Planck constant and the work function.
gradient = frac{2.13 - 0.89}{(9.0 - 6.0) × 10^{14}} = 4.13 × 10^{-15} V s h = e × gradient = 1.60 × 10^{-19} × 4.13 × 10^{-15} => h = 6.6 × 10^{-34} J s Φ = h f - e V_{s} = 6.61 × 10^{-34} × 6.0 × 10^{14} - 1.60 × 10^{-19} × 0.89 = 2.54 × 10^{-19} J => Φ = 1.6 eV
Exam technique for graphsDraw a single best-fit straight line, use a large triangle for the gradient, and quote the units (V s or J s). Extrapolate the line to find f₀: the threshold frequency is where the line crosses the f-axis, not the first data point.

07Matter waves: the de Broglie wavelength

In 1924 Louis de Broglie suggested that if light waves can behave as particles, then particles of matter can behave as waves. Every moving particle has a wavelength that depends on its momentum.

de Broglie wavelengthλ = frac{h}{p} = frac{h}{m v}   where p is the momentum of the particle. The larger the momentum, the shorter the wavelength.

For an electron accelerated from rest through a p.d. V, its kinetic energy is eV, so

E_{k} = e V = frac{p^{2}}{2 m} p = sqrt{2 m e V} => λ = frac{h}{sqrt{2 m e V}}
Example 7.1An electron is accelerated from rest through 150 V. Calculate its de Broglie wavelength.
E_{k} = e V = 1.60 × 10^{-19} × 150 = 2.40 × 10^{-17} J p = sqrt{2 m E_{k}} = sqrt{2 × 9.11 × 10^{-31} × 2.40 × 10^{-17}} = 6.61 × 10^{-24} kg m s^{-1} λ = frac{h}{p} = frac{6.63 × 10^{-34}}{6.61 × 10^{-24}} => λ = 1.0 × 10^{-10} m # about the size of an atom, so a crystal can diffract these electrons

neutron from a reactor1.67 × 10⁻²⁷2.0 × 10³2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰yes: neutron diffraction

Watch outThe wavelength depends on momentum, not just speed: an electron and a proton with the same speed have very different wavelengths; with the same kinetic energy, the proton’s is shorter by √(mp/me) ≈ 43. Keep v well below c: the simple formulas are non-relativistic.

08Electron diffraction: evidence for matter waves

UnderstandingDiffraction of particles as evidence of the wave nature of matter.

Diffraction is a wave behaviour: waves spread out after passing through a gap or around an obstacle, and the spreading is greatest when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength. In 1927 Davisson and Germer (scattering electrons off a nickel crystal) and G. P. Thomson (firing electrons through thin metal foil) both found diffraction patterns. The spacings matched de Broglie’s λ = h/p exactly.

  • The electrons produce bright concentric rings, like the pattern made by X-rays passing through a powder of tiny crystals. The atoms in the crystal act like a diffraction grating with a spacing of about 10⁻¹⁰ m.
  • Increasing the accelerating p.d. V increases the momentum, so λ decreases and the rings get smaller (r ∝ λ ∝ 1/√V).
  • A magnet held near the tube moves the pattern, showing it is made by charged particles, not by X-rays.
GuidanceA description of a scattering experiment including the location of minimum intensity for the diffracted particles based on their de Broglie wavelength is required.

When a beam of particles passes through a gap (or scatters off an object) of width b, the first minimum of intensity is at the angle θ where

First minimum of a diffraction patternsin θ ≈ θ = frac{λ}{b}   with λ = h/p, the de Broglie wavelength of the particles (θ in radians; for a circular object of diameter D the first minimum is at sin θ ≈ λ/D, more exactly 1.22λ/D).
Example 8.1Neutrons of speed 2.0 × 10³ m s⁻¹ pass through a single slit 4.0 μm wide and are detected 5.0 m beyond it. Determine the distance from the centre of the pattern to the first minimum.
λ = frac{h}{m v} = frac{6.63 × 10^{-34}}{1.67 × 10^{-27} × 2.0 × 10^{3}} = 1.99 × 10^{-10} m θ = frac{λ}{b} = frac{1.99 × 10^{-10}}{4.0 × 10^{-6}} = 4.96 × 10^{-5} rad y = D θ = 5.0 × 4.96 × 10^{-5} => y = 2.5 × 10^{-4} m (0.25 mm)
Example 8.2High-energy electrons with momentum 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ kg m s⁻¹ are scattered by carbon nuclei. The first minimum of the scattered intensity is at 42°. Estimate the diameter of a carbon nucleus.
λ = frac{h}{p} = frac{6.63 × 10^{-34}}{2.2 × 10^{-19}} = 3.0 × 10^{-15} m sin θ ≈ frac{λ}{D} D ≈ frac{λ}{sin θ} = frac{3.0 × 10^{-15}}{sin 42°} => D ≈ 4.5 × 10^{-15} m
Linking questionHow can particles diffract?
How particles diffractA particle is not a tiny ball with a definite path. Its motion is described by a wave (spread out in space) whose wavelength is h/p. The wave passes through every part of the gap or crystal and the parts interfere, so the probability of finding the particle is high in some directions and zero in others (the minima). Each particle still arrives at the screen at a single point, but many arrivals build up the diffraction pattern.
Linking questionWhat evidence indicates the diffraction of a wave?
Evidence of diffractionSpreading into the geometric shadow, and a pattern of maxima and minima whose angles depend on λ/b (or λ/d for a grating or crystal). Bright spots alone are not enough: particles going straight through also make a spot. The key evidence is the dark minima where waves cancel, and a pattern that changes with wavelength in the way the wave equations predict. Electron rings shrink as V increases exactly as λ = h/√(2meV) predicts.

09Wave–particle duality

Guiding questionWhat is meant by wave–particle duality?

Both light and matter show wave properties in some experiments and particle properties in others. Neither model alone describes all of the behaviour; each is needed. This is wave–particle duality.

Shows wave behaviourShows particle behaviour
Light (EM radiation)interference, diffraction, polarisationphotoelectric effect, Compton scattering, line spectra (photons of definite energy)
Electrons and other matterelectron diffraction, neutron diffraction, double-slit interferencedefinite mass and charge, tracks in a cloud chamber, deflection in fields, collisions
One at a timeWhen electrons are sent through a double slit one at a time, each one makes a single dot on the screen (particle behaviour). But after thousands of electrons, the dots form an interference pattern (wave behaviour). The wave tells us the probability of where each particle is found. Try it in the Matter waves tab.
Linking questionWhat are the defining features and behaviours of waves?
Defining features of wavesA wave is a travelling oscillation that transfers energy without transferring matter. It has a wavelength, frequency and speed (v = fλ). Waves superpose: they interfere constructively and destructively, and they diffract through gaps comparable to λ. Particles carry their energy in one place and move along a path. Electrons that interfere and diffract therefore show truly wave-like behaviour, and photons that knock out single electrons and recoil off electrons show particle-like behaviour.

10Compton scattering

In 1923 Arthur Compton fired X-rays at a graphite block and measured the wavelength of the X-rays scattered at different angles. The scattered X-rays had a longer wavelength than the incident ones, and the increase depended on the scattering angle θ.

The Compton shiftΔλ = λ_{f} - λ_{i} = frac{h}{m_{e} c} (1 - cos θ)
θ is the angle through which the photon is scattered. The constant frac{h}{m_{e} c} = 2.43 × 10^{-12} m is called the Compton wavelength of the electron. (The derivation is not required.)
  • θ = 0: no shift (the photon carries straight on).
  • θ = 90°: Δλ = h/mec = 2.43 pm.
  • θ = 180° (photon bounces straight back): the largest shift, 2h/mec = 4.86 pm.
  • The shift does not depend on the incident wavelength, on the intensity or on the target material.
Why it is evidence for photonsIn the wave model, the incoming wave makes electrons oscillate at the same frequency, and they re-radiate at that frequency: no change of wavelength. Compton explained the shift by treating the X-ray as a particle (a photon of energy hf and momentum h/λ) that makes an elastic collision with a free electron. The photon gives some of its energy and momentum to the electron, so the scattered photon has less energy, a lower frequency and a longer wavelength. Conservation of energy and momentum predicts exactly the formula above.
Example 10.1X-rays of wavelength 7.10 × 10⁻¹¹ m are scattered through 60° by electrons. Calculate the wavelength of the scattered X-rays.
Δλ = frac{h}{m_{e} c} (1 - cos θ) = 2.43 × 10^{-12} × (1 - cos 60°) = 1.21 × 10^{-12} m λ_{f} = 7.10 × 10^{-11} + 0.121 × 10^{-11} => λ_{f} = 7.22 × 10^{-11} m
Example 10.2Show that h/(mec) = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m.
frac{h}{m_{e} c} = frac{6.63 × 10^{-34}}{9.11 × 10^{-31} × 3.00 × 10^{8}} => frac{h}{m_{e} c} = 2.43 × 10^{-12} m

11Photon momentum and the recoiling electron

A photon has no mass but it does carry momentum. Using de Broglie’s relation the other way round:

Momentum of a photonp = frac{h}{λ} = frac{E}{c}

In Compton scattering, energy is conserved: the kinetic energy given to the electron equals the energy lost by the photon. Momentum is conserved as a vector: the incident photon’s momentum equals the vector sum of the scattered photon’s and the electron’s momenta.

E_{k} = frac{h c}{λ_{i}} - frac{h c}{λ_{f}} # vector sum: p_{i} = p_{f} + p_{e}
Example 11.1In Example 10.1 the wavelength increased from 7.10 × 10⁻¹¹ m to 7.221 × 10⁻¹¹ m (keeping an extra figure). Calculate the kinetic energy of the recoiling electron in eV, and the momentum of the incident photon.
E_{i} = frac{6.63 × 10^{-34} × 3.00 × 10^{8}}{7.10 × 10^{-11}} = 2.801 × 10^{-15} J E_{f} = frac{6.63 × 10^{-34} × 3.00 × 10^{8}}{7.221 × 10^{-11}} = 2.754 × 10^{-15} J E_{k} = 2.801 × 10^{-15} - 2.754 × 10^{-15} = 4.7 × 10^{-17} J => E_{k} ≈ 290 eV p = frac{h}{λ} = frac{6.63 × 10^{-34}}{7.10 × 10^{-11}} => p = 9.3 × 10^{-24} kg m s^{-1}
Significant figures matter hereThe energy lost is a small difference of two large numbers. Keep 4 significant figures in Ei and Ef (and in λf) or the answer will be wildly wrong.

Linking questionHow is photon scattering off an electron similar to and how is it different from the collision of two solid balls?
Photon–electron vs ball–ballSimilar: both are elastic collisions that conserve total energy and total momentum (as vectors); the target gains kinetic energy and recoils at an angle; the more sharply the incoming object is deflected, the more energy it loses. Different: the photon has no mass and always travels at c, so it cannot slow down: it loses energy by dropping its frequency (λ increases), with E = hf and p = h/λ. A ball loses energy by slowing down (Ek = ½mv², p = mv). A photon can never be stopped by the collision, and the fraction of energy lost depends on its wavelength: X-rays lose a few per cent, visible light almost nothing. Try both side by side in the Compton tab.
Linking question (NOS)Why is Compton scattering more convincing evidence for the particle nature of light than that from the photoelectric effect?
Why Compton is more convincingThe photoelectric effect shows that energy is exchanged in packets of hf, but that might be a property of how electrons in the metal absorb energy, rather than of light itself; semi-classical models could explain parts of it. In Compton scattering the photon behaves like a particle in flight: it collides with a single free electron, both energy and momentum are conserved, and the photon survives with a precisely predicted, angle-dependent wavelength. The recoiling electron was also detected at the angle and energy predicted. A wave model gives no shift at all, so the result is a direct, quantitative test that only the photon model passes.

Common exam mistakes
  • Mixing J and eV in Emax = hf − Φ.
  • Saying intensity affects the energy of the electrons: it affects only how many there are.
  • Answering “the wave model fails” without saying what it predicts and what is observed.
  • Using the gradient of a Vs–f graph as h: it is h/e.
  • Using λ = h/(mv) with the speed of light for a photon: use p = h/λ = E/c.
  • Forgetting the square root: λ ∝ 1/√V for electrons accelerated through V.
  • Writing the Compton shift with the incident wavelength in it: Δλ depends only on θ.
  • Rounding too early when finding the recoil energy in Compton scattering.

E.2 Quantum physics (HL) · one-page summary

Photons

  • E = hf = hc/λ
  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; E(eV) ≈ 1240/λ(nm)
  • Intensity ∝ photons per second
  • p = h/λ = E/c

Photoelectric effect

  • Threshold f₀: no emission below it
  • Instant emission
  • Emax depends on f, not intensity
  • Current ∝ intensity
  • Wave model fails on all four

Einstein

  • One photon → one electron
  • Emax = hf − Φ
  • Φ = hf₀; λ₀ = hc/Φ
  • eVs = Emax = ½mv²max

Graphs

  • Emax–f: gradient h, f-intercept f₀, y-intercept −Φ
  • Vs–f: gradient h/e
  • I–V: same f → same Vs; intensity → saturation current

Matter waves

  • λ = h/p = h/mv
  • Electron through V: λ = h/√(2meV)
  • Electron diffraction: rings shrink as V rises
  • First minimum: sin θ ≈ λ/b
  • Duality: dots build up a pattern

Compton

  • Δλ = (h/mec)(1 − cos θ)
  • h/mec = 2.43 pm; max shift at 180°
  • Photon loses energy, λ increases
  • Conserve energy and vector momentum
  • Shows photons carry momentum

Top 8 mistakes to avoid

  1. Convert to the same energy unit before using hf − Φ.
  2. Intensity changes the number, not the energy, of electrons.
  3. Emax is the maximum, not every electron’s energy.
  4. Vs–f gradient is h/e.
  5. Threshold wavelength is a maximum.
  6. λ ∝ 1/√V for accelerated electrons.
  7. Δλ does not depend on the incident λ.
  8. Keep 4 s.f. when subtracting photon energies.
