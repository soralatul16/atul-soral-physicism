01Stellar equilibrium

A star is a huge ball of hot gas (plasma), mostly hydrogen and helium. Two effects act on every layer of it:

Hydrostatic equilibrium
  • Gravity pulls every layer inwards, towards the centre.
  • Pressure pushes outwards: radiation pressure from the photons produced by fusion, together with the thermal (gas) pressure of the hot plasma.
A star is stable when these balance at every depth. The pressure must increase towards the centre to support the weight of all the layers above.
The stellar thermostatIf the core is squeezed slightly, it gets hotter and denser, so fusion speeds up (very sensitively), the pressure rises and pushes the star back out. If it expands, it cools, fusion slows, the pressure drops and gravity pulls it back. This negative feedback keeps a main-sequence star stable for billions of years.
Linking question (NOS)How can gas laws be used to model stars?

The plasma behaves almost like an ideal gas, so P = n k_{B} T (n = number of particles per m³). Compressing the core raises its density and temperature, so its pressure rises. Astrophysicists model a star as layers of ideal gas, each in hydrostatic equilibrium, plus energy transport outwards. This simple model correctly predicts that the Sun’s core is at about 1.5 × 10⁷ K. It is a model: real stars also have radiation pressure, convection and, in white dwarfs and neutron stars, quantum (degeneracy) pressure that does not depend on temperature.

When the balance failsWhen the core runs out of a fuel, fusion and pressure fall, and gravity wins: the core contracts and heats up until a new fuel can fuse (or, if it cannot, until something else stops it). These imbalances drive stellar evolution (section 10).

02Fusion: the energy source of stars

Nuclear fusionLight nuclei join to form a heavier nucleus. The product is more tightly bound (higher binding energy per nucleon), so the total mass falls and energy is released: E = Δmc². Fusion of nuclei lighter than iron releases energy.

In the Sun and other low-mass stars, hydrogen becomes helium by the proton–proton (pp) chain:

^{1}_{1}H + ^{1}_{1}H → ^{2}_{1}H + ^{0}_{1}e + ν (twice) ^{2}_{1}H + ^{1}_{1}H → ^{3}_{2}He + γ (twice) ^{3}_{2}He + ^{3}_{2}He → ^{4}_{2}He + 2 ^{1}_{1}H # overall: 4 protons → one helium-4 + 2 positrons + 2 neutrinos + γ-rays

Heavier stars (core above about 1.7 × 10⁷ K) convert hydrogen mostly by the CNO cycle, in which carbon, nitrogen and oxygen act as catalysts. The overall result is the same: 4 H → He-4.

Example 2.1Atomic masses: ¹H 1.007825 u, ⁴He 4.002602 u. Calculate the energy released when four hydrogen atoms become one helium atom, and the fraction of the mass converted.
Δm = 4 × 1.007825 - 4.002602 = 0.028698 u E = 0.028698 × 931.5 => E = 26.7 MeV fraction = frac{0.028698}{4 × 1.007825} => fraction = 0.71%

Using atomic masses here takes care of the electrons and the annihilation of the two positrons automatically.

Example 2.2The Sun’s luminosity is 3.83 × 10²⁶ W. Estimate the mass of hydrogen converted to helium each second.
E per reaction = 26.7 × 1.602 × 10^{-13} = 4.28 × 10^{-12} J reactions per second = frac{3.83 × 10^{26}}{4.28 × 10^{-12}} = 8.95 × 10^{37} mass of H = 4 × 1.67 × 10^{-27} × 8.95 × 10^{37} => mass of H = 6.0 × 10^{11} kg s^{-1}

About 600 million tonnes of hydrogen a second, of which 4 million tonnes (0.7%) becomes energy.

03Conditions for fusion: temperature and density

Nuclei are positively charged and repel each other. To fuse, they must get within about 10⁻¹⁵ m, where the strong force acts. This needs:

The two conditions
  • Very high temperature (about 10⁷ K for hydrogen): the nuclei move fast enough to come close despite the electric repulsion (the Coulomb barrier).
  • Very high density (about 1.5 × 10⁵ kg m⁻³ in the Sun’s core): collisions are frequent enough for the rare successful ones to release a large power.
Both are produced by gravity: the weight of the star compresses and heats the core.
Example 3.1Two protons must approach to 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m to fuse. Estimate (a) the electric potential energy at this separation and (b) the temperature at which the average kinetic energy of a proton equals it.
E_{p} = frac{k e^{2}}{r} = frac{8.99 × 10^{9} × (1.60 × 10^{-19})^{2}}{2.0 × 10^{-15}} => (a) E_{p} = 1.2 × 10^{-13} J ≈ 0.72 MeV frac{3}{2} k_{B} T = E_{p} (average kinetic energy = barrier) => (b) T = frac{2 × 1.2 × 10^{-13}}{3 × 1.38 × 10^{-23}} ≈ 6 × 10^{9} K

The Sun’s core is only about 1.5 × 10⁷ K, 400 times cooler. Fusion still happens because (1) a few protons in the high-energy tail of the speed distribution move much faster than average, and (2) quantum tunnelling lets protons pass through the barrier without having enough energy to go over it. Even so, a given proton in the Sun’s core waits about 10¹⁰ years on average before fusing, which is why the Sun lasts so long.

Why heavier fuels need hotter coresHelium (Z = 2) and carbon (Z = 6) nuclei have larger charges, so their Coulomb barriers are higher. Helium fusion needs about 10⁸ K and carbon fusion about 6 × 10⁸ K. Only stars massive enough to squeeze their cores this hard can burn these fuels.

05Black-body radiation and luminosity

Linking questionHow can the understanding of black-body radiation help determine the properties of stars?

Stars radiate very nearly as black bodies: their spectrum depends only on their surface temperature. Two laws (both in the data booklet) let us extract the temperature and the power.

Wien’s displacement law
λ_{max} T = 2.9 × 10^{-3} m K
The hotter the star, the shorter the wavelength at which it emits most strongly. Hot stars look blue-white; cool stars look red.
Stefan–Boltzmann law: luminosity
L = σ A T^{4} = σ 4π R^{2} T^{4}
The luminosity L is the total power emitted (W). σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴. The apparent brightness b is the power received per unit area at distance d:
b = frac{L}{4π d^{2}}
Example 5.1The Sun’s spectrum peaks at about 500 nm. Calculate its surface temperature. Betelgeuse is at 3600 K: calculate its peak wavelength.
T_{Sun} = frac{2.9 × 10^{-3}}{5.0 × 10^{-7}} => T_{Sun} = 5800 K λ_{max} = frac{2.9 × 10^{-3}}{3600} => λ_{max} = 8.1 × 10^{-7} m = 810 nm (infrared: the star looks red)
Example 5.2The Sun’s luminosity is 3.83 × 10²⁶ W and the Earth is 1.50 × 10¹¹ m away. Calculate the apparent brightness of the Sun at the Earth.
b = frac{3.83 × 10^{26}}{4π × (1.50 × 10^{11})^{2}} => b = 1.35 × 10^{3} W m^{-2}

06Stellar spectra: temperature and composition

Linking questionHow do emission spectra provide information about observations of the cosmos?

Light from the hot interior passes through the cooler outer atmosphere of the star. Atoms there absorb photons of exactly the energies that match their electron transitions, so the continuous black-body spectrum is crossed by dark absorption lines. Each element has its own pattern of lines, the same pattern it shows as bright emission lines in a hot gas in the laboratory.

What the spectrum tells us
  • Surface temperature: from the peak of the continuous spectrum (Wien’s law), and from which lines are strong.
  • Composition: from which elements’ lines are present. Matching the lines to laboratory emission spectra identifies each element.
  • (Beyond E.5: shifts of the lines give the star’s motion, by the Doppler effect.)

Nature of scienceIn 1925 Cecilia Payne showed that the differences between spectral classes are caused mainly by temperature, not composition, and that stars are made mostly of hydrogen and helium. The idea was so surprising that she was persuaded to call her result “spurious”; it was soon confirmed.

07Distances: AU, light years, parsecs and parallax

UnitDefinitionIn metres
astronomical unit (AU)average Earth–Sun distance1.50 × 10¹¹ m

parsec (pc)distance at which 1 AU subtends an angle of 1 arc-second3.09 × 10¹⁶ m = 3.26 ly
Stellar parallaxAs the Earth orbits the Sun, a nearby star appears to move against the much more distant background stars. The parallax angle p is half the angle the star appears to move through in six months: the angle subtended at the star by 1 AU.
d (parsec) = frac{1}{p (arc-second)}
1 arc-second = 1/3600 of a degree.
Example 7.1Show that 1 pc ≈ 3.1 × 10¹⁶ m, and calculate the distance in pc, ly and m of a star with a parallax of 0.25 arc-seconds.
1″ = frac{1}{3600} × frac{π}{180} = 4.85 × 10^{-6} rad 1 pc = frac{1 AU}{tan 1″} ≈ frac{1.50 × 10^{11}}{4.85 × 10^{-6}} = 3.09 × 10^{16} m d = frac{1}{0.25} => d = 4.0 pc = 13 ly = 1.2 × 10^{17} m
Limits of parallaxThe angles are tiny: even the nearest star (Proxima Centauri) has p = 0.77″. From the ground, the atmosphere limits measurements to about 0.01″ (100 pc). Space telescopes do much better: Hipparcos (1989–93) about 0.001″, and Gaia (from 2013) about 0.00002″, giving distances to over a billion stars.

08Determining stellar radii

Rearranging the Stefan–Boltzmann law gives the radius from the luminosity and the surface temperature:

R = sqrt{frac{L}{4π σ T^{4}}}
How each quantity is foundT from the spectrum (Wien’s law); d from parallax; b measured with a detector; then L = 4πd²b; then R from L and T. Comparing with the Sun saves time: frac{R}{R_{☉}} = sqrt{frac{L}{L_{☉}}} × (frac{T_{☉}}{T})^{2}
Example 8.1Sirius B has L = 0.056 L☉ and T = 25 000 K. L☉ = 3.83 × 10²⁶ W. Determine its radius.
L = 0.056 × 3.83 × 10^{26} = 2.1 × 10^{25} W R = sqrt{frac{2.1 × 10^{25}}{4π × 5.67 × 10^{-8} × 25000^{4}}} => R ≈ 8.8 × 10^{6} m

About the size of the Earth: Sirius B is a white dwarf.

Example 8.2Betelgeuse has L = 1.0 × 10⁵ L☉ and T = 3600 K. T☉ = 5800 K. Determine its radius in solar radii.
frac{R}{R_{☉}} = sqrt{1.0 × 10^{5}} × (frac{5800}{3600})^{2} = 316 × 2.60 => R ≈ 820 R_{☉} ≈ 5.7 × 10^{11} m (about 4 AU: it would swallow Mars’ orbit)
Ratio questionsTwo stars with the same temperature: R ∝ √L. Two stars with the same luminosity: R ∝ 1/T². Doubling T at the same R multiplies L by 16.

09The Hertzsprung–Russell (HR) diagram

RegionProperties
Main sequenceAbout 90% of stars. Fusing hydrogen to helium in the core. Runs from hot, luminous, massive stars (top left) to cool, dim, low-mass red dwarfs (bottom right). Position is set by mass.
Red giantsCool (3000–5000 K) but luminous (10–1000 L☉), so very large (10–100 R☉). Fusing helium in the core and/or hydrogen in a shell. A late stage of Sun-like stars.
SupergiantsThe most luminous stars (10⁴–10⁶ L☉), at any temperature; radii up to about 1000 R☉. Late stages of very massive stars.
White dwarfsHot (often over 10 000 K) but dim (10⁻⁴–10⁻² L☉), so tiny (about Earth-sized, ≈ 0.01 R☉). No fusion: the exposed, cooling core of a dead Sun-like star.
Instability stripA narrow, nearly vertical band crossing the giants and supergiants (about 5500–7500 K) where stars pulsate, changing in brightness and size. (Cepheid variables are not required.)
Lines of constant radiusFrom L = σ4πR²T⁴, stars with the same R lie on a straight line on the log–log HR diagram, running from top left to bottom right. Moving up at the same temperature means a bigger star. Moving down and to the left across these lines means smaller.
Sketching an HR diagram (exam)Luminosity on the vertical axis (often in L☉, log scale); temperature on the horizontal axis, decreasing to the right (log scale). Label the main sequence as a band from top left to bottom right, red giants above it on the right, supergiants across the top, white dwarfs below it on the left, and the Sun at about 5800 K, 1 L☉.
Linking question (NOS)HR diagrams have been helpful in the classification of stars by finding patterns in their properties. Which other areas of physics use classification?

Plotting two measured properties against each other revealed groups that pointed to the physics behind them (the main sequence turned out to be hydrogen burning, sorted by mass). Physics uses classification in the same way elsewhere: the periodic table of elements, the Standard Model’s quarks and leptons, the electromagnetic spectrum, types of radioactive decay, and solids as conductors, semiconductors or insulators.

10Stellar evolution and the effect of mass

Guiding question 2What physical processes lead to the evolution of stars?

A star’s mass decides almost everything: its position on the main sequence, how long it lives, which elements it can fuse and how it ends.

Main-sequence lifetimeA star with more mass has more fuel, but its core is hotter and denser, so it burns it much faster: roughly L ∝ M3.5, so lifetime ∝ M/L ∝ M−2.5. The Sun lasts about 10¹⁰ years; a 10 M☉ star only about 3 × 10⁷ years.
Initial massLife after the main sequenceEnd
Below about 0.5 M☉Red dwarfs last longer than the present age of the universe; none has died yet.(will become white dwarfs)
About 0.5–8 M☉ (e.g. the Sun)Core hydrogen runs out → core contracts and heats, hydrogen fuses in a shell → outer layers expand and cool: red giant → helium fuses to carbon and oxygen → outer layers drift off as a planetary nebula.White dwarf (carbon–oxygen core, supported by electron degeneracy pressure), slowly cooling
About 8–25 M☉Red supergiant, fusing successively heavier elements in shells up to iron → iron core collapses → supernova.Neutron star
Above about 25 M☉Supergiant → supernova.Black hole
Mass limits for remnants
  • Chandrasekhar limit, about 1.4 M☉: the largest mass a white dwarf can have. Above it, electron degeneracy pressure cannot resist gravity.
  • Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit, about 2–3 M☉: the largest mass of a neutron star. Above it, nothing stops the collapse to a black hole.
These limits apply to the mass of the remnant core, not the original star: a star loses most of its mass on the way.
Example 10.1The Sun’s main-sequence lifetime is 1.0 × 10¹⁰ years. Using lifetime ∝ M−2.5, estimate the lifetime of a 5.0 M☉ star.
t = 1.0 × 10^{10} × 5.0^{-2.5} => t ≈ 1.8 × 10^{8} years

11How are elements created?

Guiding question 1How are elements created?
  • Hydrogen, helium (and a little lithium): made in the first few minutes after the Big Bang.
  • Carbon to iron: made by fusion inside stars. A massive star near the end of its life is layered like an onion: H → He → C → Ne → O → Si → Fe, each shell hotter than the one outside it.
  • Fusion stops at iron: iron-56 is at the peak of the binding energy per nucleon curve (E.4), so fusing it would absorb energy, not release it. The iron core can no longer support itself and collapses.
  • Heavier than iron (gold, uranium…): made by neutron capture, slowly in red giants and rapidly in supernovae and in collisions of neutron stars, which scatter these elements into space.
Answer to guiding question 1Apart from hydrogen and helium, almost every atom in your body was made by fusion or neutron capture in stars that lived and died before the Sun formed. Their explosions spread these elements into gas clouds that later formed new stars, planets and people.

12Observation, technology and the future

Linking question (NOS)In which ways has technology helped to collect data from observations of distant stars?
  • Larger telescopes collect more light, so fainter, more distant stars can be studied.
  • Spectrographs (diffraction gratings) separate starlight into spectra for temperature and composition.
  • CCD detectors record about 90% of the photons that arrive, compared with about 1% for photographic plates.
  • Space telescopes avoid the blurring of the atmosphere and observe in infrared, ultraviolet and X-ray wavelengths that the atmosphere absorbs (Hubble, James Webb).
  • Astrometry satellites (Hipparcos, Gaia) measure parallaxes thousands of times more precisely than from the ground.
  • Computers process billions of measurements and run models of stellar interiors.
Guiding question 3Can observations of the present state of the universe predict the future outcome of the universe?

For stars, yes, with some confidence: by seeing stars of many masses at different stages, we can predict that the Sun will stay on the main sequence for about another 5 billion years, then become a red giant (swallowing Mercury and Venus, and possibly the Earth) and end as a white dwarf. As stars use up the gas in galaxies, fewer new stars will form. Predictions for the universe as a whole depend on its expansion, which is studied in cosmology, beyond E.5. Predictions always rest on the assumption that the laws of physics stay the same.

Common exam mistakes
  • Drawing temperature increasing to the right on the HR diagram.
  • Forgetting the 4th power of T, or not squaring R.
  • Using the diameter instead of the radius.
  • Leaving p in degrees or radians in d = 1/p (it must be in arc-seconds).
  • Confusing luminosity (W) with apparent brightness (W m⁻²).
  • Saying “radiation pressure” alone: the gas (thermal) pressure is just as important.
  • Saying a white dwarf is “burning”: it has no fusion and is cooling.
  • Applying the Chandrasekhar limit to the original star rather than the core.
  • Saying stars fuse elements heavier than iron to release energy.

Equilibrium

  • Gravity in = radiation + gas pressure out
  • Fusion acts as a thermostat
  • Fuel runs out → imbalance → evolution
  • Gas laws: P = nkBT models the core

  • 4 ¹H → ⁴He: 26.7 MeV, 0.7% of mass

  • Needs ~10⁷ K and high density
  • Coulomb barrier; tunnelling helps

  • λmaxT = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K
  • L = σ4πR²T⁴
  • b = L/(4πd²)
  • Absorption lines → composition
  • OBAFGKM: hot → cool

Distances and radii

  • d(pc) = 1/p(″)
  • 1 pc = 3.26 ly = 3.09 × 10¹⁶ m
  • 1 AU = 1.50 × 10¹¹ m
  • R = √(L/4πσT⁴)
  • R/R☉ = √(L/L☉)(T☉/T)²

HR diagram

  • L up (log), T decreasing → (log)
  • MS: top left to bottom right
  • Giants top right, WDs bottom left
  • Supergiants across the top
  • Constant-R lines; instability strip

Evolution

  • Lifetime ∝ M−2.5
  • < 8 M☉: red giant → PN → white dwarf
  • 8–25 M☉: supergiant → SN → neutron star
  • > 25 M☉: → black hole
  • Chandrasekhar 1.4 M☉ (core)

Top 8 mistakes to avoid

  1. Temperature decreases to the right on an HR diagram.
  2. T to the 4th power, R squared.
  3. Parallax angle in arc-seconds.
  4. Luminosity (W) ≠ brightness (W m⁻²).
  5. Radius, not diameter.
  6. White dwarfs have no fusion.
  7. Mass limits apply to the remnant core.
  8. No energy from fusing beyond iron.
