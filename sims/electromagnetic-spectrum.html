7.5 · One family, many wavelengths

The Electromagnetic Spectrum

From radio waves to γ-rays, every electromagnetic wave is transverse and travels at the same speed c in free space. They differ only in wavelength and frequency — a continuous family spanning more than twenty powers of ten.

c = fλ

The principal regions, longest to shortest wavelength

  • Radio — longer than about 10⁻¹ m
  • Microwave — 10⁻¹ m to 10⁻³ m
  • Infrared — 10⁻³ m to 7 × 10⁻⁷ m
  • Visible — 7 × 10⁻⁷ m to 4 × 10⁻⁷ m (700 nm to 400 nm)
  • Ultraviolet — 4 × 10⁻⁷ m to 10⁻⁸ m
  • X-rays — 10⁻⁸ m to 10⁻¹³ m
  • γ-rays — shorter than about 10⁻¹⁰ m

These boundaries are approximate and the regions blend into one another — the X-ray and γ-ray ranges even overlap, distinguished by their origin rather than wavelength. A common memory aid, long to short, is “Roman Men Invented Very Unusual X-ray Guns”.

A student lists four electromagnetic waves with wavelengths 3 × 10⁻² m, 5 × 10⁻⁷ m, 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m and 2 × 10⁻ⁱ m.
Question
(a) Name the region each wavelength belongs to. [2]
3 × 10⁻² m → microwave; 5 × 10⁻⁷ m → visible (green); 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m → X-ray; 2 × 10⁻ⁱ m → infrared.
(b) State which has the highest frequency, and justify. [2]
The X-ray (1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m). Since c = fλ is the same for all, the shortest wavelength has the highest frequency.
(c) State what all four waves have in common. [2]
All are transverse electromagnetic waves and all travel at the same speed, c = 3.00 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹, in free space.
AS · Enrichment

An electromagnetic wave is a pair of oscillating fields travelling together: an electric field E and a magnetic field B, at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel. Nothing material moves — the changing fields regenerate one another and carry energy through empty space.

E and B, perpendicular and in step

Red = electric field (vertical plane). Blue = magnetic field (drawn receding, into the page). Both oscillate in phase and travel to the right at the speed of light.

Why it is a transverse wave

The electric field oscillates in one plane; the magnetic field oscillates in a plane at 90° to it. Both are perpendicular to the direction in which the wave travels, so an electromagnetic wave is transverse.

The two fields rise and fall in phase — both reach their maxima together and both are zero together — with the same frequency and wavelength.

A changing electric field sets up a changing magnetic field, and that changing magnetic field sets up an electric field again. This self-sustaining cycle means the wave needs no medium and travels through a vacuum at c = 3.00 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹.

Because the electric field has a definite plane of vibration, electromagnetic waves can be polarised — the subject of the next section.

A beam of microwaves travels horizontally towards the east. Its electric field oscillates vertically.
Question
(a) State the direction in which the magnetic field oscillates. [1]
Horizontally, pointing north–south — perpendicular to both the vertical electric field and the eastward direction of travel.
(b) State and explain whether this confirms the wave is transverse. [2]
Yes. Both oscillating fields are at right angles to the direction of energy travel, which is the defining feature of a transverse wave.
(c) State why the microwaves can travel from a satellite to Earth through the vacuum of space. [1]
Electromagnetic waves are not mechanical: the oscillating E and B fields sustain each other, so no material medium is needed.
AS · Standard

Whatever the region, every electromagnetic wave is transverse and travels at the same speed in free space: c = 3.00 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹. That single fact links wavelength and frequency through c = fλ — a long wavelength always means a low frequency, and vice versa.

Pick a region

Each region has a huge wavelength range, but the same speed c threads through all of them. Selecting a region below shows a representative wavelength and the frequency it implies.

Note: c is the speed in a vacuum (free space). In glass, water or any medium an EM wave slows down; its frequency stays the same, so its wavelength shortens.

c = fλ in action

f = cλc = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹
A radio station broadcasts at a frequency of 95.8 MHz. (c = 3.00 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹)
Question
(a) Calculate the wavelength of the broadcast. [2]
λ = cf=3.00 × 10⁸95.8 × 10⁶≈3.13 m
Consistent with the radio region.
(b) An X-ray has a wavelength of 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Calculate its frequency. [2]
f = cλ=3.00 × 10⁸1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰=3.0 × 10¹⁸ Hz
(c) The X-ray enters glass, where it slows down. State what happens to its frequency and wavelength. [2]
The frequency is unchanged (set by the source). Since v = fλ and v is now smaller, the wavelength decreases.

Violet (~400 nm) has the shortest wavelength and highest frequency; red (~700 nm) has the longest wavelength and lowest frequency.

Colour, wavelength, frequency

f = cλ
Red light has a wavelength of 700 nm and violet light a wavelength of 400 nm in free space. (c = 3.00 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹)
Question
(a) Calculate the frequency of the red light. [2]
f = 3.00 × 10⁸700 × 10⁻⁹≈4.3 × 10¹⁴ Hz
(b) State, with a reason, which colour has the higher frequency. [2]
Violet. It has the shorter wavelength, and since f = c/λ with c fixed, a shorter wavelength gives a higher frequency (about 7.5 × 10¹⁴ Hz).
(c) State the region immediately beyond the violet end of the visible spectrum. [1]
Ultraviolet (shorter wavelength than violet).
AS · Reference

The whole spectrum at a glance, from longest wavelength to shortest. Wavelength and frequency ranges are approximate — the regions blend into one another, and X-rays and γ-rays overlap, being distinguished by how they are produced rather than by wavelength.

RegionWavelength / mFrequency / HzHow it is producedTypical uses
Radio> 10⁻¹< 3 × 10⁹Oscillating electrons in aerials and tuned (LC) circuitsRadio and TV broadcasting, long-range communication
Microwave10⁻³ – 10⁻¹3 × 10⁹ – 3 × 10¹¹Magnetron or klystron; electron oscillations in special tubesWi-Fi, mobile phones, radar, satellite links, ovens
Infrared7 × 10⁻⁷ – 10⁻³3 × 10¹¹ – 4 × 10¹⁴Vibration of molecules in warm and hot objectsThermal imaging, remote controls, optical fibre, heaters
Visible4 × 10⁻⁷ – 7 × 10⁻⁷4 × 10¹⁴ – 8 × 10¹⁴Outer-electron transitions in atoms; hot objects, LEDsHuman sight, photography, lighting, fibre communication
Ultraviolet10⁻⁸ – 4 × 10⁻⁷8 × 10¹⁴ – 3 × 10¹⁶Higher-energy electron transitions; gas-discharge lamps, the SunFluorescence, sterilisation, security marks; causes suntan
X-ray10⁻¹³ – 10⁻⁸3 × 10¹⁶ – 3 × 10²¹Rapid deceleration of fast electrons hitting a metal target; inner-shell transitionsMedical imaging, airport security, crystallography
γ-ray< 10⁻¹⁰> 10¹⁹Emitted by the atomic nucleus during radioactive decay / nuclear reactionsCancer radiotherapy, sterilising equipment, astronomy

All regions are transverse electromagnetic waves and travel at the same speed, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, in free space. Only the wavelength and frequency differ — and they are tied together by c = fλ.

Reading the table

Moving down the table, wavelength falls and frequency rises (because c = fλ is fixed), and the photons carry progressively more energy — which is why γ-rays and X-rays are ionising and dangerous, while radio waves are not. The production mechanism also shifts: long waves come from electrons oscillating in circuits, the middle of the spectrum from electron transitions in atoms, and the shortest γ-rays from the nucleus itself.

AS · Standard

Four problems using c = fλ across the spectrum. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹. Try each before revealing the solution.

Worked Example · Frequency from wavelength
A microwave oven uses radiation of wavelength 0.12 m. Find its frequency and name the region.
f = cλ = 3.00 × 10⁸0.12=2.5 × 10⁹ Hz

That is 2.5 GHz — a microwave, as expected for a wavelength of a few centimetres.

Worked Example · Wavelength from frequency
An ultraviolet lamp emits radiation of frequency 1.0 × 10¹⁶ Hz. Find its wavelength and confirm the region.
λ = cf = 3.00 × 10⁸1.0 × 10¹⁶=3.0 × 10⁻⁸ m

Between 4 × 10⁻⁷ m and 10⁻⁸ m — firmly in the ultraviolet.

Worked Example · Ordering by frequency
Place these in order of increasing frequency: infrared, radio, X-ray, visible.

Frequency rises as wavelength falls, so order of increasing frequency is the order of decreasing wavelength:

radio < infrared < visible < X-ray.

Worked Example · Identify the region
A wave has a wavelength of 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ m. Name the region and estimate its frequency.

600 nm lies between 400 nm and 700 nm, so it is visible (orange).

f = 3.00 × 10⁸6.0 × 10⁻⁷=5.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz

The traps on the electromagnetic spectrum. Each card pairs the wrong idea with the correct physics.

"Different EM waves travel at different speeds in a vacuum."

In free space every electromagnetic wave — radio to γ — travels at exactly the same speed, c = 3.00 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹.

All EM waves share the same c in a vacuum.
"Electromagnetic waves need a medium to travel."

Unlike sound, EM waves are not mechanical — they travel through a vacuum, which is how sunlight reaches us.

EM waves cross empty space.
"Higher frequency means a longer wavelength."

Since c = fλ with c fixed, frequency and wavelength are inversely related: higher frequency means shorter wavelength.

f ∝ 1/λ (c constant).
"Radio waves are a kind of sound wave."

Radio waves are transverse electromagnetic waves. Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave — completely different.

Radio = EM (transverse), not sound.
"Visible light fills most of the spectrum."

The visible band (400–700 nm) is a tiny sliver; the full spectrum spans more than twenty powers of ten in wavelength.

Visible light is a very small part.
"EM waves are longitudinal, like sound."

All electromagnetic waves are transverse — which is why they can be polarised (topic 7.6).

EM waves are transverse.
