01Models before the nucleus

A model of the atom is a simplified picture that explains the evidence available at the time. Each new model in the history of the atom was forced by an experiment the old one could not explain.

ModelPictureEvidence behind it
Dalton (1803)atoms are tiny, solid, indivisible spheres; each element has its own kindchemical elements combine in fixed whole-number ratios
Thomson (1904), “plum pudding”a sphere of spread-out positive charge with negative electrons embedded in itthe discovery of the electron (1897): atoms contain small negative particles, so the rest must be positive

In the plum-pudding model the positive charge and the mass are spread through the whole atom (about 10⁻¹⁰ m across). The electric field inside is therefore weak everywhere. A fast, heavy α particle passing through should be deflected by a tiny fraction of a degree, and never turned back.

Nature of scienceA model is kept while it agrees with experiment. One reliable result that contradicts it, repeated by others, is enough to force a new model. That is exactly what happened next.

02The Geiger–Marsden–Rutherford experiment

In 1909, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, working under Ernest Rutherford in Manchester, fired α particles (helium nuclei, charge +2e, about 5 MeV) at a very thin gold foil and measured how they were scattered.

  • The source sits in a lead box with a narrow hole, so the α particles form a narrow beam.
  • The whole apparatus is evacuated: α particles travel only a few centimetres in air.
  • The gold foil is very thin (a few hundred atoms thick), so each α particle is scattered by at most one nucleus. Gold can be beaten very thin, and its nuclei are heavy and highly charged (Z = 79).
  • Each α particle hitting the zinc sulfide screen makes a tiny flash (a scintillation), counted by eye through a microscope. The screen and microscope rotate to any scattering angle θ.
ObservationSize of the effect
Most α particles pass straight through or are deflected by only a few degreesthe great majority
A small fraction are deflected through large anglesfalls steeply with angle
A very few are deflected by more than 90°, back towards the sourceabout 1 in 8000
Guidance: SL is qualitativeAt SL you must describe the experiment and explain its conclusions. You do not need to calculate scattering angles. The HL extension (sections 9–11) adds closest approach and nuclear size.

03Rutherford’s nuclear model

Rutherford said the large-angle results were “as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you”. In 1911 he explained them with a new model.

ObservationConclusion
Most α particles pass straight throughmost of the atom is empty space
Some are deflected, a very few through more than 90°there is a very strong electric field in a tiny region, so the positive charge is concentrated in a very small nucleus
Even fast, heavy α particles can be turned backthe nucleus contains nearly all the mass of the atom (a light nucleus would just recoil)
Deflections are always away from the nucleusthe force is repulsive: the nucleus is positive, like the α particle
The nuclear modelAn atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus, about 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m across, containing almost all its mass. Electrons move in the mostly empty space around it, out to about 10⁻¹⁰ m. The nucleus is about 10⁴ to 10⁵ times smaller than the atom.
Example 3.1An atom has a radius of about 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m and its nucleus a radius of about 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ m. Estimate the fraction of the atom’s volume taken up by the nucleus.
frac{V_{nucleus}}{V_{atom}} = (frac{r_{nucleus}}{r_{atom}})^{3} = (frac{10^{-14}}{10^{-10}})^{3} => frac{V_{nucleus}}{V_{atom}} = 10^{-12}
Common mistake“The α particles bounced off the nucleus.” They never touch it at these energies: they are repelled by the electric force and turn back before reaching it. And “most were deflected backwards” is wrong: only about 1 in 8000 were.
Linking question (NOS)How have observations led to developments in the model of the atom?
From observation to modelThe electron (Thomson, 1897) led to the plum-pudding model. Large-angle α scattering (1909) led to the nuclear model (1911). The line spectrum of hydrogen led to the Bohr model (1913, section 12). Missing mass in nuclei led to the discovery of the neutron (Chadwick, 1932). Each step kept what still worked and replaced what the new evidence contradicted.

04Nuclide notation and isotopes

The nucleus contains protons (charge +e) and neutrons (no charge), together called nucleons. A particular nucleus is called a nuclide and is written:

Nuclide notation^{A}_{Z}X   A = nucleon number (protons + neutrons), Z = proton number, X = chemical symbol. Number of neutrons N = A − Z. In a neutral atom the number of electrons equals Z.
IsotopesNuclei of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A). Example: ^{12}_{6}C and ^{14}_{6}C both have 6 protons, but 6 and 8 neutrons. Isotopes have the same chemistry, because that depends on the electrons.
Example 4.1State the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in the ion ^{23}_{11}Na⁺.
protons = Z = 11 neutrons = A - Z = 23 - 11 = 12 => electrons = 11 - 1 = 10 (the + ion has lost one electron)
GuidanceYou do not need to remember chemical symbols. A question will give Z, or the name, whenever you need it.

05Emission and absorption spectra

Hot solids, liquids and dense gases give a continuous spectrum: all wavelengths, merging into one another. Isolated atoms, in a low-pressure gas, behave very differently.

  • Emission spectrum: a low-pressure gas is excited by heating or by an electric discharge. It emits light of only certain wavelengths, seen through a diffraction grating or prism as bright lines on a dark background.
  • Absorption spectrum: white light (a continuous spectrum) passes through a cool gas. The gas absorbs only certain wavelengths, giving dark lines on a continuous spectrum.
  • For any one element, the dark absorption lines are at exactly the same wavelengths as its bright emission lines.
Why the absorption lines are darkThe atoms that absorb photons do re-emit them, but in all directions. Very little of the re-emitted light carries on in the original direction, so those wavelengths are missing from the beam. Looked at from the side, the same gas shows faint bright emission lines.

06Discrete energy levels

Why only certain wavelengths? Because the electrons in an atom can only have certain energies, called energy levels.

Energy levelsAn electron bound in an atom can only have certain discrete (separate, fixed) energies. The lowest is the ground state; higher ones are excited states. Energies are negative because work must be done to remove the electron completely: 0 eV is the energy of an electron just free of the atom. The energy needed to free an electron from the ground state is the ionisation energy.
Spectra are the evidenceIf an electron could have any energy, atoms would emit every wavelength: a continuous spectrum. The fact that each element emits and absorbs only certain wavelengths shows that only certain energy differences exist inside the atom, so the energy levels are discrete.

07Photons and transitions: ΔE = hf

When an electron moves from a higher level to a lower one (a transition), the atom loses energy by emitting one photon. To move up, the atom must absorb exactly the right energy.

Photon energy in a transitionΔE = E_{upper} - E_{lower} = h f = frac{h c}{λ}
h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. Bigger gap → higher frequency → shorter wavelength. A handy check: λ (in nm) ≈ frac{1240}{ΔE (in eV)}.
  • Emission: the electron falls; one photon of energy ΔE is emitted.
  • Absorption: a photon is absorbed only if its energy exactly equals a gap (or is enough to ionise the atom). A photon cannot be partly absorbed.
  • Collisions: a free electron colliding with an atom can give just part of its kinetic energy, so it can excite the atom if its energy is at least the gap.
  • With n levels there are n(n − 1)/2 possible transitions, and so that many lines.
Example 7.1In the atom above, an electron falls from −1.6 eV to −3.7 eV. Calculate the wavelength of the photon emitted.
ΔE = -1.6 - (-3.7) = 2.1 eV ΔE = 2.1 × 1.60 × 10^{-19} = 3.36 × 10^{-19} J λ = frac{h c}{ΔE} = frac{6.63 × 10^{-34} × 3.00 × 10^{8}}{3.36 × 10^{-19}} => λ = 5.92 × 10^{-7} m (590 nm, yellow-orange)
Example 7.2The transition from −5.5 eV to −10.4 eV emits one photon. Calculate its frequency and state the region of the spectrum.
ΔE = 4.9 eV = 4.9 × 1.60 × 10^{-19} = 7.84 × 10^{-19} J f = frac{ΔE}{h} = frac{7.84 × 10^{-19}}{6.63 × 10^{-34}} => f = 1.18 × 10^{15} Hz # λ = c/f = 2.54 × 10⁻⁷ m = 254 nm: ultraviolet (the strong mercury line used in fluorescent lamps)
Common mistakeUsing one level’s energy (such as 3.7 eV) as the photon energy. The photon energy is always the difference between two levels.

08Spectra and the stars

Every element has its own set of energy levels, so its own unique pattern of lines, like a barcode or a fingerprint. Matching lines with those measured in the laboratory identifies the elements present: this is spectroscopy. It gives the chemical composition of a gas, a flame, a sample in a forensic laboratory, or a star billions of kilometres away.

Helium was found in the Sun firstIn 1868 a yellow line at 587.6 nm was seen in the spectrum of the Sun’s outer layers during an eclipse. It matched no known element. It was named helium after the Greek for Sun (helios), and was not found on Earth until 1895.
Linking questionHow can emission spectra allow for the properties of stars to be deduced?
What a star’s spectrum tells usThe dark absorption lines in a star’s spectrum come from cooler gas in its outer layers: they give its composition. Which lines are strong also depends on the temperature of that gas, which is how stars are classified into spectral types (linking to E.5 and the peak wavelength of the continuous spectrum).
Linking questionHow can emission spectra be used to calculate the distances and velocities of celestial bodies?

If a star or galaxy is moving away from us, the whole pattern of lines is shifted to longer wavelengths (redshift); towards us, to shorter wavelengths (blueshift). The pattern is still recognisable, so the shift can be measured. For speeds much less than c:

Doppler shift of spectral lines (C.5)frac{Δλ}{λ} ≈ frac{v}{c}
Example 8.1The hydrogen line at 656.3 nm in the laboratory is observed at 662.9 nm in the spectrum of a galaxy. Determine the speed of the galaxy and whether it is approaching or receding.
Δλ = 662.9 - 656.3 = 6.6 nm v = c frac{Δλ}{λ} = 3.00 × 10^{8} × frac{6.6}{656.3} => v = 3.0 × 10^{6} m s^{-1}, moving away (redshift)

For distant galaxies, the speed of recession is proportional to distance (Hubble’s law), so the redshift of their spectral lines also gives their distance.

09Nuclear radius and density HL

Scattering experiments with high-energy electrons (which feel only the electric force, not the strong force) show that the radius of a nucleus depends on its nucleon number:

Nuclear radiusR = R_{0} A^{1/3}   with R₀ = 1.20 × 10⁻¹⁵ m (the Fermi radius, roughly the radius of one nucleon). 1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m.

Volume is proportional to R³, so the volume of a nucleus is proportional to A: each nucleon takes up the same space, as if the nucleons were packed together like marbles in a bag.

Example 9.1Show that all nuclei have the same density, and estimate it. (u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg)
m ≈ A u V = frac{4}{3} π R^{3} = frac{4}{3} π R_{0}^{3} A ρ = frac{m}{V} = frac{A u}{frac{4}{3} π R_{0}^{3} A} = frac{u}{frac{4}{3} π R_{0}^{3}} # A cancels, so the density is the same for every nucleus ρ = frac{1.66 × 10^{-27}}{frac{4}{3} π (1.20 × 10^{-15})^{3}} => ρ ≈ 2.3 × 10^{17} kg m^{-3}
Example 9.2Calculate the radius of a ^{238}_{92}U nucleus.
R = 1.20 × 10^{-15} × 238^{1/3} = 1.20 × 10^{-15} × 6.20 => R = 7.4 × 10^{-15} m
How dense?2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³ is about 10¹⁴ times the density of water. A teaspoon (5 cm³) of nuclear matter would have a mass of about 10¹² kg. Neutron stars (E.5) are made of matter this dense.

10Distance of closest approach HL

An α particle fired head-on at a nucleus slows down as it is repelled, stops for an instant, and comes straight back. At that point all of its kinetic energy has become electric potential energy.

Closest approach (energy conservation)E_{k} = frac{k q_{1} q_{2}}{d} = frac{k (2e)(Ze)}{d}   so   d = frac{2 k Z e^{2}}{E_{k}}
k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻². The electric potential energy of two charges is kq₁q₂/r (D.2). Make Ek joules: 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J.
Example 10.1An α particle with kinetic energy 5.0 MeV moves head-on towards a gold nucleus (Z = 79). Calculate the distance of closest approach.
E_{k} = 5.0 × 10^{6} × 1.60 × 10^{-19} = 8.0 × 10^{-13} J d = frac{k (2e)(79e)}{E_{k}} = frac{8.99 × 10^{9} × 2 × 79 × (1.60 × 10^{-19})^{2}}{8.0 × 10^{-13}} => d = 4.5 × 10^{-14} m (45 fm)
An upper limit for the radiusThe α particle is turned back before it touches the nucleus, so the nucleus must be smaller than d. For gold, R = 1.20 × 1971/3 = 7.0 fm, much less than 45 fm. This is how Rutherford first set an upper limit on nuclear size.
Linking questionHow is the distance of closest approach calculated using conservation of energy?

Total energy is conserved: Ek + Ep is constant. Far away, Ep ≈ 0 and all the energy is kinetic. At the closest point of a head-on collision the α particle is momentarily at rest, so Ek = 0 and all the energy is electric potential energy, kq₁q₂/d. (The gold nucleus is about 50 times heavier, so its recoil can be ignored.) For a glancing collision the α particle still has some kinetic energy at its closest point, so it does not get as close.

GuidanceIn these questions the energies are low enough that only the electric force acts. You will not be asked to include the strong force.

11Deviations from Rutherford scattering HL

Rutherford’s scattering formula assumes that the only force on the α particle is the electric (Coulomb) repulsion of a point charge. It predicts that the number scattered through angle θ is proportional to 1/sin⁴(θ/2), and it fits the gold-foil data very well.

When α particles of higher energy are used, or lighter target nuclei (smaller Z, so a weaker repulsion), the measured numbers at large angles fall below Rutherford’s prediction. The α particles are now getting close enough to touch the nucleus, where the short-range strong nuclear force also acts.

Example 11.1For aluminium (Z = 13, A = 27), estimate the α-particle energy at which deviations from Rutherford scattering begin. Take the α radius as 1.9 fm.
R_{Al} = 1.20 × 27^{1/3} = 3.6 fm # they touch when the centres are 3.6 + 1.9 = 5.5 fm apart E_{k} = frac{k (2e)(13e)}{d} = frac{8.99 × 10^{9} × 26 × (1.60 × 10^{-19})^{2}}{5.5 × 10^{-15}} = 1.09 × 10^{-12} J => E_{k} ≈ 6.8 MeV
Using the deviationThe energy at which the deviation starts tells us the distance at which the α particle reaches the nucleus, and so gives the nuclear radius. For gold the same estimate gives about 26 MeV, far above the 5 MeV used by Geiger and Marsden. That is why their results fitted Rutherford’s formula so well.

12The Bohr model of hydrogen HL

Rutherford’s model had a serious problem. An electron orbiting a nucleus is accelerating, and classical physics says an accelerating charge radiates energy. The electron should spiral into the nucleus in about 10⁻¹¹ s, giving out a continuous spectrum. Atoms are stable, and give line spectra. In 1913 Niels Bohr proposed a model for hydrogen:

  • The electron moves in certain allowed circular orbits, and in these it does not radiate.
  • Each orbit has a fixed energy. The energy of level n is:
Energy levels of hydrogenE_{n} = - frac{13.6}{n^{2}} eV   n = 1, 2, 3, … (the principal quantum number)
n = 1: −13.6 eV · n = 2: −3.40 eV · n = 3: −1.51 eV · n = 4: −0.85 eV · n = ∞: 0
  • A photon is emitted or absorbed only when the electron moves between orbits: hf = Eupper − Elower.
  • Transitions ending on n = 1 are ultraviolet (Lyman series); those ending on n = 2 include the four visible lines (Balmer series); those ending on n = 3 are infrared (Paschen series).
Example 12.1Calculate the wavelength of the photon emitted when the electron in hydrogen moves from n = 3 to n = 2.
E_{3} = - frac{13.6}{9} = -1.51 eV,   E_{2} = - frac{13.6}{4} = -3.40 eV ΔE = 1.89 eV = 3.02 × 10^{-19} J λ = frac{h c}{ΔE} = frac{6.63 × 10^{-34} × 3.00 × 10^{8}}{3.02 × 10^{-19}} => λ = 6.58 × 10^{-7} m (the red line of hydrogen)
Example 12.2Determine the ionisation energy of a hydrogen atom in the n = 2 state.
E_{2} = - frac{13.6}{2^{2}} = -3.40 eV => ionisation energy = 0 - (-3.40) = 3.40 eV

13Quantised angular momentum HL

Bohr’s key assumption was that the angular momentum of the electron is quantised:

Bohr’s conditionm v r = frac{n h}{2 π}
m = electron mass, v = its speed, r = orbit radius, n = 1, 2, 3, … The allowed orbits, and their energies, follow from this.

Combine it with the electric force providing the centripetal force, mv²/r = ke²/r²:

v = frac{n h}{2 π m r} m v^{2} = frac{k e^{2}}{r}   →   r = frac{n^{2} h^{2}}{4 π^{2} m k e^{2}} # so r ∝ n²: the radius of orbit n is n² times the radius of the first orbit E = frac{1}{2} m v^{2} - frac{k e^{2}}{r} = - frac{k e^{2}}{2 r} # so E ∝ −1/n², and with the constants E₁ = −13.6 eV
Example 13.1Calculate the radius of the n = 1 orbit and the speed of the electron in it. (m = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²)
r = frac{(6.63 × 10^{-34})^{2}}{4 π^{2} × 9.11 × 10^{-31} × 8.99 × 10^{9} × (1.60 × 10^{-19})^{2}} = 5.3 × 10^{-11} m v = frac{h}{2 π m r} = frac{6.63 × 10^{-34}}{2 π × 9.11 × 10^{-31} × 5.31 × 10^{-11}} => v = 2.2 × 10^{6} m s^{-1} (under 1% of c)
A link to E.2: the orbit fits a whole number of electron wavesThe electron has a de Broglie wavelength λ = h/mv. If a whole number of wavelengths fits round the orbit, 2πr = nλ = nh/mv, which rearranges to mvr = nh/2π: exactly Bohr’s condition. The allowed orbits are the ones where the electron wave forms a standing wave.
Linking question (NOS)Under what circumstances does the Bohr model fail?
Limitations of the Bohr model
  • It works only for hydrogen and one-electron ions (He⁺, Li²⁺). It cannot predict the spectrum of helium or any atom with more than one electron.
  • It cannot explain why some lines are brighter than others, or the fine structure (lines that split into close pairs).
  • It gives the electron a definite orbit and speed at the same time, which the uncertainty principle forbids. Modern quantum mechanics replaces orbits with orbitals: regions where the electron is likely to be found.
  • It does not explain why angular momentum is quantised; it just assumes it.
Guiding question 3: why old models are still validThe Bohr model gives the correct energy levels of hydrogen, and it is still the quickest way to picture where spectral lines come from. The nuclear model is still correct about the tiny dense nucleus. Older models are kept wherever they give the right answers simply; we just know where they stop working.

Common exam mistakes
  • Giving Rutherford’s conclusions without the observations that support them.
  • Saying the α particles “hit” or “bounced off” the nucleus.
  • Using the energy of one level instead of the difference between two.
  • Mixing eV and J in ΔE = hf.
  • Saying a photon with more energy than a gap is absorbed: it must match exactly (unless it ionises the atom).
  • HL: using charge e instead of 2e for the α particle.
  • HL: multiplying R₀ by A instead of A1/3.
  • HL: treating the closest approach as the nuclear radius.

15One-page summary

E.1 Structure of the atom · one-page summary

Geiger–Marsden

  • α particles at thin gold foil, in a vacuum
  • Most pass straight through → mostly empty space
  • About 1 in 8000 > 90° → small, dense, positive nucleus
  • Repulsion, not contact

The nucleus

  • ᴬZX: A nucleons, Z protons
  • N = A − Z neutrons
  • Isotopes: same Z, different N
  • Nucleus ≈ 10⁻¹⁵–10⁻¹⁴ m; atom ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m

Spectra

  • Emission: bright lines from a hot low-pressure gas
  • Absorption: dark lines on a continuous spectrum
  • Same wavelengths for one element
  • Lines identify elements, even in stars

Energy levels

  • Discrete levels; ground state lowest
  • ΔE = hf = hc/λ; λ(nm) ≈ 1240/ΔE(eV)
  • Photon absorbed only if energy matches a gap
  • n levels → n(n − 1)/2 lines

Nucleus HL

  • R = R₀A1/3, R₀ = 1.20 fm
  • Density ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³ for all nuclei
  • Closest approach: Ek = k(2e)(Ze)/d
  • Deviations at high Ek: strong force acts

Bohr HL

  • En = −13.6/n² eV
  • mvr = nh/2π
  • r ∝ n²; r₁ = 5.3 × 10⁻¹¹ m
  • Fails for atoms with more than one electron

Top 8 mistakes to avoid

  1. Pair every Rutherford conclusion with its observation.
  2. α particles are repelled; they do not touch the nucleus.
  3. Photon energy is a difference between levels.
  4. Convert eV to J before using h in J s.
  5. A photon must match a gap exactly; an electron can give part of its energy.
  6. Absorption lines are at the same λ as emission lines.
  7. HL: α charge is 2e; cube-root A.
  8. HL: closest approach is an upper limit for R.

Models of the atom compared

Guiding question 3In what ways are previous models of the atom still valid despite recent advances in understanding?
ModelPictureExplainedCould not explainStill useful for
Dalton (1803)solid, indivisible spheresfixed ratios in chemical reactionselectrons, ions, spectragas laws and kinetic theory (B.3): atoms as hard spheres
Thomson (1904)positive “pudding” with electrons embeddedatoms are neutral but contain electronslarge-angle α scatteringthe idea that atoms contain charges that can be separated
Rutherford (1911)tiny dense positive nucleus, electrons around it in empty spaceGeiger–Marsden scatteringwhy atoms are stable; line spectranuclear physics: size, charge and mass of the nucleus
Bohr (1913)electrons in fixed orbits with quantised angular momentumhydrogen’s line spectrum and ionisation energyatoms with more than one electron; line intensitiesenergy-level diagrams and photon energies
Quantum (1926 on)electrons as standing waves in orbitals; nucleus of protons and neutronsall spectra, chemical bonding, the periodic tablestill being extended (quarks inside nucleons)the current model
Nature of scienceNo model is thrown away completely. Each is kept for the job it does well: chemists still draw Bohr-style shells, and kinetic theory treats atoms as Dalton’s hard spheres. What changes is our knowledge of where each model stops working, and that is always decided by experiment.
