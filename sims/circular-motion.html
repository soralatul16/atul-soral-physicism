Speed is constant, but velocity is not. Velocity is a vector, and its direction changes every instant as the object rounds the circle. A changing velocity means acceleration — even with no change in speed.

The key relationships

v = ωr
Linear speed links to angular speed ω and radius r.
a꜀ = v²/r = ω²r
Centripetal acceleration — always directed toward the centre.
F꜀ = mv²/r = mω²r
The net inward force required to keep the object on its circular path.
T = 2π/ω, f = 1/T
Period T and frequency f of one full revolution.

Why "centripetal", not "centrifugal"

The velocity vector points along the tangent — the direction the object would fly if suddenly freed. The acceleration and net force point inward, toward the centre. That inward force is called centripetal ("centre-seeking"). It is not a new kind of force; it is the name for the role played by whatever real force happens to point inward.

The centrifugal "force" is a misconception. In an inertial frame there is no outward force on the object. The feeling of being flung outward is your body's inertia — it wants to travel in a straight line while the seat, string, or track pushes you inward.

Where the centripetal force comes from

There is no "centripetal force" generator. In every real situation, an ordinary force takes on the inward-pointing role — exactly the four scenarios in this simulator:

🪢

Tension

A ball whirled on a string — the string tension pulls inward.

🛞

Friction

A car rounding a bend — friction between tyres and road.

🪐

Gravity

Planets orbiting the Sun, held by gravitational attraction.

⚛️

Electrostatic

An electron bound to the nucleus by the Coulomb force.

Orbital speed depends on distance, not mass

For gravity, the centripetal force is GMm/r² = mv²/r. The orbiting mass m cancels, giving v = √(GM/r). That is why Jupiter — 318 times Earth's mass — still orbits far slower than Earth: it is farther out. The same maths, with the Coulomb force, sets an electron's speed at each Bohr radius.

Q1 · Reasoning

An object moves around a circle at a perfectly constant speed. Explain how it can still be accelerating.

Velocity is a vector — it has direction as well as magnitude. Although the speed is constant, the direction of motion changes continuously. A changing velocity is, by definition, an acceleration, directed toward the centre.
Q2 · Calculate

A 0.50 kg ball is whirled on a string of radius 0.80 m at 4.0 m·s⁻¹. Find the centripetal force. Check with the ball preset.

F꜀ = mv²/r = (0.50 × 4.0²)/0.80 = 8.0/0.80 = 10 N, along the string toward the hand.
Q3 · Predict

Double the speed at fixed radius. What happens to the centripetal force? Test it in the simulator.

Since F꜀ ∝ v², doubling the speed quadruples the force.
Q4 · Orbits

Jupiter is over 300 times more massive than Earth, yet it orbits the Sun more slowly. Use the planet preset and explain why.

Setting gravity equal to the centripetal force, GMm/r² = mv²/r, the planet's mass cancels: v = √(GM/r). Orbital speed depends only on the Sun's mass and the distance r. Jupiter is much farther out, so it moves slower — its own mass is irrelevant.
Q5 · Misconception

A student says an outward centrifugal force pulls a whirling ball away from the hand. What is wrong, and what happens if the string breaks?

In an inertial frame the only horizontal force is the inward tension. If the string breaks the ball flies off along the tangent in a straight line (Newton's first law), not radially outward. The "outward" feeling is inertia, not a real force.
Q6 · Data analysis

In the Data tab you plot F against v² at fixed r and m. What does the gradient represent?

From F = (m/r)·v², a graph of F against v² is a straight line through the origin with gradient m/r. Multiplying the gradient by r recovers the mass.
