Theme E.3 · Forces inside the nucleus

Nuclear Energy

A nucleus weighs less than the parts that build it. That missing mass, through E = mc², is the binding energy that holds it together — and the binding energy per nucleon is the single best measure of how stable any nucleus is.

Protons crammed into a nucleus repel each other fiercely, yet nuclei hold together. Something stronger must act over the tiny distances inside the nucleus — the strong nuclear force.

A nucleus holding itself together

Red nucleons are protons, grey are neutrons. Green links show the short-range strong attraction between close neighbours; red arrows are the long-range electric repulsion that every proton feels from every other proton.

Which force wins, and where

Evidence for the strong force, and why neutrons help

The strong nuclear force is attractive, acts between any two nucleons (proton or neutron), and dies away beyond about 3 fm. The electric force between protons is weaker nucleon-for-nucleon but reaches right across the whole nucleus. In a stable nucleus the short-range attraction exactly balances the long-range repulsion.

This is why heavier nuclei need proportionally more neutrons. Every proton repels every other proton no matter how far apart, but the strong force only pulls on near neighbours. Adding neutrons adds strong-force "glue" without adding to the repulsion, which is what keeps large nuclei bound.

Take a nucleus apart into its separate protons and neutrons and the loose pieces weigh more than the nucleus did. That difference is the mass defect, and it is the key to nuclear energy.

Assembling a nucleus

Watch the separate nucleons snap together. The bound nucleus has slightly less mass than the parts — the shortfall is carried away as binding energy when the nucleus forms.

Turning missing mass into energy

Binding energy is the energy you must pay

The binding energy of a nucleus is the energy needed to pull it completely apart into free nucleons — equally, it is the energy released when those nucleons come together. Using E = Δm c², a mass defect of just one unified atomic mass unit (1 u) is worth 931.5 MeV, an enormous amount compared with chemical reactions.

Dividing by the number of nucleons gives the binding energy per nucleon — the fairest measure of how tightly, on average, each nucleon is held. That single number, plotted against nucleon number, is the master curve of nuclear physics.

Plot binding energy per nucleon against nucleon number and a clear peak appears near iron. This single curve is the map of nuclear stability — the higher a nuclide sits, the more tightly each of its nucleons is held.

The binding energy per nucleon curve

Click anywhere on the curve to read that nuclide's binding energy per nucleon, or use the slider to jump to key examples. The higher a nuclide sits, the more tightly bound and stable it is — the peak around iron-56 (about 8.8 MeV per nucleon) is the most stable region of all.

Reading the curve

Why the curve is almost flat above A ≈ 60

The curve rises steeply for light nuclei, peaks near iron, then falls away slowly. Because the strong force is short-ranged, once a nucleus is large enough that each nucleon is surrounded by neighbours, adding more nucleons barely changes the binding energy per nucleon — so the curve is roughly constant above a nucleon number of about 60.

Both ends of the curve sit lower than the middle, so iron-56 is the most stable nuclide of all. A nucleus becomes more tightly bound by moving toward this peak — the idea that later underpins fusion (Theme E.5) and fission (Theme E.4), which you meet in the next topics.

Four original problems on mass defect, binding energy, and energy release. Work each one before revealing the solution.

Worked Example · Mass defect to binding energy
A helium-4 nucleus is made of 2 protons and 2 neutrons. Using m(proton) = 1.00728 u, m(neutron) = 1.00866 u and m(He-4 nucleus) = 4.00151 u, find the mass defect and the total binding energy. (1 u = 931.5 MeV/c²)

Mass of separate parts = 2(1.00728) + 2(1.00866) = 2.01456 + 2.01732 = 4.03188 u.

Δm = 4.03188 − 4.00151 = 0.03037 u
E = Δm × 931.5 = 0.03037 × 931.5 ≈ 28.3 MeV

Binding energy per nucleon = 28.3 / 4 ≈ 7.07 MeV, which places helium-4 well up the curve — unusually tightly bound for such a light nucleus.

Worked Example · Comparing stability
The total binding energy of helium-4 is 28.3 MeV and that of carbon-12 is 92.2 MeV. Which nucleus is more tightly bound, and by how much per nucleon?

Binding energy per nucleon = total binding energy ÷ nucleon number.

He-4: 28.3 / 4 = 7.08 MeV per nucleon
C-12: 92.2 / 12 = 7.68 MeV per nucleon

Carbon-12 is more tightly bound by about 0.6 MeV per nucleon, so it sits higher on the curve and is the more stable of the two.

Worked Example · Energy released in a decay
Polonium-210 decays by alpha emission: ²¹⁰₈₄Po → ²⁰⁶₈₂Pb + ⁴₂He. Using m(Po-210) = 209.98287 u, m(Pb-206) = 205.97447 u and m(He-4) = 4.00260 u, find the energy released. (1 u = 931.5 MeV/c²)

Total mass of the products = 205.97447 + 4.00260 = 209.97707 u.

Δm = 209.98287 − 209.97707 = 0.00580 u
E = Δm × 931.5 = 0.00580 × 931.5 ≈ 5.40 MeV

The lost mass reappears as the kinetic energy of the alpha particle and the recoiling lead nucleus — that is the energy released in the decay.

Worked Example · Mass to energy
In a reaction the total mass decreases by 0.21 u. How much energy is released, in joules? (1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹)

Convert the mass change to kilograms: Δm = 0.21 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ = 3.49 × 10⁻²⁸ kg.

E = Δm c² = 3.49 × 10⁻²⁸ × (3.0 × 10⁸)² ≈ 3.1 × 10⁻¹¹ J

Check against the shortcut: 0.21 u × 931.5 = 196 MeV, and 196 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹³ ≈ 3.1 × 10⁻¹¹ J. ✓ Both routes agree.

The ideas that trip students up on binding energy and nuclear energy. Each card pairs the wrong intuition with the correct physics.

"A high binding energy means an unstable nucleus."

It is the opposite. Binding energy is the energy you must supply to break the nucleus apart, so a higher binding energy per nucleon means a more tightly bound, more stable nucleus.

Higher binding energy per nucleon = more stable.
"The nucleus weighs the same as its protons and neutrons."

A bound nucleus always weighs less than its separate nucleons. The missing mass (the mass defect) is equivalent to the binding energy through E = mc².

Bound nucleus mass < sum of its parts.
"Binding energy is extra energy stored inside the nucleus."

It is the other way round. Binding energy is the energy you would have to put in to pull the nucleus apart; a bound nucleus sits at lower energy (and lower mass) than its separate nucleons.

Binding energy is the energy needed to separate the nucleons.
"Every stable nucleus has equal numbers of protons and neutrons."

Only light nuclei have N ≈ Z. As nuclei get heavier they need proportionally more neutrons, so the N/Z ratio of stable nuclei climbs to about 1.5 for the heaviest ones.

Heavy stable nuclei need more neutrons than protons.
"The strong force reaches across the whole nucleus."

The strong force is extremely short-ranged (about 3 fm). Only neighbouring nucleons feel it, whereas the electric repulsion between protons acts across the entire nucleus.

Strong force ≈ 3 fm; repulsion is long-range.
"Mass is destroyed and turned into energy."

Mass and energy are two measures of the same thing. The mass does not vanish; the system's rest mass decreases and the equivalent energy is carried away by the products and radiation.

Mass-energy is conserved, not destroyed.
