1.6 Momentum · Supplement

Mass in motion

Momentum tells you how hard it is to stop something. Collide and explode trolleys to see that total momentum is always conserved, drop eggs onto different surfaces to see how impulse and impact time decide the force, and practise with IGCSE-style questions.

01Momentum

DefinitionMomentum is defined as mass × velocity.
p = m v
p
momentum, in kilogram metres per second (kg m/s)
m
mass, in kilograms (kg)
v
velocity, in metres per second (m/s)
Rearranged forms
m = frac{p}{v}
v = frac{p}{m}

A heavy lorry moving slowly can have as much momentum as a light car moving fast. The more momentum something has, the harder it is to stop.

Example 1.1Calculate the momentum of a 1200 kg car moving at 15 m/s.
p = m v = 1200 × 15 => 18 000 kg m/s
Example 1.2A 0.16 kg cricket ball has a momentum of 5.6 kg m/s. Calculate its velocity.
v = frac{p}{m} = frac{5.6}{0.16} => 35 m/s

02Direction matters

Velocity has a direction, so momentum has a direction too: it is a vector. In one-dimensional problems, choose one direction as positive (usually to the right). Anything moving the other way has a negative velocity and a negative momentum.

Common mistakeForgetting the minus sign for an object moving the opposite way. Always draw an arrow and mark which direction is positive.

03Impulse

DefinitionImpulse is defined as force × the time for which the force acts. Impulse equals the change in momentum.
impulse = F Δt = Δ(m v)
F
force, in newtons (N)
Δt
time for which the force acts, in seconds (s)
Δ(mv)
change in momentum, in kg m/s
UnitsImpulse is measured in newton seconds (N s). Since impulse = change in momentum, 1 N s = 1 kg m/s.
Example 3.1A footballer kicks a 0.45 kg ball from rest. The foot exerts an average force of 900 N for 0.010 s. Calculate the impulse and the speed of the ball.
impulse = F Δt = 900 × 0.010 => 9.0 N s # impulse = change in momentum, and the ball starts at rest: 9.0 = 0.45 × v v = frac{9.0}{0.45} => 20 m/s
Example 3.2A 0.060 kg tennis ball hits a wall at 20 m/s and bounces straight back at 15 m/s. Calculate the impulse on the ball.
# Take the direction towards the wall as positive. initial momentum = 0.060 × (+20) = +1.2 kg m/s final momentum = 0.060 × (−15) = −0.90 kg m/s impulse = final − initial = −0.90 − (+1.2) => −2.1 N s # The size of the impulse is 2.1 N s, directed away from the wall.
Common mistakeFor a bounce, the change in velocity is NOT 20 − 15 = 5 m/s. Because the direction reverses, it is 20 + 15 = 35 m/s.

04Resultant force and momentum

DefinitionResultant force is defined as the change in momentum per unit time.
F = frac{Δp}{Δt}
F
resultant force (N)
Δp
change in momentum (kg m/s)
Δt
time taken (s)

This is Newton’s second law. If the mass is constant, Δp = mΔv, so:

F = frac{m Δv}{Δt} = m × frac{Δv}{Δt} = m a

The key idea: for the same change in momentum, a longer time means a smaller force.

Example 4.1A 70 kg passenger moving at 15 m/s is brought to rest by a seat belt in 0.50 s. Calculate the average force on the passenger.
Δp = 70 × 15 − 0 = 1050 kg m/s F = frac{Δp}{Δt} = frac{1050}{0.50} => 2100 N

05Conservation of momentum

Principle of conservation of momentumWhen no external resultant force acts on a system, the total momentum before an event is equal to the total momentum after it.
total momentum before = total momentum after m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2 = m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2

Here u is the velocity before and v the velocity after. Momentum is conserved in every collision and explosion, as long as no outside force (such as friction) acts.

Why?During a collision, A pushes on B and B pushes back on A with an equal and opposite force (Newton’s third law), for the same time. So the impulses are equal and opposite: whatever momentum A loses, B gains.

06Collisions

Example 6.1 · Objects stick togetherA 2.0 kg trolley moving at 3.0 m/s hits a stationary 1.0 kg trolley. They stick together. Calculate their velocity after the collision.
total momentum before = 2.0 × 3.0 + 1.0 × 0 = 6.0 kg m/s total momentum after = (2.0 + 1.0) × v 3.0 v = 6.0 => v = 2.0 m/s
Example 6.2 · Head-on collisionA 3.0 kg trolley moving at +4.0 m/s collides with a 1.0 kg trolley moving at −2.0 m/s. Afterwards the 3.0 kg trolley moves at +1.5 m/s. Calculate the velocity of the 1.0 kg trolley.
before = 3.0 × 4.0 + 1.0 × (−2.0) = 10 kg m/s after = 3.0 × 1.5 + 1.0 × v_2 10 = 4.5 + v_2 => v_2 = +5.5 m/s (to the right)
Beyond the syllabus, but usefulMomentum is always conserved, but kinetic energy usually is not: some is transferred to the thermal store and to sound. In Example 6.1, Ek falls from 9.0 J to 6.0 J. Try it in the Collision Lab.

07Explosions and recoil

If everything starts at rest, the total momentum before is zero, so the total momentum after must also be zero. The parts move off in opposite directions with equal-sized momenta.

Example 7.1A 4.0 kg rifle fires a 0.010 kg bullet at 400 m/s. Calculate the recoil velocity of the rifle.
total momentum before = 0 0 = 0.010 × 400 + 4.0 × v 4.0 v = −4.0 => v = −1.0 m/s (backwards)

The same idea explains rockets: hot gas is pushed out backwards, so the rocket gains equal momentum forwards.

08Safety: making the impact time longer

In a crash, the change in momentum is fixed (the person must go from moving to stopped). Using F = Δp ÷ Δt, increasing the time of the impact reduces the force, and so reduces injury.

FeatureHow it increases Δt
Crumple zonesThe front and back of the car crumple slowly in a crash, so the car takes longer to stop.
Seat beltsStretch slightly, so the passenger stops over a longer time.
AirbagsInflate and then slowly deflate as the head pushes into them.
HelmetsCrushable foam lining squashes on impact.
Crash mats, soft playground surfacesDeform as you land.
Bending your knees when you landYou stop over a longer time.
Full-mark answer“The airbag increases the time taken for the passenger’s momentum to change to zero. Since F = Δp/Δt and Δp is the same, the force on the passenger is smaller.”
