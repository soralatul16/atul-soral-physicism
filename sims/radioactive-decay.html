Reading the numbers

Stability is a balancing act between two forces inside the nucleus.

Isotopes and the nuclide symbol

An atom is written AZX, where Z is the proton number (which element it is) and A is the nucleon number (protons + neutrons). Two nuclides of the same element with different neutron counts are isotopes — same Z, different A. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are both carbon, but carbon-14 is unstable.

Two forces, pulling opposite ways

Protons repel each other electrostatically. Holding the nucleus together is the strong nuclear force — a powerful attraction between any two nucleons, but only over a very short range (about the width of one or two nucleons). A nucleus is stable only when these two effects balance.

Why the band bends (HL)

Light nuclei are stable near N ≈ Z. As Z grows, the long-range repulsion between protons builds up faster than the short-range strong force can compensate, so heavier stable nuclei need extra neutrons — the band drifts toward N ≈ 1.5 Z. Beyond Z = 83 no nucleus is fully stable: they all decay, most often by alpha emission.

Line vs band — read this. The dashed diagonal is only the reference N = Z. Stability is the green band: it sits on the line for light nuclei and lifts above it as Z rises. Uranium-238 (Z = 92, N = 146) has a sensible-looking N/Z ≈ 1.59, but it lies beyond where the band ends (Z > 83), so it is radioactive — that is why the marker turns red even though it looks "reasonable".

Ionising power vs penetration are opposites

An alpha particle is big and highly charged, so it rips electrons off almost every atom it passes — huge ionising power, but it loses its energy within a few centimetres. Gamma rays barely interact at all: weak ionisers, but they sail through metres of air and need lead to stop.

What this means for safety

Outside the body an alpha source is nearly harmless — skin stops it. Swallowed or inhaled, it is the most dangerous of the three, dumping all its energy into living tissue. Gamma is the reverse: dangerous from across the room because it penetrates so easily.

A positron (β⁺) travels through matter much like an electron — same tiny mass, same size of charge, so similar penetration. The difference comes at the end of its path: it meets an ordinary electron and the two annihilate, converting their mass into a pair of gamma photons.

Bending the beam

A mixed beam of α, β⁻ and γ enters a field. Charged radiations curve; the neutral one carries straight on. Switch the field on and see them split.

Why they separate

Charge decides the direction of the bend; charge, mass and speed decide how much.

In a magnetic field

A moving charge feels a force perpendicular to its motion, so it travels a circular arc of radius r = mv / (qB). Alpha and beta carry opposite charges, so they curve to opposite sides. Gamma has no charge and no force — it goes straight.

Why alpha barely bends

An alpha is about 8000 times heavier than a beta. Even though it has twice the charge and moves slower, the mass dominates: its radius is roughly 400× larger, so its arc is almost straight. The light, fast beta whips around a tight curve the other way.

In an electric field

Between charged plates each particle feels a steady sideways force, giving a parabolic path — like a projectile under gravity. Alpha drifts toward the negative plate, beta toward the positive plate, and again gamma passes straight through undeflected.

If a beam splits three ways in a magnetic field — one arc bending hard, a gentle arc the other way, and a straight line between them — you are looking at beta, alpha and gamma in one shot.

Where does the decay energy go? (HL)

Every decay releases a fixed amount of energy, Q. In alpha decay only one particle leaves, so it always carries the same energy — a single sharp line. In beta decay the energy is shared between the beta particle and a neutrino, so the beta can get anything from 0 up to Q. Worked examples

Fresh problems — try each before revealing. Use mₑ = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg, e = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C, h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00×10⁸ m s⁻¹.

Worked example 1

Radon-220 (Z = 86) decays by alpha emission. Write the balanced decay equation and name the type of change to the nucleus.

Alpha removes 2 protons and 2 neutrons: A → A − 4, Z → Z − 2.
22086Rn → 21684Po + 42α

Check: 220 = 216 + 4 ✓ and 86 = 84 + 2 ✓. The proton number changes, so this is a transmutation — a new element (polonium) is formed.

Worked example 2

A nuclide has 6 protons and 8 neutrons. Its neutron-to-proton ratio is high, so it decays by beta-minus emission. Write the equation and explain what happens to a nucleon.

A = 6 + 8 = 14, Z = 6, so the parent is carbon-14. In β⁻ decay a neutron turns into a proton, emitting an electron and an antineutrino: Z → Z + 1, A unchanged.

146C → 147N + 0−1β⁻ + ν̄

Check: 14 = 14 + 0 ✓ and 6 = 7 + (−1) ✓. Losing a neutron and gaining a proton lowers the N/Z ratio, moving the nucleus toward the stability band.

Worked example 3

A beta-minus particle travels at 1.5×10⁸ m s⁻¹ perpendicular to a magnetic field of 8.0 mT. Calculate the radius of its circular path.

r = mv / (qB)
r = (9.11×10⁻³¹ × 1.5×10⁸) / (1.60×10⁻¹⁹ × 8.0×10⁻³)
r = 1.37×10⁻²² / 1.28×10⁻²¹ = 0.11 m

An alpha particle in the same field would trace a radius roughly 400× larger — which is why its path looks almost straight beside the beta's tight curve.

Worked example 4

Nitrogen-13 has 7 protons and 6 neutrons — it is proton-rich. Predict its decay mode, write the equation, and calculate the energy of a gamma photon of wavelength 9.9×10⁻¹³ m that a daughter might emit.

Too few neutrons (N < Z) means the nucleus is below the stability band, so it decays by beta-plus: a proton becomes a neutron, Z → Z − 1.

137N → 136C + 0+1β⁺ + ν
E = hc / λ = (6.63×10⁻³⁴ × 3.00×10⁸) / 9.9×10⁻¹³ = 2.0×10⁻¹³ J (≈ 1.3 MeV)

Common misconceptions, cleared up

The traps that cost marks in nuclear physics. Each card pairs the wrong idea with the fix.

✕

"Alpha is the most dangerous because it penetrates the most."

✓

Alpha is the least penetrating — paper or skin stops it. It is dangerous only inside the body, where its intense ionising power damages tissue. Gamma penetrates most and is the external hazard.

✕

"Gamma emission changes the element."

✓

Gamma carries away only energy — A and Z are unchanged, so it is the same element, just in a lower energy state. Only alpha and beta emission cause transmutation.

✕

"Heating a source, or a chemical reaction, speeds up its decay."

✓

Radioactive decay is spontaneous and random. It is a property of the nucleus and is unaffected by temperature, pressure, or chemistry — unlike ordinary reactions.

✕

"The beta electron was sitting inside the nucleus."

✓

There are no electrons in the nucleus. The electron is created at the instant of decay, when a neutron converts into a proton (plus an antineutrino).

✕

"When a nucleus decays, the atom disappears."

✓

Nothing disappears — the nucleus transmutes into a different nuclide (the daughter), which is very much still there. Nucleons and charge are conserved.

✕

"Adding neutrons always makes a nucleus more stable."

✓

Only the right N/Z ratio is stable. Too many neutrons pushes a nucleus above the band → β⁻ decay; too few pushes it below → β⁺ decay. Stability is a balance, not "more neutrons".

✕

"Gamma can be deflected by a strong enough magnet."

✓

Gamma has no charge, so no magnetic or electric force acts on it. It passes straight through any field. Only the charged α and β are deflected.

Check your understanding

Original questions — attempt each before revealing.

Q1 · Equations

Thorium-230 (Z = 90) emits an alpha particle. Write the balanced equation and give the daughter's A and Z.

²³⁰₉₀Th → ²²⁶₈₈Ra + ⁴₂α. The daughter radium has A = 226, Z = 88 (A − 4, Z − 2). Check: 230 = 226 + 4 and 90 = 88 + 2.
Q2 · Identifying

In a magnetic field, three radiations enter together. One curves gently downward, one curves sharply upward, one is undeflected. Identify each and justify.

Undeflected = gamma (no charge). The two that curve go opposite ways because their charges are opposite: the sharp curve is beta (light, so small radius), the gentle curve is alpha (heavy, so large radius). Their directions are set by the signs −1 and +2.
Q3 · Penetration

A source's radiation passes through paper but is completely stopped by 3 mm of aluminium. What type is it, and what everyday material would also stop it?

It passes paper (so not alpha) but stops at a few mm of aluminium (so not gamma): it is beta. A few millimetres of aluminium, or a thin sheet of metal, absorbs it. Gamma would have required thick lead.
Q4 · Stability

Fluorine-18 has 9 protons and 9 neutrons, giving N/Z = 1, but it is proton-rich for its size and decays. Which mode do you expect, and what happens to Z?

For an element this light the stable ratio is close to N/Z ≈ 1.1, so F-18 sits just below the band → beta-plus decay. A proton becomes a neutron, so Z decreases by 1 (to oxygen-18), while A stays 18.
Q5 · Fields

Explain why an alpha particle is deflected far less than a beta particle in the same magnetic field.

The radius is r = mv/(qB). The alpha's mass is ~8000× the beta's; even with twice the charge and a lower speed, the large mass gives a much bigger radius (~400×). A bigger radius means a straighter, less-deflected path.
Q6 · Neutrino (HL)

Alpha particles from a given decay all have the same energy, but beta particles come out with a continuous range of energies. What did this difference reveal?

A two-body decay must give a fixed energy (as with alpha). The continuous beta spectrum meant energy was being shared with an unseen third particle — the (anti)neutrino — which carries the balance. This is how the neutrino was first inferred.
