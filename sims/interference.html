8.3 · Waves meet and combine

Interference

When two waves of the same type overlap, their displacements add. Where they arrive in step they reinforce; where they arrive out of step they cancel. From two coherent sources this builds a fixed pattern of bright and dark — and lets us measure the wavelength of light with a ruler.

AS Level · Core

When two waves of the same type meet, their displacements add at every instant — the principle of superposition. Whether they reinforce or cancel depends only on how they line up.

How two waves combine

Constructive & destructive — how they work

Constructive interference: the waves arrive in phase (in step). A crest lands on a crest, a trough on a trough, so the displacements add — the resultant has a larger amplitude (brighter light, louder sound, bigger ripple). This happens when one wave has travelled a whole number of wavelengths further than the other:

path difference = nλ

Destructive interference: the waves arrive exactly out of phase (antiphase). A crest lands on a trough, so the displacements subtract. With equal amplitudes they cancel completely — darkness, silence, flat water. This happens when the path difference is an odd number of half-wavelengths:

path difference = (n + 12)λ
n = 0, 1, 2, … is the order. Phase and path difference are linked by  phase difference = (2π / λ) × path difference: a whole-λ shift is 2π (back in step), a half-λ shift is π (exactly opposite).

The pattern from two sources

Reading the pattern

Two coherent sources S₁ and S₂ send out circular waves that overlap everywhere. Along some directions the path difference stays a whole number of wavelengths → antinodal lines of constructive interference (bright / loud).

Between them the path difference stays an odd number of half-wavelengths → nodal lines of destructive interference (dark / quiet).

Press Show nodal lines to mark them. Increase the separation d or shorten λ and the lines crowd closer together.

The whole pattern is stationary only because the two sources are coherent — their phase difference never changes.
AS Level · Core

A clear, steady pattern only appears under certain conditions. The key word is coherence. Switch the sources between coherent and incoherent and watch the pattern form or wash away.

Coherent vs incoherent sources

Coherent sources keep a constant phase difference, so the bright and dark fringes stay fixed — you see a clear, stationary pattern.

The terms, and the conditions

Interference — the superposition of two or more waves to give a resultant with points of steady reinforcement and cancellation.

Coherence — two sources are coherent if they have a constant phase difference. This requires them to have the same frequency (and hence wavelength).

To observe clear, steady fringes you need:

  • Coherent sources — a constant phase difference. In practice this means deriving both waves from a single source (double slit from one beam; two speakers from one signal generator).
  • The same frequency / wavelength.
  • Similar (ideally equal) amplitude — so the dark fringes are properly dark and the contrast is good.
  • The waves must overlap in the region and (for transverse waves such as light) be unpolarised or polarised the same way.
  • For light: the source must be monochromatic (single wavelength) for sharp fringes.

Two separate lamps can never give visible light fringes: their phase difference changes randomly millions of times a second, so any pattern averages out to uniform brightness.

Two-source interference is shown with every kind of wave — each with its own real setup. Pick one and change the source separation and wavelength; the maxima and minima move exactly as the path-difference rule predicts.

What you detect

Water — ripple tank

Two dippers on the same vibrating beam are coherent point sources. Their circular waves overlap to give fixed lines of calm water (nodal lines) between lines of large disturbance (antinodal lines).

Sound — two loudspeakers

Two speakers wired to one signal generator are coherent. Walk along a line in front of them and you pass through alternating loud (constructive) and quiet (destructive) points.

Microwaves — two gaps

Microwaves from one transmitter pass through two gaps in a metal sheet. A receiver moved along a line beyond the gaps shows a regular rise and fall in signal — maxima and minima.

Light — Young's double slit

Monochromatic light through two very close slits produces bright and dark fringes on a screen. Because light's wavelength is tiny, the slits must be close together and the screen far away — this is the experiment that measures λ (next tab).

AS Level · Core

Young's double-slit experiment turns the interference pattern into a measurement of wavelength. The fringe spacing x, the slit separation a and the slit-to-screen distance D give λ directly.

Fringes on the screen

The equation

λ = a·xD ⇒ x = λ·Da
Slit separation a0.50 mm
Distance to screen D1.50 m
Wavelength λ600 nm
Fringe spacing x1.80 mm
The bright fringes are evenly spaced. Narrower slits (smaller a), a longer wavelength, or a more distant screen all widen the fringes.
  • x = spacing between the centres of adjacent bright (or adjacent dark) fringes.
  • Valid when D >> a (small-angle approximation) — always true in practice.
  • Measure many fringes and divide, to reduce the error in x.
Derivation · where λ = ax/D comes from
1 · Set-up
Two slits S1, S2 a distance a apart are lit by monochromatic light. A screen is a distance D away, with D >> a. Consider a bright fringe at point P, a distance y from the central fringe, at angle θ from the slits.
2 · Path difference
The extra distance travelled by the wave from the far slit is the path difference = a sinθ.
3 · Condition for a bright fringe
A bright fringe of order n occurs when the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths:   a sinθ = nλ.
4 · Small-angle approximation
Because D >> a, the angle θ is tiny, so sinθ ≈ tanθ = yn / D. Substituting: a yn / D = nλ, so   yn = nλD / a.
5 · Fringe spacing
Adjacent bright fringes differ by one order, so the spacing x = yn+1 − yn = λD / a.
6 · Rearranged for λ
Rearranging gives the examinable form:   λ = ax / D. Measuring x, a and D yields the wavelength of light.
Practice · Exam-style
In a double-slit experiment, slits 0.40 mm apart are lit with light of wavelength 590 nm. The screen is 1.80 m away.
(a) Calculate the fringe spacing x. [2]
x = λD / a = (590 × 10−9 × 1.80) / (0.40 × 10−3) = 2.66 × 10−3 m ≈ 2.7 mm.
(b) State and explain what happens to x if the slit separation is halved. [2]
Since x = λD/a, x is inversely proportional to a. Halving a doubles the fringe spacing to about 5.3 mm.
(c) The red light is replaced by blue light. State the effect on the fringes. [1]
Blue light has a shorter wavelength, so x = λD/a is smaller — the fringes move closer together.
✓ That is a full-mark double-slit calculation.
AS Level · Core

Marks in interference come from the path-difference conditions, from stating the coherence requirements precisely, and from clean use of λ = ax/D with SI units. Work through these as an examiner marks them.

Worked Example 1
Two loudspeakers are connected to the same signal generator producing a note of frequency 1700 Hz. A student walking parallel to the speakers hears a series of loud and quiet points. Explain the quiet points, and find the path difference there. (speed of sound = 340 m s−1)

The speakers are driven by one generator, so they are coherent (constant phase difference, same frequency). Their sound waves overlap and superpose.

At a quiet point the waves arrive in antiphase and interfere destructively, so the path difference is an odd number of half-wavelengths:

λ = v / f = 340 / 1700 = 0.20 m
path difference = (n + ½)λ = ½λ,  1½λ, … = 0.10 m, 0.30 m, …

The nearest quiet point to the central loud point has a path difference of ½λ = 0.10 m.

Worked Example 2
In a Young's double-slit experiment, 10 bright fringes span a distance of 18 mm on a screen 2.0 m from slits separated by 0.65 mm. Calculate the wavelength of the light.

Fringe spacing from the measurement (10 fringes → 9 gaps, but at this level "10 fringes span 18 mm" is taken as 10 spacings unless stated; here use the spacing per fringe):

x = 18 mm / 10 = 1.8 mm = 1.8 × 10−3 m

Apply λ = ax / D:

λ = (0.65 × 10−3 × 1.8 × 10−3) / 2.0 = 5.9 × 10−7 m = 590 nm
Tip: measuring across many fringes and dividing reduces the percentage uncertainty in x. Watch the mm → m conversions.
Worked Example 3
A double-slit pattern is produced with red light. State and explain two separate changes that would each increase the fringe spacing.

Since x = λD / a, the fringe spacing increases if:

  • Move the screen further away (increase D): x ∝ D.
  • Use slits closer together (decrease a): x ∝ 1/a.
  • (Also valid: use a longer wavelength, e.g. deeper red: x ∝ λ.)

Any two, each with the correct reasoning from x = λD/a, score full marks.

AS Level · Core

The commonest ways candidates lose interference marks. Fix these now.

"Coherent just means the sources are identical."
Coherent = a constant phase difference (same frequency).

The phase difference between the sources must stay fixed in time. That is why both waves are taken from one original source.

"Two bright lamps will show interference fringes."
Independent lamps are incoherent — no fringes.

Their phase difference changes randomly and extremely fast, so any pattern averages out to uniform brightness. You must split one source.

"Interference destroys energy at the dark fringes."
Energy is redistributed, not lost.

The energy missing from the dark fringes reappears in the bright fringes. Total energy is conserved — it is just moved around.

"Constructive interference needs zero path difference."
Any whole number of wavelengths works.

Path difference = nλ (n = 0, 1, 2, …) all give constructive interference. n = 0 is just the central maximum.

"In λ = ax/D, x is the distance to the first fringe."
x is the spacing between adjacent fringes.

x is the gap from one bright fringe to the next. Measure across many fringes and divide to get it accurately.

"The fringe equation works for any slit-screen distance."
It needs D >> a (small-angle approximation).

λ = ax/D relies on sinθ ≈ tanθ. In real experiments D (metres) is far larger than a (sub-millimetre), so this holds.
