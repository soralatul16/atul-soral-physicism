A.4 · Higher Level

Angular momentum & rotational energy

Spin stores both momentum and energy. Meet angular momentum L = Iω and the law that keeps it constant, see a torque change it through angular impulse, and track the rotational kinetic energy ½Iω² — the rotational close of linear mechanics.

HL · Higher Level
Momentum, for spin

Straight-line momentum is p = mv — how much "motion" a moving mass carries. Its rotational twin is angular momentum L = Iω: the moment of inertia takes the place of mass, and angular velocity takes the place of velocity. A heavy, fast-spinning flywheel has a large L and is hard to stop; a light, slow one barely any.

Like its linear cousin, L is the quantity that bookkeeps rotation. Change it and you need a torque; leave it alone and it persists. Everything in this chapter — conservation, impulse, energy — is built on this one definition.

Build L = Iω

The disc spins at ω and carries moment of inertia I. The gauge shows their product L = Iω — the angular momentum. Raise either and L grows in exact proportion.

Derivation · L = Iω

Add up the ordinary momentum of every particle going round.

Step 1 · one particle
p = m v = m (r ω)
A particle a distance r from the axis moves at v = rω (Part 2), carrying linear momentum mv.
Step 2 · its angular momentum
ℓ = p r = (m r ω) r = m r² ω
Angular momentum of a particle is its momentum × distance from the axis — giving m r² ω.
Step 3 · add the whole body
L = Σ m r² ω = (Σ m r²) ω
Every particle shares ω, so it factors out of the sum.
Step 4 · recognise I
L = I ωunit kg m² s⁻¹
The sum Σmr² is the moment of inertia I. So L = Iω — the exact twin of p = mv.

The working

L = I ωp = mv for rotation
Question
A flywheel of moment of inertia 0.80 kg m² spins at 15 rad s⁻¹.
(a) Calculate its angular momentum. [2]
L = Iω = (0.80)(15) =12 kg m² s⁻¹
(b) A second identical flywheel spins at half the rate. State its angular momentum. [1]
Half the ω gives half the L: 6.0 kg m² s⁻¹, since L = Iω is linear in ω.
(c) State the linear quantity that L is the rotational analogue of. [1]
Linear momentum p = mv: I replaces m and ω replaces v.
HL · Higher Level
When nothing twists it, L is locked

If the resultant torque on a body is zero, its angular momentum cannot change: L = Iω stays constant. This is the rotational version of conservation of momentum, and it has a dramatic consequence — if the body changes shape so that I falls, ω must rise to keep the product fixed.

This is the figure-skater effect. Pull your arms in and your moment of inertia drops; with no external torque, your spin speeds up. Pull the slider below to draw the masses in and watch I fall, ω climb, and L hold rock-steady.

The skater effect

Angular momentum L is held constant (no external torque). As you pull the masses in, I = Icore + 2md² drops and ω = L / I shoots up — the spin accelerates even though nothing pushed it. Watch the L bar stay fixed while ω changes.

Derivation · I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂

Start from τ = ΔL/Δt and set the torque to zero.

Step 1 · torque changes L
τnet = ΔLΔt
Rewriting τ = Iα: the resultant torque equals the rate of change of angular momentum.
Step 2 · no resultant torque
τnet = 0 ⟹ ΔL = 0
If nothing twists the system, its angular momentum cannot change.
Step 3 · L is conserved
L = I ω = constant
The product Iω stays fixed, even if the body reshapes itself.
Step 4 · the trade-off
I₁ ω₁ = I₂ ω₂
Shrink I and ω must grow in proportion. Halve the moment of inertia and the spin rate doubles.

The working

I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂τ = 0
Question
A skater spins at 2.0 rad s⁻¹ with a moment of inertia of 4.5 kg m². She pulls her arms in, reducing it to 1.5 kg m².
(a) Calculate her new angular velocity. [2]
I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂ ⟹ ω₂ = (4.5)(2.0)1.5 =6.0 rad s⁻¹
Three times smaller I, three times faster spin.
(b) State the condition that makes this possible. [1]
There is no resultant external torque on her, so angular momentum L = Iω is conserved.
(c) Her angular momentum before was 9.0 kg m² s⁻¹. State its value after. [1]
Unchanged: 9.0 kg m² s⁻¹ (that is exactly what "conserved" means). Check: (1.5)(6.0) = 9.0. ✓
HL · Higher Level
A torque, applied for a while

To change angular momentum you need a torque acting over time. The product is the angular impulse ΔL = τΔt — the exact rotational twin of linear impulse FΔt = Δp. A big torque for a short burst, or a gentle torque held for longer, can deliver the same change in spin.

Because ΔL = Δ(Iω), with I fixed this becomes a change in angular velocity: Δω = τΔt / I. Set a torque and a time below, press Apply the kick, and watch the wheel speed up by exactly the predicted amount.

Deliver an angular impulse

The wheel (I = 2.0 kg m²) starts at rest. The angular impulse τΔt is deposited as a change in angular momentum ΔL, lifting its spin to ω = ΔL / I. Larger torque or longer push → larger ΔL.

Derivation · ΔL = τΔt

Rearrange Newton's second law for rotation over a time interval.

Step 1 · rotation's second law
τnet = ΔLΔt
The resultant torque equals the rate of change of angular momentum.
Step 2 · multiply by Δt
ΔL = τnet Δt
Torque × time is the angular impulse — the change it produces in L.
Step 3 · in terms of Iω
τ Δt = Δ(I ω) = I ωf − I ωi
Since L = Iω, the angular impulse is the change in Iω.
Step 4 · the change in spin
Δω = τ ΔtI
With I constant, the impulse translates directly into extra angular velocity. Compare FΔt = Δp.

The working

ΔL = τΔt = Δ(Iω)FΔt = Δp
Question
A constant torque of 5.0 N m acts on a wheel (I = 2.5 kg m²) for 4.0 s. The wheel starts from rest.
(a) Calculate the angular impulse delivered. [1]
ΔL = τΔt = (5.0)(4.0) =20 kg m² s⁻¹
(b) Hence find the final angular velocity. [2]
ΔL = Iωf ⟹ ωf = 202.5 =8.0 rad s⁻¹
(c) State the linear quantity angular impulse is analogous to. [1]
Linear impulse FΔt = Δp. Torque replaces force, and change in L replaces change in p.
HL · Higher Level
The energy of spin

A spinning body stores kinetic energy even if its centre of mass goes nowhere. Adding up ½mv² for every particle gives the rotational kinetic energy Eₖ = ½Iω² — the exact twin of ½mv². It can also be written in terms of angular momentum as Eₖ = L² / 2I, a form that is handy whenever L is conserved.

Notice the square: double the angular velocity and the stored energy quadruples. This is why flywheels are such effective energy stores, and why a fast spin is so much harder to bring to rest than a slow one.

Store energy in a spin

The gauge tracks Eₖ = ½Iω². Because of the square on ω, sliding the speed up fills the energy bar far faster than sliding I up — a reminder that spin energy grows with the square of angular velocity.

Derivation · Eₖ = ½Iω²

Sum the ordinary kinetic energy ½mv² of every particle.

Step 1 · one particle
E = ½ m v² = ½ m (r ω)²
Each particle moves at v = rω, so its kinetic energy is ½m(rω)².
Step 2 · add them up
Ek = Σ ½ m r² ω² = ½ (Σ m r²) ω²
The shared ω² factors out of the sum over all particles.
Step 3 · recognise I
Ek = ½ I ω²twin of ½mv²
The sum Σmr² is the moment of inertia I.
Step 4 · in terms of L
Ek = ½ I ω² = (Iω)²2I = L²2I
Substituting L = Iω gives the equivalent form Eₖ = L²/2I.

The working

Ek = ½Iω² = L²2I
Question
A flywheel has moment of inertia 0.50 kg m² and spins at 20 rad s⁻¹.
(a) Calculate its rotational kinetic energy. [2]
Ek = ½Iω² = ½(0.50)(20)² =100 J
(b) The spin rate is doubled. State the new kinetic energy. [1]
Energy ∝ ω², so doubling ω multiplies E by four: 400 J.
(c) Its angular momentum is 10 kg m² s⁻¹. Verify Eₖ using L²/2I. [2]
Ek = L²2I = (10)²2(0.50) =100 J
Matches part (a) — the two forms agree.
HL · Higher Level
Two bodies become one

When a spinning disc is suddenly coupled to a stationary one — think of two discs clamped together, or a lump of clay dropped onto a turntable — there is no external torque, so angular momentum is conserved: I₁ω₁ = (I₁ + I₂)ωf. The combined object spins slower because its moment of inertia has grown.

But kinetic energy is not conserved. The "rotational collision" is inelastic: friction between the surfaces dissipates energy as heat as they lock together. Press Engage and compare the energy before and after — L stays fixed, Eₖ drops.

Rotational collision

Disc 1 spins; disc 2 is at rest. On engaging, they lock and share a common ωf = I₁ω₁ / (I₁+I₂). Angular momentum is conserved; the drop in the energy bar is dissipated as heat.

Derivation · combined spin & energy loss

Conserve L through the coupling, then compare the energy.

Step 1 · conserve L
Lbefore = Lafter
No external torque acts during the brief coupling, so angular momentum is unchanged.
Step 2 · write both sides
I₁ ω₁ = (I₁ + I₂) ωf
Before: only disc 1 moves. After: both discs rotate together with combined inertia I₁+I₂.
Step 3 · common angular speed
ωf = I₁ ω₁I₁ + I₂
Adding inertia with the same L forces the spin to slow.
Step 4 · energy is lost
Eafter = L²2(I₁+I₂) < Ebefore = L²2I₁
Same L but larger I means less energy — the difference becomes heat. The collision is inelastic.

The working

I₁ω₁ = (I₁+I₂)ωf
Question
A disc of moment of inertia 3.0 kg m² spins at 8.0 rad s⁻¹. A stationary disc of 1.0 kg m² is dropped onto it and the two rotate together.
(a) Calculate the common angular velocity. [2]
ωf = I₁ω₁I₁+I₂ = (3.0)(8.0)4.0 =6.0 rad s⁻¹
(b) Calculate the kinetic energy before and after. [2]
Ei = ½(3.0)(8.0)² = 96 J
Ef = ½(4.0)(6.0)² = 72 J
(c) Account for the missing 24 J. [1]
It is dissipated as heat (and a little sound) by friction as the surfaces slip and lock. The collision is inelastic — only L is conserved, not Eₖ.
HL · Higher Level

Five exam-style problems spanning the whole chapter — definition, conservation, impulse, energy, and coupling. Try each before revealing the full solution.

Worked example 1 · L = Iω
A cylinder of moment of inertia 0.040 kg m² rotates at 50 rad s⁻¹. Find its angular momentum, and state the torque needed to keep it spinning at constant speed (ignore friction).
L = Iω = (0.040)(50) = 2.0 kg m² s⁻¹

At constant angular speed, ΔL = 0, so by τ = ΔL/Δt the resultant torque is zero. With no friction, no driving torque is required — the cylinder keeps its angular momentum indefinitely.

Worked example 2 · conservation
A playground roundabout (I = 120 kg m²) turns at 1.5 rad s⁻¹. A child of effective moment of inertia 30 kg m² steps onto the edge, bringing it to rest relative to the roundabout. Find the new angular velocity.

No external torque about the axis, so angular momentum is conserved. The child adds inertia but no initial spin:

I₁ω₁ = (I₁+I₂)ωf ⟹ ωf = (120)(1.5)120+30 = 180150 = 1.2 rad s⁻¹

The roundabout slows from 1.5 to 1.2 rad s⁻¹ as its moment of inertia grows.

Worked example 3 · angular impulse
A wheel (I = 0.60 kg m²) spinning at 12 rad s⁻¹ is braked to rest by a constant frictional torque in 3.0 s. Find the braking torque.
ΔL = Iωf − Iωi = (0.60)(0 − 12) = −7.2 kg m² s⁻¹
τ = ΔLΔt = −7.23.0 = −2.4 N m

The magnitude of the braking torque is 2.4 N m; the minus sign shows it opposes the rotation.

Worked example 4 · energy & conservation together
A skater of moment of inertia 5.0 kg m² spins at 3.0 rad s⁻¹. She pulls in to 2.0 kg m². Find her new spin rate, and show that her kinetic energy increases. Where does the extra energy come from?
ωf = I₁ω₁I₂ = (5.0)(3.0)2.0 = 7.5 rad s⁻¹
Ei = ½(5.0)(3.0)² = 22.5 J    Ef = ½(2.0)(7.5)² = 56.25 J

Energy rises by about 34 J. Angular momentum is conserved, but energy is not: the skater does work with her muscles pulling her arms inward against the outward (centripetal-demanding) pull, and that work becomes extra rotational KE. Note this is the reverse of the inelastic coupling case, where energy is lost.

Worked example 5 · rolling & energy share
A disc (I = ½MR²) of mass 2.0 kg and radius 0.10 m rolls without slipping at 4.0 m s⁻¹. Find its total kinetic energy and the fraction that is rotational.

Rolling without slipping gives ω = v/R. Total KE is translational plus rotational:

Etrans = ½Mv² = ½(2.0)(4.0)² = 16 J
Erot = ½Iω² = ½(½MR²)(v/R)² = ¼Mv² = 8.0 J
Etotal = 16 + 8.0 = 24 J

The rotational share is 8.0 / 24 = 1/3. For a uniform disc, one third of the rolling energy is in the spin — independent of radius and speed.

HL · Higher Level

The traps that cost marks in rotational dynamics. Each pairs the tempting wrong idea with the correct physics.

"If angular momentum is conserved, so is kinetic energy."

Conservation of L (no external torque) does not imply conservation of Eₖ. When I changes, Eₖ = L²/2I changes too.

Only L is fixed when τ = 0. Energy can rise (skater does work pulling in) or fall (inelastic coupling dissipates heat).
"A torque always speeds a body up."

A torque changes angular momentum in the direction it acts. A braking torque opposes the spin and reduces L.

τ = ΔL/Δt. The sign of the torque sets whether L grows or shrinks — a torque can slow, stop, or reverse rotation.
"Pulling your arms in gives you more angular momentum."

No external torque acts, so L is unchanged. What changes is the split between I and ω.

L = Iω stays constant. Reducing I forces ω up by the same factor — the product is fixed.
"Angular impulse is just torque."

Torque alone does not fix the change in L — the time it acts matters just as much.

Angular impulse = τΔt. A small torque over a long time can change L as much as a large torque briefly.
"Rotational KE is ½Iω, like L = Iω."

Energy carries a square and a half, exactly as ½mv² does — it is not linear in ω.

Eₖ = ½Iω². Doubling ω quadruples the energy, while L = Iω only doubles.
"Two spinning discs that lock together keep all their energy."

Coupling is a rotational inelastic collision. The surfaces slip as they lock, dissipating energy.

L is conserved so ωf = I₁ω₁/(I₁+I₂), but Eₖ drops — the loss appears as heat and sound.
