7.6 · Vibrations in one plane

Polarisation

Only transverse waves can be polarised. A polarising filter passes vibrations in just one plane; turn a second filter to an angle θ and Malus's law, I = I₀cos²θ, tells you exactly how much intensity gets through.

AS · Standard

Light from a lamp or the Sun is unpolarised: its electric field oscillates in every plane at once. A polarising filter only lets through the component of the field along its transmission axis, leaving a plane-polarised wave vibrating in a single plane.

Unpolarised in, polarised out

Left: unpolarised light — vibrations in many planes (end-on view). The filter passes only the component along its axis, so the emerging light vibrates in one plane and carries half the original intensity.

Only transverse waves polarise

Polarisation is only possible because a transverse wave has a direction of vibration perpendicular to its travel — so a filter can select one plane. A longitudinal wave vibrates along the direction of travel, with no such choice of plane, so it cannot be polarised.

Think of shaking a rope through a vertical slot: only the vertical vibrations get through. Turn the slot horizontal and nothing passes. A push–pull (longitudinal) wave would pass whatever the slot's orientation.

Passing unpolarised light through one ideal polariser halves its intensity to ½I₀ and leaves it plane-polarised along the transmission axis. That the trick works at all is direct evidence that light is a transverse wave.

A beam of unpolarised light of intensity I₀ is passed through a single ideal polarising filter.
Question
(a) State the intensity of the light transmitted by the filter. [1]
½I₀. Averaged over all the random vibration planes, exactly half the intensity passes.
(b) Describe the state of the light after the filter. [1]
It is plane-polarised, vibrating only in the plane of the filter's transmission axis.
(c) The experiment is repeated with a sound wave and a slotted barrier. State what is observed and why. [2]
The sound passes whatever the slot orientation, and is not polarised. Sound is longitudinal, vibrating along its direction of travel, so there is no plane for a filter to select.
AS · Standard

Send plane-polarised light of intensity I₀ into a second filter (the analyser) whose axis is at angle θ to the light's plane. Only the field component along the analyser's axis survives, and intensity depends on its square — Malus's law.

Turn the analyser

At θ = 0 the filters are aligned (full brightness); at θ = 90° they are crossed (darkness); at θ = 45° exactly half gets through.

Malus's law

I = I₀ cos²θ

Derivation · where cos²θ comes from

The analyser passes only the field component along its own axis. Reveal one line at a time.

Step 1 · resolve the amplitude
A′ = A cosθ
Only the component of the electric field amplitude A along the analyser's axis is transmitted.
Step 2 · intensity depends on amplitude²
I ∝ A′² = A²cos²θ
For any wave the intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude (from 7.1).
Step 3 · Malus's law
I = I₀ cos²θ
Since the incident intensity I₀ ∝ A², the transmitted intensity is I₀cos²θ.
Step 4 · the special angles
θ = 0 → I₀ · θ = 45° → ½I₀ · θ = 90° → 0
Aligned gives full transmission; crossed gives none; half-way gives half.
Plane-polarised light of intensity 12 W m⁻² is incident on an analyser.
Question
(a) Calculate the transmitted intensity when the analyser axis is at 60° to the plane of polarisation. [2]
I = I₀cos²θ = 12 × cos²60°=12 × (0.5)²=3.0 W m⁻²
(b) Determine the angle at which the transmitted intensity would be 6.0 W m⁻². [2]
cos²θ = 6.012 = 0.5⇒θ = cos⁻¹(√0.5)=45°
(c) State the transmitted intensity when the analyser is turned to 90°, and name this arrangement. [2]
Zero (cos 90° = 0). The filters are then said to be crossed.
AS · Standard

With several filters, apply Malus's law at each one, using the angle between its axis and the plane of the light reaching it. The famous surprise: two crossed filters block everything, yet slipping a third filter between them lets light reappear.

Three polarisers in a row

Filter 1 is fixed at 0°. Incident light is unpolarised with intensity I₀. Intensities are shown relative to I₀.

Intensity after each filter

Iₙ = Iₙ₋₁ cos²(Δθ)
Unpolarised light of intensity I₀ passes through three ideal polarisers. Filter 1 is vertical (0°), filter 2 is at 30° and filter 3 is at 90° (horizontal, crossed with filter 1).
Question
(a) State the intensity after filter 1. [1]
½I₀, plane-polarised vertically (unpolarised light through one filter halves).
(b) Calculate the intensity after filter 2. [2]
I₂ = ½I₀ cos²30°=½I₀ × 0.75=0.375 I₀
(c) Calculate the final intensity after filter 3, and comment. [3]
I₃ = I₂ cos²(90°−30°) = 0.375 I₀ × cos²60°=0.375 I₀ × 0.25=0.094 I₀
Although filters 1 and 3 alone are crossed (would give zero), the middle filter re-polarises the light at 30°, so some gets through.
AS · Standard

Four problems on polarisers and Malus's law. Try each before revealing the solution.

Worked Example · One polariser
Unpolarised light of intensity 20 W m⁻² passes through a single ideal polarising filter. Find the transmitted intensity.

A single filter on unpolarised light transmits half the intensity:

I = ½I₀ = ½ × 20=10 W m⁻²

The emerging light is now plane-polarised along the filter's axis.

Worked Example · Malus's law
Plane-polarised light of intensity 16 W m⁻² meets an analyser with its axis at 30° to the plane of polarisation. Find the transmitted intensity.
I = I₀cos²θ = 16 × cos²30° = 16 × 0.75=12 W m⁻²
Worked Example · Finding the angle
An analyser transmits one quarter of the incident plane-polarised intensity. Find the angle between the analyser axis and the plane of polarisation.
cos²θ = 0.25⇒cosθ = 0.5⇒θ = 60°
Worked Example · Three filters
Unpolarised light of intensity I₀ passes through three polarisers at 0°, 45° and 90° in turn. Find the final intensity.

After filter 1 (unpolarised → half): ½I₀, polarised at 0°.

after P2: ½I₀ cos²45° = ½I₀ × 0.5 = 0.25 I₀
after P3: 0.25 I₀ cos²(90°−45°) = 0.25 I₀ × 0.5=0.125 I₀

Without the middle filter, 0° and 90° are crossed and would transmit nothing — the 45° filter is what lets 1/8 of the light through.

AS · Standard

The traps on polarisation and Malus's law. Each card pairs the wrong idea with the correct physics.

"Any wave can be polarised."

Only transverse waves have a plane of vibration to select. Longitudinal waves, such as sound, cannot be polarised.

Polarisation ⇒ transverse wave.
"Malus's law is I = I₀ cosθ."

Intensity depends on the square of the amplitude, so it is I = I₀ cos²θ.

I = I₀ cos²θ.
"Every polariser halves the intensity."

Only the first filter acting on unpolarised light halves it. For already-polarised light the next filter follows cos²θ.

Half only for unpolarised → first filter.
"θ is the angle between the filter and the beam."

θ is the angle between the plane of polarisation of the light and the analyser's transmission axis.

θ = plane of light to analyser axis.
"Crossed polarisers always block all light."

Two crossed filters give zero — but a third filter placed between them, at an angle, lets some light through again.

A middle filter re-polarises; light reappears.
"At 45° the analyser blocks the light."

At 45°, cos²45° = 0.5, so exactly half the polarised intensity is transmitted — not zero.

θ = 45° → half transmitted.
