1 · How the marks actually work

MYP papers are marked strand by strand, and each criterion is scored on a level from 0 to 8. You reach the top band by showing the skill each strand names, using the command term the question gives you.

Command terms set the depth

  • State: one fact or value. "State the range of human hearing."
  • Describe: what happens, in detail, without the reason.
  • Explain: what happens and why, linked to particles, energy or the wave equation.
  • Calculate: equation, then substitution, then answer with unit.
  • Distinguish: make the difference explicit (A is louder because it has a larger amplitude).
  • Evaluate / Discuss: benefits and limitations, then a justified conclusion.

What a top-band (7–8) answer looks like

A 7–8
Explain and apply sound ideas to unfamiliar situations (sonar, seismology, music), and analyse information to make judgments.
B 7–8
Testable hypothesis with reasoning; explain how variables are changed and measured; a complete, safe method with sufficient data.
C 7–8
Correct tables and graphs, accurate interpretation, and evaluation of the hypothesis and method with explained improvements.
D 7–8
Explain an application, discuss and evaluate its implications, use precise scientific language, and document sources fully.

Do and don't in the exam hall

✔ DO

  • Say longitudinal, compressions and rarefactions, and say that particles vibrate parallel to the direction of travel.
  • Link pitch to frequency and loudness to amplitude every time.
  • In echo questions, halve the total distance: d = vt / 2.
  • Convert units first: ms → s, kHz → Hz, MHz → Hz.
  • On oscilloscopes, count divisions for one full cycle, then multiply by the time-base.
  • Quote numbers from the data in Criterion C.

✘ DON'T

  • Don't say sound can travel through a vacuum, or that it is an EM wave.
  • Don't say "the air travels to your ear". Energy travels; particles only vibrate.
  • Don't say louder sounds travel faster. Speed depends on the medium and temperature.
  • Don't read the period from a distance axis, or the wavelength from a time axis.
  • Don't write "human error". Say "reaction time of about 0.2 s".
  • Don't forget that ultrasound is sound (above 20 kHz), not radiation.

AKnowing & Understanding

An exam summary of the core ideas, with simulations and model answers. For the full chapter notes, open Waves → Sound.

What sound is

  • A longitudinal mechanical wave made by a vibrating object.
  • Particles vibrate parallel to the direction of travel, forming compressions (high pressure) and rarefactions (low pressure).
  • It needs a medium, so it cannot travel through a vacuum (bell-jar experiment).
  • It transfers energy, not matter.

Wave equations

v = f λspeed = frequency × wavelength
T = 1fperiod in s, frequency in Hz

Frequency is set by the source. When sound enters a new medium, v and λ change but f stays the same.

Speed of sound

MediumSpeed / m/s
Air (20 °C)≈ 343
Water≈ 1500
Soft tissue≈ 1540
Steel≈ 5000–6000

Solids are fastest because their particles are close and strongly bonded. In air, speed rises about 0.6 m/s per °C: v ≈ 331 + 0.6T.

Pitch, loudness, timbre

  • Pitch depends on frequency: higher f gives a higher note.
  • Loudness depends on amplitude: bigger amplitude, more energy, louder.
  • Timbre (quality) depends on the waveform shape, the mix of harmonics. It's why a flute and a sitar sound different on the same note.
  • Human hearing is 20 Hz – 20 kHz. Infrasound is below 20 Hz; ultrasound is above 20 kHz.

Echoes & ultrasound

d = v × t2the pulse travels there and back
  • Uses: sonar (sea depth, fish), prenatal scans, parking sensors, bats and dolphins, cleaning jewellery.
  • Medical ultrasound is non-ionising, so it is safe for unborn babies.

Reading an oscilloscope

T = divisions per cycle × time-base
f = 1T

Amplitude = height from the centre line to a peak, in divisions × Y-gain. More waves on the screen means higher pitch; taller waves mean louder.

Sound behaves like a wave

  • Reflection: echoes. Hard, flat surfaces reflect well; soft surfaces absorb (concert-hall panels).
  • Refraction: sound bends when its speed changes, e.g. at night it bends back towards the ground.
  • Diffraction: sound spreads round doorways because its wavelength is similar to the gap.
  • Interference: noise-cancelling headphones use antiphase waves.

Noise & hearing safety

  • Sound level is measured in decibels (dB). +10 dB = 10 × the intensity.
  • Long exposure above about 85 dB can damage the hair cells in the cochlea permanently.
  • Protection: reduce time, add distance, wear ear defenders, use quieter machines.

Weak answer vs top-band answer

Question: Explain why sound cannot travel through a vacuum but light can. [3]

1 / 3

Because there is no air in space.

True, but it doesn't explain why air matters, and it says nothing about light.
3 / 3

Sound is a mechanical wave: it travels when particles vibrate and pass the energy to neighbouring particles ✓. A vacuum has no particles, so the vibration cannot be passed on ✓. Light is an electromagnetic wave of oscillating electric and magnetic fields, so it needs no medium ✓.

Cause, mechanism, and the contrast that was asked for.

BInquiring & Designing

Common sound investigations: speed of sound by echo or two microphones, string length or tension against frequency, and distance against noise level. Learn one model plan and adapt it.

B i · Research question

Top band

How does the vibrating length of a nylon string (0.20–0.80 m), at a fixed tension, affect the frequency of the note it produces?

Then explain the problem: a shorter string has a shorter wavelength for its basic vibration (λ = 2L), so with v fixed the frequency rises.

B ii · Hypothesis

Top band

If the vibrating length is halved, then the frequency will double (f ∝ 1/L), because λ = 2L and the wave speed on the string depends only on tension and string type, so f = v/λ = v/2L.

B iii · Variables

B iv · Method & safety

  1. Set up the sonometer with a fixed hanging mass.
  2. Set L = 0.80 m between the bridges.
  3. Pluck at the centre and record f from the app (3 times).
  4. Repeat for 0.60, 0.40, 0.30, 0.20 m.
  5. Calculate 1/L and plot f against 1/L.

Safety

  • Keep feet clear of the hanging masses.
  • Wear eye protection in case the string snaps.
  • Keep volumes low when using tone generators.

Reducing timing error is a top-band idea for speed-of-sound plans: time 20 echoes, use a distance over 50 m, or use two microphones and a data logger instead of a stopwatch.

CProcessing & Evaluating

Turn raw data into the right graph, read gradients, and evaluate errors. Sound data often needs a transformation (1/L, or 2d) to give a straight line.

C i · Present

  • Units in the headers: Frequency f / Hz.
  • Add calculated columns (1/L, mean t, v = 2d/t).
  • Plot the IV on the x-axis. Choose axes that give a straight line.

C ii · Interpret

Top band

As L fell from 0.80 m to 0.20 m, f rose from 125 Hz to 500 Hz. The product f × L stayed at 100 Hz m, so f is inversely proportional to L. The graph of f against 1/L is a straight line through the origin with gradient 100 Hz m.

DReflecting on the impacts of science

Connect sound physics to real issues such as hearing loss, ocean noise, sonar and medical scans, then weigh the implications.

The four strands

  • D i: explain how sound science solves a problem (e.g. ultrasound scans).
  • D ii: discuss and evaluate implications: moral, economic, social, environmental, political, cultural.
  • D iii: precise language (frequency, not "speed of the note").
  • D iv: full, consistent references.

Top-band structure (S-I-E-C)

  1. Science: the physics of the application.
  2. Implications: at least three types.
  3. Evaluate: benefits and limitations.
  4. Conclude: "On balance… because…".

Good topics: noise-cancelling headphones, sonar and whales, prenatal ultrasound, hearing aids, noise at work, quiet electric cars.

Weak answer vs top-band answer

Question: Discuss the use of ultrasound for prenatal scans. [5]

2 / 5

Ultrasound is good because you can see the baby. It is safe.

No physics, no reason it is safe, and only one side.
5 / 5

Ultrasound pulses reflect at tissue boundaries, and the echo times (d = vt/2, v ≈ 1540 m/s) build an image ✓. It is non-ionising, so unlike X-rays it does not damage DNA ✓. Social: parents see the baby and problems are found early ✓. Economic: machines are cheaper than MRI and portable, but trained staff are needed ✓. Ethical: scans can be misused for sex selection, so some countries regulate them. On balance, highly beneficial when properly regulated ✓.

Science, several implications, a limitation, and a judgement.
