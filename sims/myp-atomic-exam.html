1 · How the marks actually work

Each criterion is scored from 0 to 8, strand by strand. In atomic physics most marks are lost by mixing up words (radiation and radioactive, irradiation and contamination, fission and fusion), unbalanced decay equations, and half-life answers with no working.

Command terms in this unit

  • Define: isotope, half-life, activity. Learn the exact wording.
  • Compare: α, β and γ side by side: nature, charge, mass, ionising power, penetration.
  • Describe: the stages in order (how a GM tube works, how a power station works).
  • Explain: observation → reason (most α particles go straight through → most of the atom is empty space).
  • Determine: daughter nuclei, numbers of α and β decays, half-lives read from graphs.
  • Justify: choose an isotope using radiation type and half-life, giving a reason for each.
  • Discuss / Evaluate: nuclear power, waste, medical uses: both sides, then a justified conclusion.

What a top-band (7–8) answer looks like

A 7–8
Apply ideas to unfamiliar isotopes and situations (choose a tracer, find the leak, date the rock) and solve multi-step problems (background correction → half-lives → activity).
B 7–8
Plan safe investigations: absorption by aluminium, the inverse-square law for γ, dice models of decay. Include background counts, long counting times, repeats and full safety.
C 7–8
Correct count rates for background, find half-lives from graphs more than once, explain random scatter, test relationships (C × d² constant), evaluate the reliability of random data.
D 7–8
Discuss nuclear power, waste, accidents and medical uses from several angles (economic, environmental, ethical, political), with real examples and references.

Do and don't in the exam hall

✔ DO

  • Balance equations: the top numbers (A) and the bottom numbers (Z) must add up on both sides.
  • Write α as ⁴₂He and β⁻ as ⁰₋₁e.
  • Subtract the background count before any half-life calculation.
  • Say “ionising radiation damages DNA”.
  • Use “half the unstable nuclei decay”, not “half the mass”.
  • Choose an isotope by radiation type + half-life, with a reason for each.

✘ DON'T

  • Don't say a β particle is an orbiting electron. It comes from the nucleus.
  • Don't say irradiated food becomes radioactive.
  • Don't say half-life changes with temperature or sample size.
  • Don't say that after two half-lives everything has gone. A quarter is left.
  • Don't mix up fission (splitting) and fusion (joining).
  • Don't say control rods slow neutrons. They absorb them; the moderator slows them.

AKnowing & Understanding

Everything examiners can ask in an MYP atomic-physics unit: the school list, plus nuclear stability, background radiation, irradiation and contamination, the inverse-square law, dating and E = mc² (extension).

Models of the atom

  • Dalton (1803): tiny, solid, indivisible spheres.
  • Thomson (1897): discovered the electron, giving the plum-pudding model (A).
  • Rutherford (1911): α-scattering showed a tiny, dense, positive nucleus with mostly empty space (B).
  • Bohr (1913): electrons in fixed energy levels (C). Chadwick (1932): the neutron.
  • Models change when new evidence cannot be explained by the old model.

  • A = nucleon (mass) number = protons + neutrons. Z = proton (atomic) number.
  • Neutrons: N = A − Z. Atom ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m; nucleus ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ m.

Isotopes & nuclear stability

  • Isotopes: the same number of protons, different numbers of neutrons (C-12, C-13, C-14). The chemistry is the same; the nuclear stability differs.
  • Light nuclei are stable with N ≈ Z; heavy nuclei need more neutrons than protons.
  • Too many neutrons → β⁻; too many protons → β⁺; too heavy (Z > 82) → α.

Detecting radiation

  • GM tube: radiation ionises the argon gas → an avalanche of ions → a pulse of current → one count on the counter.
  • Others: photographic film badges, the cloud chamber (shows tracks), the spark counter.
  • Count rate = counts ÷ time. Activity (Bq) = decays per second.
  • Background radiation: radon (the largest source), rocks, cosmic rays, food, medical scans. Always subtract it.

Decay equations & chains

  • α: A − 4, Z − 2. β⁻: A unchanged, Z + 1 (n → p + e⁻). β⁺: Z − 1 (p → n + e⁺). γ: no change, only energy.
  • Decay chain: one unstable daughter decays into the next until a stable nucleus is reached (U-238 → … → Pb-206).
  • Number of α decays = ΔA ÷ 4; then use Z to find the β decays.

Half-life

fraction left = 12nn = number of half-lives = t ÷ t½
  • Half-life: the time for half of the unstable nuclei (or the activity) to decay.
  • Decay is random and spontaneous: unaffected by temperature, pressure or chemistry.
  • Read the half-life from a graph more than once (800 → 400, then 400 → 200) and take the mean.

Fission, fusion & chain reactions

  • Fission: a slow neutron is absorbed by U-235 → it splits into two fragments + 2–3 neutrons + energy.
  • Chain reaction: these neutrons cause more fissions. Controlled in a reactor; uncontrolled in a bomb.
  • Fusion: light nuclei join (H → He in stars). Needs about 10⁷ K to overcome the repulsion between protons.
  • ★ Energy comes from lost mass: E = mc².

Nuclear power station

  • Fuel rods: enriched uranium. Moderator (water or graphite): slows neutrons.
  • Control rods (boron, cadmium): absorb neutrons, controlling the rate. Coolant: carries heat away.
  • Heat → steam → turbine → generator → electricity. Thick concrete shields against radiation.

Effects on living things & safety

  • Ionising radiation damages DNA → mutations → cancer. High doses: radiation sickness and burns.
  • Irradiation: exposed from outside, not made radioactive. Contamination: radioactive material on or in the body.
  • Outside the body, γ and β are most dangerous; inside, α is most dangerous.
  • Dose is measured in sieverts (Sv). Protect yourself with time, distance, shielding: tongs, lead boxes, film badges.

Weak answer vs top-band answer

Question: Justify which isotope should be used as a medical tracer: X (α, 6 h), Y (γ, 6 h), Z (γ, 25 years). [4]

1 / 4

Y, because gamma is the safest and it doesn’t last long.

The right choice, but no physics: why γ, and why 6 h rather than 25 years?
4 / 4

Y ✓. γ rays pass out of the body, so a camera outside can detect them; α would be absorbed inside and would ionise cells strongly ✓. A half-life of 6 h is long enough to complete the scan ✓, but short enough that the activity drops quickly, keeping the patient’s dose low; Z would keep irradiating the patient for years ✓.

Radiation type + reason, half-life + reason, and why the others are rejected.

BInquiring & Designing

Atomic-physics investigations use sealed sources, GM tubes, absorbers and models (dice, coins). Safety and background counts are always part of a top-band plan.

Investigations you could be asked to design

  • How does the thickness of aluminium affect the β count rate?
  • How does distance affect the γ count rate (inverse-square law)?
  • Which material best absorbs γ (lead, concrete, steel, water)?
  • The dice or coin model of decay: how does the probability affect the half-life?
  • Does background radiation vary with location or building material?
  • A protactinium generator (teacher demo): measuring a real half-life.

B i–ii · Research question & hypothesis

RQ

How does the distance between a cobalt-60 γ source and a GM tube (10, 15, 20, 25, 30 cm) affect the corrected count rate?

Hypothesis

If the distance doubles, then the corrected count rate will fall to a quarter, because the γ rays spread out over a sphere whose area is proportional to d², so the intensity is proportional to 1/d².

B iv · Safety: always include

  • Handle sealed sources with long tongs; never touch them.
  • Point the source away from people; keep the distance large and the time short.
  • Return the source to its lead-lined box straight after use; keep a signed log.
  • No eating or drinking; wash your hands afterwards.
  • Students under 16 often only watch teacher demonstrations: follow local rules.

Weak plan vs top-band plan

Task: Describe how you would make the count-rate readings accurate and safe. [4]

1 / 4

I would be careful and repeat the readings.

Vague: how? Nothing about background, counting time or specific safety steps.
4 / 4

Measure the background for 5 minutes and subtract it from each reading ✓. Count for 60 s (longer at low counts), because decay is random ✓. Repeat three times and calculate the mean ✓. Handle the source with tongs, point it away from people and return it to its lead box straight after use ✓.

Specific, linked to random decay, with real safety steps.

CProcessing & Evaluating

Radioactivity data is random, so top-band answers talk about scatter, counting time and repeats, and always correct for background first.

C i · Process the data

  • Mean count → corrected count = mean − background.
  • Half-life from a graph: read it at least twice and take the mean.
  • Test the inverse-square law: is C × d² constant?
  • Straight-line tricks: C against 1/d²; ★ ln C against t (gradient = −λ).

C ii–iii · Interpret & discuss the hypothesis

Top band

The corrected count fell from 580 to 25 counts per minute as the aluminium thickness rose from 0 to 5 mm, falling by about 35% for each extra millimetre: an exponential, not linear, decrease. This supports the hypothesis that β particles are gradually absorbed as they ionise aluminium atoms. The scatter in the repeats (±8) is what we expect from random decay.

DReflecting on the impacts of science

Few topics have bigger real-world impacts: cancer treatment, low-carbon electricity, nuclear weapons, accidents and waste that lasts for millennia.

The four strands

  • D i: explain how the science is applied (tracers, radiotherapy, smoke detectors, dating, power stations).
  • D ii: discuss and evaluate implications: economic, environmental, social, ethical, political, cultural.
  • D iii: precise terms: ionising, isotope, half-life, irradiation, contamination, fission, moderator.
  • D iv: references with in-text citations.

Good D topics for this unit

  • Is nuclear power the answer to climate change?
  • Storing radioactive waste for 10 000 years
  • Chernobyl and Fukushima: lessons and public fear
  • India’s thorium programme
  • Food irradiation: safe or scary?
  • Fair access to cancer radiotherapy
  • The radium girls and workplace safety
  • Hiroshima, Nagasaki and the ethics of scientists
  • Fusion: the energy of the future?

A structure for “Discuss / Evaluate”

  1. Explain the science in one or two sentences.
  2. Benefit with a real example and data.
  3. Risk or limitation with an example.
  4. Another angle: ethical, political, environmental, economic.
  5. Conclusion: “On balance… because…”.

Weak answer vs top-band answer

Question: Evaluate nuclear power as a way of reducing carbon emissions. [6]

2 / 6

Nuclear power doesn’t make CO₂ so it is good for climate change, but it can explode like Chernobyl.

One benefit and one risk, both undeveloped; no waste, cost or conclusion.
6 / 6

Fission of U-235 releases heat that produces steam to drive turbines, with almost no CO₂ while running ✓. Unlike solar and wind, it provides reliable 24-hour power ✓. However, spent fuel stays radioactive for thousands of years and needs deep geological storage ✓. Accidents such as Fukushima (2011) forced about 150 000 people to evacuate and damaged public trust ✓. Plants are also expensive and slow to build ✓. On balance, nuclear power is a valuable low-carbon partner to renewables, but only with strict safety regulation and a long-term waste plan ✓.

The science, two benefits, three drawbacks and a justified conclusion.

Decay rules

  • β⁻: A same, Z + 1 (n → p + e⁻)
  • β⁺: A same, Z − 1 (p → n + e⁺)
  • γ: no change; it removes extra energy
  • Check: the top numbers balance; the bottom numbers balance

Words to use exactly

  • Half the unstable nuclei decay
  • Random and spontaneous
  • Irradiated, not made radioactive
  • Control rods absorb; the moderator slows
