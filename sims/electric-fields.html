D.2 · Standard Level

Electric fields

Every charge reshapes the space around it. Measure that influence as field strength E = F/q, map it with field lines, build the uniform field between parallel plates, and balance a charged oil drop the way Millikan first weighed the electron's charge.

SL · Standard Level
What it means & why it matters

Electric field strength E is the force per unit positive charge at a point: E = F/q. It describes the influence a source charge has on the space around it, before any test charge is even placed there.

For a point charge the field obeys an inverse-square law, E = kQ/r², and it is a vector — it points away from positive charges and towards negative ones. Drag the sliders and watch both the strength and the direction respond.

Field of a point charge

The arrow at the test point shows the field there: longer where the field is stronger, pointing away from a positive Q and towards a negative Q. The lower graph is the inverse-square fall-off E = kQ/r².

Derivation · E = kQ / r²

Combine the definition of field strength with Coulomb's law.

Step 1 · definition
E = Fq
Field strength is the force per unit positive test charge q placed at the point.
Step 2 · Coulomb's law
F = kQ qr²
The force between the source Q and the test charge q a distance r away (Part 1).
Step 3 · substitute
E = 1q · kQ qr²
Divide the Coulomb force by the test charge q.
Step 4 · the test charge cancels
E = kQr²unit N C⁻¹
q cancels — proof the field belongs to the source and position alone, not to the test charge.

The working

E = kQr²k = 8.99×10⁹
Question
A small sphere carries a charge of +8.0 nC.
(a) Calculate the field strength 0.20 m from it. [2]
E = kQr² = (8.99×10⁹)(8.0×10⁻⁹)(0.20)² =1.8 × 10³ N C⁻¹
(b) State its direction. [1]
Radially away from the sphere, since the charge is positive.
(c) The distance is doubled to 0.40 m. State the new field strength. [1]
Inverse-square: double r divides E by four → 4.5 × 10² N C⁻¹.
SL · Standard Level
What it means & why it matters

Field lines are a map of the field. Their arrow shows the direction of the force on a positive charge, and their spacing shows the strength — crowded lines mean a strong field.

Choose a configuration below to see the pattern. Lines always leave positive charges and enter negative ones, they never cross, and between parallel plates they become straight and evenly spaced — a uniform field.

Field-line patterns

Switch between a single charge, a dipole, and parallel plates. Notice how the arrows and spacing change — and that lines never touch or cross.

Reading field lines

Direction. An arrow on a line gives the direction of the force a small positive charge would feel there — away from +, towards −.

Strength. Where lines bunch together the field is strong; where they spread apart it is weak. For a point charge they crowd near the charge, matching E = kQ/r².

Rules. Lines start on positive charge and end on negative charge (or at infinity), and never cross — the field has a single, definite direction at every point.

Uniform field. Between parallel plates the lines are straight, parallel and equally spaced: the field is the same everywhere between them.

Question
A student sketches the field around two point charges.
(a) State one rule that tells you field lines can never cross. [1]
At any point the field has a single direction; crossing lines would give two directions at once, which is impossible.
(b) How does the diagram show where the field is strongest? [1]
Where the lines are closest together (most crowded) — near the charges.
(c) Describe the field pattern between two oppositely charged parallel plates. [2]
Straight, parallel, equally spaced lines running from the positive to the negative plate — a uniform field (edge effects aside).
SL · Standard Level
What it means & why it matters

Two parallel plates with a voltage V across them, a distance d apart, create a uniform field — the same strength and direction everywhere between them: E = V/d.

This is how real devices (capacitors, ink-jet printers, oscilloscopes) steer charges with a known, constant force. Change V or d and watch the field respond, measured in V m⁻¹ (identical to N C⁻¹).

The uniform field

The arrows are straight, parallel and equal — the hallmark of a uniform field. Raising V or squeezing the plates closer (smaller d) packs the lines tighter and strengthens E = V/d.

Derivation · E = V / d

Move a charge across the gap and write the work two ways.

Step 1 · work from the field
W = F d = (q E) d
The uniform force on a charge is qE; moving it the full gap d does work qEd.
Step 2 · work from the voltage
W = q V
By definition of potential difference, moving charge q across voltage V does work qV.
Step 3 · equate
q E d = q V
Both expressions are the same work, so set them equal; the charge q cancels.
Step 4 · result
E = Vdunit V m⁻¹ = N C⁻¹
The field is the voltage divided by the plate separation — uniform throughout the gap.

The working

E = Vd
Question
Two parallel plates 2.0 cm apart have a potential difference of 600 V across them.
(a) Calculate the field strength between the plates. [2]
E = Vd = 6000.020 =3.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹
(b) An electron is released at the negative plate. State the energy, in eV, it gains at the positive plate. [1]
It crosses the full 600 V, so it gains 600 eV.
(c) Express that energy in joules. [2]
W = qV = (1.60×10⁻¹⁹)(600) =9.6 × 10⁻¹⁷ J
SL · Standard Level
What it means & why it matters

Millikan balanced a charged oil drop between parallel plates. When the upward electric force exactly cancels gravity, the drop hangs motionless: qE = mg.

Measuring the balancing voltage gives the drop's charge — and every drop turns out to carry a whole-number multiple of the elementary charge e. That is the evidence that charge is quantised.

Balance the oil drop

The drop is negative, so the upward electric force is qE = qV/d (d = 1.0 cm). Raise V until the drop floats — then qE = mg. The balancing voltage depends on how many electrons it carries.

Derivation · finding the charge

Set the two forces equal at balance.

Step 1 · forces balance
q E = m g
At rest the upward electric force equals the downward weight.
Step 2 · field between plates
E = Vd ⟹ qVd = m g
The field between the plates is uniform, E = V/d (Tab 3).
Step 3 · solve for q
q = m g dV
Rearrange for the charge measured from the balancing voltage V.
Step 4 · quantisation
q = n en = 1, 2, 3 …
Every drop's charge is a whole-number multiple of e = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C — charge is quantised.

Forces on the drop

q E = m g ⟹ q = m g dV
Question
An oil drop of weight 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ N hangs stationary between plates 1.0 cm apart with 500 V across them.
(a) State the condition on the forces at balance. [1]
The upward electric force equals the downward weight: qE = mg, i.e. qV/d = mg.
(b) Calculate the charge on the drop. [2]
q = mg·dV = (3.2×10⁻¹⁴)(0.010)500 =6.4 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
(c) How many excess electrons does it carry? [2]
n = qe = 6.4×10⁻¹⁹1.60×10⁻¹⁹ =4 electrons
A whole number — evidence that charge is quantised.
SL · Standard Level

Five problems across field strength, forces, parallel plates and the balanced oil drop. Try each before revealing the full solution.

Worked example 1 · Field of a point charge
Find the electric field strength 0.10 m from a +5.0 nC point charge in air.
E = kQr² = (8.99×10⁹)(5.0×10⁻⁹)(0.10)² ≈ 4.5 × 10³ N C⁻¹

Directed radially away from the charge, since Q is positive.

Worked example 2 · Force from a field
A charge of −3.0 nC sits at a point where the field strength is 2.0 × 10³ N C⁻¹. Find the force on it, and its direction relative to the field.
F = qE = (3.0×10⁻⁹)(2.0×10³) = 6.0 × 10⁻⁶ N

The magnitude is 6.0 µN. Because the charge is negative, the force points opposite to the field direction.

Worked example 3 · Parallel plates
Parallel plates are 4.0 cm apart with 1.2 kV across them. Find the field between them.
E = Vd = 12000.040 = 3.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹

Uniform everywhere between the plates (ignoring the edges).

Worked example 4 · Balanced oil drop
An oil drop of mass 3.3 × 10⁻¹⁵ kg is held stationary between plates 6.0 mm apart. It carries 2 excess electrons. Find the balancing voltage. (g = 9.8 m s⁻²)
q = 2e = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ C    mg = (3.3×10⁻¹⁵)(9.8) = 3.23 × 10⁻¹⁴ N
V = mg·dq = (3.23×10⁻¹⁴)(0.0060)3.2×10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 6.1 × 10² V
Worked example 5 · Superposition
Two equal +4.0 nC charges are fixed 0.20 m apart. Find the resultant field at the midpoint between them.

Each charge produces a field at the midpoint of equal size but pointing in opposite directions (each points away from its own charge).

Enet = E₁ − E₂ = 0

The resultant field at the midpoint is zero — the two contributions cancel by symmetry.

SL · Standard Level

The usual traps on electric fields. Each card pairs the tempting wrong idea with the correct physics.

"Field lines are real, physical threads."

They are a drawing tool. Their direction shows the force on a positive charge; their spacing shows the strength.

Lines are a model, not objects.
"A bigger test charge sits in a stronger field."

E = F/q is independent of the test charge. A bigger q feels a bigger force, but the ratio — the field strength — is unchanged.

E depends on the source and position only.
"Between parallel plates the field is stronger near a plate."

The field is uniform — the same strength and direction everywhere between the plates (apart from the edges).

Uniform field: E = V/d throughout.
"The field points the way a negative charge moves."

Field direction is the force on a positive test charge. A negative charge is pushed the opposite way.

E is defined for a positive test charge.
"N C⁻¹ and V m⁻¹ are different quantities."

They are the same unit for field strength. E = F/q gives N C⁻¹; E = V/d gives V m⁻¹; the two are identical.

N C⁻¹ = V m⁻¹.
"No test charge there means no field there."

The field exists in the space around a source whether or not a charge is placed in it — it is a property of the source.

The field is there regardless.
