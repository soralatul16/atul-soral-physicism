What is momentum?

Momentum measures how hard it is to stop a moving object — it depends on both its mass and its velocity, and it has direction.

Momentum = mass × velocity

p = m v

Momentum p (kg·m/s) is the “quantity of motion” an object carries. Its physical meaning: it measures how hard it is to stop something once it is moving — or how much effort it took to get it moving. The more momentum, the bigger the force (or the longer the time) needed to stop it.

It depends on both mass and velocity, and it is a vector — it points the way the object moves (a negative velocity points the other way).

Everyday examples

  • A loaded lorry and a car at the same speed: the lorry has far more momentum, so it needs a bigger braking force and a longer distance to stop.
  • An oil tanker moves slowly, but its enormous mass gives it huge momentum — it must cut its engines kilometres before the port.
  • A bullet has tiny mass but such high speed that its momentum is still large.
  • A parked car has zero momentum — no motion to stop.

Try it: doubling the mass or doubling the velocity doubles the momentum arrow.

Impulse = force × time

impulse = F Δt = Δ(mv)

A resultant force F acting for a time Δt gives an impulse, and the impulse equals the change in momentum.

The shaded area of the force–time graph is the impulse (N·s) — and that equals Δ(mv).

Conservation of momentum

m1u1 + m2u2 = m1v1 + m2v2

With no external force, the total momentum of the two trolleys is the same before and after the collision — even though kinetic energy may be lost.

Force = rate of change of momentum

F = Δp / Δt

Drop the egg. It hits with the same change in momentum Δp whatever it lands on — but a soft surface makes it stop over a longer time Δt, so the force is smaller and the egg survives. A hard floor stops it almost instantly → huge force → crack!

This is why cars have crumple zones and airbags, and why we bend our knees when we land.

Worked examples

1 · Momentum of a car

A car of mass 1500 kg travels at 20 m/s. Calculate its momentum.

Show answer ▾

p = m v = 1500 × 20 = 30 000 kg·m/s, in the direction of travel. (Momentum is a vector, so direction must be stated.)

2 · A collision (conservation of momentum)

A 2 kg trolley moving at 3 m/s hits a stationary 1 kg trolley and they stick together. Find their common velocity afterwards.

Show answer ▾

Total momentum before = (2 × 3) + (1 × 0) = 6 kg·m/s. After, the combined mass is 3 kg moving at v, so 3v = 6 → v = 2 m/s in the original direction. Momentum is conserved even though kinetic energy is not.

3 · Impulse and force (why we bend our knees)

A ball of momentum 6 kg·m/s is brought to rest. Find the average force if it stops in (a) 0.10 s on a hard floor, (b) 1.0 s on a cushion.

Show answer ▾

F = Δp ÷ Δt. (a) F = 6 ÷ 0.10 = 60 N. (b) F = 6 ÷ 1.0 = 6 N. The same change in momentum spread over a longer time gives a much smaller force — the principle behind cushions, airbags and crumple zones.

Common misconceptions

✗ Momentum and kinetic energy are the same

Momentum is p = mv (a vector); kinetic energy is ½mv² (a scalar). They are different quantities.

✓ Momentum has size and direction

Opposite directions take opposite signs — this matters in collisions.

✗ A heavier object always has more momentum

Only if velocities are equal. A light, fast object can out-momentum a heavy, slow one.

✓ It depends on mass and velocity

p = mv — both matter.

✗ Momentum is lost in a collision

With no external force, total momentum is conserved — even if kinetic energy is lost to heat and sound.

✓ Total momentum before = total after

Add the vectors (watch the signs).

✗ A bigger force always means a bigger impulse

Impulse is F × Δt — the time matters too. A small force over a long time can give a large impulse.

✓ Impulse = F Δt = change in momentum

Longer contact time → smaller force for the same Δp.
