Momentum & Impulse

Momentum, p = mv, is the "quantity of motion" a moving object carries. In any collision or explosion where no external force acts, the total momentum is conserved. A force acting for a time changes momentum — that change is the impulse, FΔt = Δ(mv). Launch carts into each other, watch the momentum before and after, and see why a longer contact time means a gentler force.

Conclusion

A loaded lorry is harder to stop than a bicycle at the same speed, and a fast cricket ball is harder to stop than a slow one. Both facts come down to momentum — mass times velocity.

Momentum

The momentum of an object is its mass multiplied by its velocity. It is a vector: its direction is the direction of motion, and in one dimension we show that with a + or − sign.

p=mv
p is momentum in kg·m·s⁻¹ (or N·s), m is mass (kg) and v is velocity (m·s⁻¹).

Impulse

A resultant force acting for a time changes an object's momentum. The impulse is the force multiplied by the time for which it acts, and it equals the change in momentum:

impulse=FΔt=Δ(mv)
Impulse is measured in newton-seconds (N·s), the same unit as momentum.

Resultant force and momentum

Rearranging the impulse equation gives one of the most powerful statements in mechanics: the resultant force equals the rate of change of momentum.

F=ΔpΔt
For a fixed change in momentum, spreading it over a longer time Δt gives a smaller force F.
Why airbags and crumple zones work. In a crash the passenger's momentum change Δp is fixed — they must be brought to rest. An airbag or crumple zone increases the time Δt over which that happens, so F = Δp/Δt is smaller and the force on the passenger is reduced. Try it in the Impulse & force set-up.

Conservation of momentum

When no external resultant force acts on a system, its total momentum stays constant. So for two objects in a collision or explosion:

m1u1+m2u2=m1v1+m2v2
Total momentum before = total momentum after. Velocities to the left are negative.
1

Elastic collision

Objects bounce apart. Both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved.

2

Inelastic collision

Objects stick together and move as one. Momentum is conserved, but some kinetic energy is transferred to other stores (heat, sound).

3

Explosion

Objects start together at rest and push apart. Total momentum stays zero, so they move in opposite directions — this is recoil.

In every case the momentum before equals the momentum after. Kinetic energy is only conserved in a perfectly elastic collision.

Q1 · Define

Define momentum and state its equation and unit.

Momentum is mass multiplied by velocity: p = mv. It is a vector quantity, measured in kg·m·s⁻¹ (equivalently N·s), with direction the same as the velocity.
Q2 · Calculate

A 1500 kg car travels at 20 m·s⁻¹. Calculate its momentum.

Use p = mv = 1500 × 20 = 30 000 kg·m·s⁻¹ (3.0 × 10⁴ N·s) in the direction of motion.
Q3 · Impulse

A force of 12 N acts on a trolley for 3.0 s. Find the impulse and the change in momentum.

Impulse = FΔt = 12 × 3.0 = 36 N·s. Since impulse equals the change in momentum, Δp = 36 kg·m·s⁻¹.
Q4 · Force

A 0.15 kg ball hits a wall at 8.0 m·s⁻¹ and stops in 0.020 s. Calculate the average force on the ball.

Δp = mΔv = 0.15 × 8.0 = 1.2 kg·m·s⁻¹. Then F = Δp/Δt = 1.2 / 0.020 = 60 N.
Q5 · Conservation

A 2.0 kg trolley moving at 3.0 m·s⁻¹ collides with a stationary 1.0 kg trolley and they stick together. Find their common velocity afterwards.

Momentum before = 2.0 × 3.0 + 1.0 × 0 = 6.0 kg·m·s⁻¹. After, they move together with total mass 3.0 kg: v = 6.0 / 3.0 = 2.0 m·s⁻¹. Check this with the inelastic set-up.
Q6 · Explain

Explain, using F = Δp/Δt, how an airbag reduces the force on a car passenger in a crash.

The passenger's change in momentum Δp is fixed — they are brought to rest either way. An airbag increases the time Δt over which they stop, so from F = Δp/Δt the average force F is smaller. A smaller force means less injury. See this in the Impulse & force set-up by increasing Δt.
