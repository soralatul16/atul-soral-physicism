Interactive Lab

What is the centre of gravity?

Every object behaves as if all of its weight acts at a single point. Find that point, and you can predict balancing, hanging and toppling.

The balance point

The centre of gravity (C of G) is the single point through which the whole weight of an object appears to act.

Rest the plank on the pivot. It only balances when the pivot sits directly under the C of G. Anywhere else and the heavier side swings down.

Adding mass shifts the C of G toward the heavy end — so the balance point moves too.

Finding the C of G of a lamina

A plane lamina is a thin flat sheet. To find its C of G:

1. Hang it freely from a hole near the edge.
2. Hang a plumb line (a string with a weight) from the same point and mark the vertical.
3. Repeat from a different hole.

The lines cross at the centre of gravity — because the C of G always hangs directly below the pivot.

Click a hole to hang the lamina from it.

Stability & toppling

Tilt the object. The weight still acts straight down from the C of G.

If the line of action stays inside the base → it falls back (stable). Once it passes the pivot edge → it topples over.

Tipping angle θc = tan⁻¹( (w/2) ÷ h ). A wider base and a lower C of G raise θc — you must tilt further to topple. Collect readings in the Investigate tab.

Worked examples

1 · Why a bus is designed low and wide

A double-decker bus is tested by tilting it on a platform. Explain, in terms of its centre of gravity, why passengers are not allowed to stand on the upper deck.

Show answer ▾

Standing passengers upstairs raise the centre of gravity. With a higher C of G, a smaller tilt angle is enough for the line of action of the weight to fall outside the wheelbase, so the bus topples more easily. Keeping mass low (seated, lower deck) keeps the C of G low and the bus stable.

2 · Centre of gravity outside the object

Where is the centre of gravity of a ring (a flat washer)? Can the C of G lie outside the material of an object?

Show answer ▾

Yes. The C of G of a ring is at its centre — in the hole, where there is no material at all. The C of G is simply the balance point of the weight distribution; it does not have to lie on the object. (Same reason a boomerang or an L-shape balances about a point in empty space.)

3 · The lamina experiment

A student finds the C of G of an irregular card by hanging it from one hole and marking the plumb line. Their teacher says one line is not enough. Why not — and how many holes are needed?

Show answer ▾

One plumb line only tells you the C of G lies somewhere along that line. You need at least two lines from two different holes; their intersection fixes the point. A third line is used as a check — it should pass through the same point.

Common misconceptions

✗ The C of G is always in the middle

Only for a uniform, symmetrical object. For an irregular or loaded object it shifts toward the heavier part.

✓ It is the balance point of the weight

It depends on how the mass is spread out, not on the shape's geometric centre.

✗ An object tips as soon as it is tilted

It only tips once the line of action of its weight passes beyond the pivoting edge of the base.

✓ Tilt returns while the weight line stays over the base

Up to that point the turning effect pulls it back down — it is stable.

✗ A heavier object is always more stable

Stability depends on base width and C of G height, not weight alone.

✓ Low C of G + wide base = stable

Racing cars are low and wide for exactly this reason.

✗ The C of G must be inside the object

It can lie in empty space — e.g. the hole of a ring or the gap of an L-shape.

✓ It is only a point where weight acts

Nothing needs to physically be there.
