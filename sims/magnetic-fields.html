D.2 · Standard Level

Magnetic fields

Magnets and currents both fill space with a magnetic field. See the looping pattern of a bar magnet, the circles around a current-carrying wire, the field through a coil, and the bar-magnet-like field of a solenoid — and use the right-hand rules to find their direction.

SL · Standard Level
What it means & why it matters

A magnetic field is the region where a magnetic force acts. Around a bar magnet it forms closed loops that leave the north pole and return to the south pole outside the magnet, continuing north again inside.

A small compass placed in the field lines up with it — that is exactly how we trace the pattern. Flip the poles below and watch every arrow reverse.

Field of a bar magnet

Field lines run N → S outside the magnet and are most crowded at the poles, where the field is strongest. The little compass needle always lines up along the local field direction.

Question
A student maps the field around a bar magnet with a plotting compass.
(a) State the direction a field line points outside the magnet. [1]
From the north pole to the south pole (the direction a compass N-pole points).
(b) Where is the field strongest, and how does the diagram show it? [2]
At the poles, shown by the field lines being closest together there.
(c) Explain why two field lines can never cross. [1]
The field has a single direction at each point; crossing lines would give two directions at once.
SL · Standard Level
What it means & why it matters

An electric current makes its own magnetic field. Around a long straight wire the field forms concentric circles centred on the wire — there are no poles.

The direction is given by the right-hand grip rule: point your right thumb along the current and your fingers curl the way the field circles. Reverse the current and the circles reverse too.

Field around a wire

The wire runs straight through the page: a dot means current flows out towards you, a cross means it flows away. The circles crowd nearer the wire, where the field B ∝ I / r is strongest.

The right-hand grip rule

Thumb along the current, fingers curl with the field. Point your right thumb in the direction the current flows; your curling fingers show the way the circular field lines point around the wire.

Current out of the page (dot): the field circles anticlockwise.

Current into the page (cross): the field circles clockwise.

Strength. The field weakens with distance and grows with current.

B = μ₀ I2π r

Why the pattern changes: B ∝ I, so a larger current adds more field lines (they get denser). B ∝ 1/r, so the circles sit close near the wire (strong) and spread apart farther out (weak) — the spacing is not uniform.

Question
A long straight vertical wire carries a current directly upward.
(a) Describe the shape of its magnetic field. [1]
Concentric circles in horizontal planes, centred on the wire.
(b) The current points out of the page. State the direction the field circles. [1]
Anticlockwise, by the right-hand grip rule.
(c) The current is doubled and you move twice as far away. State the overall effect on B. [2]
B ∝ I/r: doubling I doubles B, doubling r halves it — so B is unchanged.
SL · Standard Level
What it means & why it matters

Bend the wire into a loop and the circular fields of each part add together through the middle, giving a concentrated field along the axis. One face of the loop acts like a north pole, the other like a south.

Use the right-hand grip rule the other way round: curl your fingers along the current and your thumb points along the field through the centre — out of the north face.

Field through a coil

The coil is seen face-on: the arrows show the current running round the loop. A central dot means the field points out of the page (towards you), a cross means into it. Raise the current and more field threads the centre.

The axial field

Adding up. Every segment of the loop contributes a circular field; inside the loop these all point the same way, reinforcing into a strong axial field.

Poles. Field lines emerge from one face (north) and enter the other (south) — the loop behaves like a tiny magnet.

Right-hand rule. Curl the right-hand fingers along the current; the thumb points out of the north face, along the field through the centre.

Reversing. Reverse the current and the two faces swap: north becomes south.

B = μ₀ N I2 r

At the centre of the coil B ∝ I and ∝ N (number of turns), so more current or more turns means a stronger field — shown here by more field dots. A smaller radius r strengthens it too.

Question
A single circular loop carries a current.
(a) Describe the field at the centre of the loop. [1]
A straight, concentrated field directed along the axis, perpendicular to the plane of the loop.
(b) How would you find which face is the north pole? [2]
Curl the right-hand fingers along the current direction; the thumb points out of the north face (right-hand grip rule).
(c) The current is reversed. State the effect on the poles. [1]
The poles swap — the former north face becomes south and vice versa.
SL · Standard Level
What it means & why it matters

Stack many loops into a solenoid and their fields combine into a strong, uniform field along the inside — and the whole coil behaves exactly like a bar magnet, with a north pole at one end and a south at the other.

This is the basis of the electromagnet: switch the current off and the field vanishes; reverse it and the poles swap. Add turns and the field grows.

Field of a solenoid

Dots along the top and crosses along the bottom show the current circulating. Inside, the field arrows are straight, parallel and evenly spaced — uniform — then spread out at the ends like a bar magnet's poles.

Like a bar magnet

Inside. The field is strong and uniform, pointing straight along the axis from the south end to the north end inside the coil.

Outside. The lines loop from the north end round to the south end — identical to a bar magnet.

Finding the poles. Look at an end: if the current runs anticlockwise it is a north pole, clockwise a south pole.

Making it stronger. More turns, a larger current, or an iron core all increase the field.

B = μ₀ n In = turns per metre

Inside the solenoid B ∝ n and ∝ I, so adding turns or current adds more field lines. The inside lines are evenly spaced — that even spacing is what "uniform field" means; they only fan out and weaken at the ends.

Question
A solenoid is connected to a battery.
(a) Describe the field inside the solenoid. [1]
Strong and uniform, directed along the axis of the coil.
(b) The field outside resembles that of which object? [1]
A bar magnet — lines loop from the north end to the south end.
(c) State two ways to increase the field strength. [2]
Any two of: more turns, a larger current, or adding an iron core.
SL · Standard Level

Five short problems applying the field patterns and the right-hand rules. Try each before revealing the solution.

Worked example 1 · Grip rule
A current flows out of the page in a straight wire. Which way do the magnetic field circles point?

Point the right thumb out of the page (towards you); the fingers curl anticlockwise. So the field circles anticlockwise around the wire.

Worked example 2 · Solenoid poles
Looking at one end of a solenoid, the current runs clockwise. Is that end a north or a south pole?

Clockwise current viewed from an end makes that face a south pole (anticlockwise would make it north). A handy memory: the letters of S and N drawn with arrowheads show the circulation sense.

Worked example 3 · Compass near a wire
A plotting compass sits beneath a wire. When a large current is switched on, the needle swings. Explain why.

The current creates a circular magnetic field around the wire. Beneath the wire this field has a horizontal component that adds to the Earth's field, so the compass needle turns to line up with the resultant field instead of pointing north.

Worked example 4 · Comparing fields
How is the field pattern of a solenoid different from that of a single straight wire?

A straight wire gives concentric circles with no poles. A solenoid combines many loops into a uniform axial field inside and a bar-magnet pattern outside, with a distinct north and south pole.

Worked example 5 · Field strength
Where is a bar magnet's field strongest, and how is that shown on a field diagram?

At the poles. On a diagram this is shown by the field lines being most closely spaced there — crowded lines mean a strong field.

SL · Standard Level

The usual traps on magnetic fields. Each card pairs the tempting wrong idea with the correct physics.

"Field lines start at N and simply stop at S."

Magnetic field lines are continuous loops: N→S outside the magnet and S→N inside it.

They form closed loops with no ends.
"Only magnets make magnetic fields."

Any electric current produces a magnetic field — concentric circles around a wire, an axial field through a coil.

Moving charge (current) is a source of magnetism.
"A solenoid's field is strongest at its ends."

Inside a long solenoid the field is strong and uniform throughout; it only spreads and weakens at the open ends.

Uniform inside, weaker outside.
"Field lines can cross where the field is strong."

They never cross — the field has one definite direction at every point in space.

Crossing would mean two directions at once.
"Reversing the current leaves the field unchanged."

Reversing the current reverses the field everywhere, swapping the north and south poles of a coil or solenoid.

Reverse I → reverse B.
"A compass points to true (geographic) north."

It aligns with the local magnetic field, pointing to magnetic north — which differs from true north by the local declination.

It follows the magnetic field, not geography.
