Reading the coil's field

Through the centre the field is straight and perpendicular to the plane of the coil — this is where it is strongest.

Outside, the lines loop around from one face to the other, exactly like a bar magnet. One face acts as a north pole, the other as a south pole.

Reverse the current and the two faces swap: the field through the centre points the other way.

Question
A flat circular coil carries a steady current.
(a) Describe the field at the centre of the coil. [2]
Straight and perpendicular to the plane of the coil, passing through the centre; this is where the field is strongest.
(b) State what the coil's field resembles when viewed from a distance. [1]
That of a short bar magnet — one face is a north pole, the other a south pole.
(c) The current is reversed. State the effect on the field through the centre. [1]
It points in the opposite direction; the north and south faces swap.

Stack many coils into an air-core solenoid and the fields add to give a strong, almost uniform field inside, looping around the outside from one end to the other — just like a bar magnet.

Field of an air-core solenoid

Add turns to pack the interior field tighter; raise the current for faster flow. Each line is a closed loop. Drag the compass to probe. Reverse to swap the poles.

The solenoid grip rule

Curl your right fingers the way the conventional current flows around the coils; your thumb points to the solenoid's north pole.

Looking at one end: if the current appears to flow anticlockwise, that end is a north pole; clockwise means a south pole.

Inside, the field is strong and nearly uniform; outside, it matches a bar magnet.

Question
An air-core solenoid carries a current. Looking at its right-hand end, the current appears to flow clockwise.
(a) State the magnetic polarity of the right-hand end. [1]
A south pole (clockwise as viewed from that end).
(b) Describe the field inside the solenoid. [2]
Strong and almost uniform — the field lines are straight, parallel and evenly spaced, pointing from the S end towards the N end inside.
(c) State how the external field compares with that of a bar magnet. [1]
It is the same pattern — lines loop from the N end round to the S end.

Five sketching-and-interpretation tasks across the four required patterns. Decide your answer, then reveal it.

Task · Bar magnet
Describe the magnetic field pattern around a bar magnet.
Field lines run from the north pole to the south pole outside the magnet, forming continuous closed loops (S→N inside). They are closest together at the poles, where the field is strongest, and never cross.
Task · Straight wire
A vertical wire carries a current upwards. State the shape and direction of its magnetic field.
Concentric horizontal circles around the wire. By the right-hand grip rule (thumb up), the field is anticlockwise when viewed from above.
Task · Straight wire (into page)
A straight wire carries current into the page. State the direction of its field lines.
Clockwise circles (grip rule: thumb into the page → fingers curl clockwise).
Task · Solenoid
The current in a solenoid flows clockwise when viewed from the right-hand end. Which end is the north pole?
The right-hand end is a south pole (clockwise = south), so the left-hand end is the north pole. Right-hand grip rule: fingers follow the current, thumb points to N.
Task · Circular coil
Describe the field at and near the centre of a flat circular coil.
At the centre the field is straight and perpendicular to the plane of the coil, and strongest there. The coil's overall field looks like that of a short bar magnet, with a north face and a south face.

The usual traps on magnetic field patterns. Each card pairs the wrong idea with the correct physics.

"Field lines run from south to north outside the magnet."

Outside the magnet the lines run from north to south. They continue south-to-north inside to form closed loops.

Outside: N → S.
"Magnetic field lines start and stop, like electric ones."

Magnetic field lines have no beginning or end — they are always continuous closed loops.

Closed loops, no ends.
"The field of a straight wire points straight out (radial)."

The field forms concentric circles around the wire, not radial lines.

Concentric circles.
"Field lines can cross where two fields overlap."

The resultant field has one direction at each point, so magnetic field lines never cross.

Lines never intersect.
"The field is the same strength all around a magnet."

It is strongest where the lines are most crowded — at the poles — and weaker where they spread out.

Strongest at the poles.
"Use the left hand to find a wire's field direction."

Use the right-hand grip rule to get the field direction from the current. (The left hand is for the force on a current — that's D.3.)

Right-hand grip rule for field.
