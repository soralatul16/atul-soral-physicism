7.2 · Which way do the particles move?

Transverse & Longitudinal Waves

Both kinds of progressive wave carry energy by making particles oscillate. The difference is the direction of those oscillations relative to the wave's travel. Here you will compare the two and, crucially, learn to read and interpret their displacement graphs.

AS · Standard

In a transverse wave the particles oscillate at right angles to the direction in which the wave carries its energy. Watch the tracked particle move straight up and down while the crest travels sideways.

Oscillation perpendicular to travel

The red particle moves only along the vertical dashed line — perpendicular to the green arrow, which shows the direction of energy transfer.

What makes a wave transverse

The displacement of every particle is perpendicular to the direction of propagation. A crest (maximum positive displacement) and a trough (maximum negative displacement) travel along while each particle simply vibrates about its own equilibrium point.

Examples

Waves on a stretched rope or string, ripples on a water surface, waves on a stretched spring moved side to side, and all electromagnetic waves (light, radio, X-rays).

Because the oscillation has a definite direction in the plane perpendicular to travel, a transverse wave can be polarised — a property longitudinal waves do not share (explored fully in 7.6).

AS · Standard

In a longitudinal wave the particles oscillate parallel to the direction of travel. This produces regions where the medium is squeezed together (compressions) and pulled apart (rarefactions) that move along with the wave.

Oscillation parallel to travel

The red particle moves left and right along the direction of travel. Labels C and R mark a compression and a rarefaction — notice they travel along, but the particles do not.

What makes a wave longitudinal

The displacement of every particle is parallel to the direction of propagation. Where particles bunch up the medium is compressed (high pressure); where they spread out it is rarefied (low pressure). One wavelength is the distance from one compression to the next.

Examples

Sound in air, water or solids, a slinky pushed and pulled along its length, and the primary (P) seismic waves inside the Earth.

There is no single plane of vibration, so a longitudinal wave cannot be polarised.

AS · Standard

Driven by the same source at the same frequency, the two waves differ only in the direction the particles move. Everything else — amplitude, wavelength, period, speed, energy transfer — is defined in exactly the same way.

Same source, two behaviours

Top: a transverse wave (particles move ⊥). Bottom: a longitudinal wave (particles move ∥). Both share the same frequency and travel to the right.

AS · Standard

A longitudinal wave is hard to picture, so we represent it with a displacement graph — displacement along the direction of travel plotted against position. The exam skill is linking that graph back to where the compressions and rarefactions actually are.

Particles above, their graph below

Reading the graph

Take displacement to the right as positive. A particle at a compression or a rarefaction has zero displacement — it is the particles on either side that have moved towards or away from that point.

compression: s = 0, gradient −
rarefaction: s = 0, gradient +

Distance graph vs time graph — don't confuse them

A displacement–distance graph is a snapshot of the whole wave at one instant; the distance between repeating features is the wavelength λ. A displacement–time graph follows a single particle; the time between repeating features is the period T. Both can be drawn for transverse and longitudinal waves — the axis is still “displacement”, whatever direction that displacement physically points.

Question
The displacement–distance graph of a sound wave in air takes displacement to the right as positive. At point P the curve crosses the axis sloping downwards; at point Q, one quarter of a wavelength further on, the displacement is a maximum negative value.
(a) State whether P is at a compression or a rarefaction, and justify it. [2]
P is a compression. The displacement is zero with a negative gradient, so particles just before P are displaced right (+) and those just after are displaced left (−) — both moving towards P, crowding the medium.
(b) At point Q the displacement is a maximum. State the displacement of the medium's density there (maximum, minimum or normal). [1]
Normal (undisturbed) density. Maximum displacement occurs midway between a compression and a rarefaction, where the local spacing of particles is unchanged.
(c) The graph repeats every 0.80 m. State the wavelength and the distance from P to the next compression. [2]
Wavelength = 0.80 m (the repeat distance). The next compression is one whole wavelength from P, i.e. 0.80 m further on.
AS · Standard

Four problems on classifying waves and interpreting their graphs. Try each before revealing the solution.

Worked Example · Classifying waves
For each wave, state whether it is transverse or longitudinal: (i) a radio wave, (ii) sound in water, (iii) a P seismic wave, (iv) a ripple on a pond.

(i) Transverse — it is electromagnetic. (ii) Longitudinal — sound is always longitudinal. (iii) Longitudinal — P (primary) waves are compression waves. (iv) Transverse — the water surface moves up and down as the ripple travels outward.

Worked Example · Compare light and sound
State two differences and one similarity between a light wave and a sound wave.

Differences: light is transverse while sound is longitudinal; light can travel through a vacuum while sound needs a material medium (also acceptable: light can be polarised, sound cannot).

Similarity: both are progressive waves that transfer energy without transferring matter, and both obey v = fλ.

Worked Example · Reading a longitudinal graph
A displacement–distance graph of a sound wave (right = positive) shows two adjacent points where the curve crosses zero: at A the gradient is positive, at B the gradient is negative. Identify the compression and the rarefaction.

At B (zero displacement, negative gradient) particles either side move towards B — a compression. At A (zero displacement, positive gradient) particles either side move away from A — a rarefaction.

Worked Example · Distance graph or time graph?
A graph of a wave shows the displacement repeating every 4.0 ms. State which quantity this gives, and name the type of graph.

A repeat measured in time gives the period, T = 4.0 ms. The horizontal axis must be time, so it is a displacement–time graph of one particle (not a snapshot in space, which would give the wavelength).

AS · Standard

The traps examiners set on wave type and graph interpretation. Each card pairs the wrong idea with the correct physics.

"Longitudinal waves have crests and troughs."

Crests and troughs belong to transverse waves. Longitudinal waves have compressions and rarefactions.

Longitudinal → compressions & rarefactions.
"At a compression the displacement is largest."

Displacement is zero at the centre of a compression (and of a rarefaction). It is the pressure and density that peak there.

Compression centre: s = 0, pressure max.
"A displacement graph means the particles move up and down."

For a longitudinal wave the plotted displacement is along the travel direction. The upward axis is just a way to draw it, not the real motion.

The axis shows size & sign, not vertical motion.
"Sound can travel through a vacuum."

Longitudinal mechanical waves need particles to pass the disturbance on. In a vacuum there are none, so sound cannot travel.

Sound needs a medium; only EM crosses a vacuum.
"Transverse and longitudinal waves obey different wave equations."

Both are progressive waves and both satisfy v = fλ; amplitude, period and frequency mean the same for each.

Both obey v = fλ.
"Any wave can be polarised."

Only transverse waves can be polarised, because only they have a definite plane of vibration. Longitudinal waves cannot.

Only transverse waves polarise.
