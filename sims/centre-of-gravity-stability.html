Every object behaves as if its whole weight acts at a single point — its centre of gravity. Find that point and you can predict whether an object balances, rights itself, or topples over.

What is the centre of gravity?

The centre of gravity is the point through which the entire weight of an object appears to act. For a uniform, symmetrical object it lies at the geometric centre; for an irregular object it can be found by experiment. The weight of the object can always be drawn as a single downward force acting from this point.

W=mg
The whole weight W (in newtons) acts vertically downward from the centre of gravity; m is mass (kg) and g the gravitational field strength (N·kg⁻¹).

Finding it for an irregular lamina

A thin flat sheet is called a lamina. To find the centre of gravity of an irregular lamina:

1

Hang it freely

Suspend the lamina from a pin through a hole near its edge so it can swing freely. It settles with its centre of gravity vertically below the pin.

2

Drop a plumb line

Hang a plumb line (a mass on a thread) from the same pin and draw the vertical line it marks on the lamina.

3

Repeat and cross

Repeat from a different hole. The centre of gravity lies where the plumb lines cross. A third line is a useful check.

It works because a freely hanging object always comes to rest with its centre of gravity directly beneath the point of suspension — otherwise there would be a resultant moment turning it.

Centre of gravity and stability

When an object is tilted, its weight still acts straight down from the centre of gravity. Whether it rights itself or topples depends on where that line of action falls relative to the base:

↩

Line inside the base

The weight produces a moment that turns the object back onto its base — it is stable and returns.

↪

Line outside the base

Once the line of action passes beyond the pivoting edge, the moment turns the object further over — it topples.

So an object is more stable when it has a wider base and a lower centre of gravity: both let you tilt it through a larger angle before the line of action leaves the base. That is why racing cars are low and wide, and why a tall, narrow wardrobe tips easily.

Three types of equilibrium

S

Stable

A small push raises the centre of gravity; it returns to its original position (a cone resting on its base).

U

Unstable

A small push lowers the centre of gravity; it moves further away and topples (a cone balanced on its point).

N

Neutral

A push keeps the centre of gravity at the same height; it stays where it is put (a ball on a flat table).

Line of action. The single most useful idea here is the vertical line drawn down from the centre of gravity. Keep it inside the base and the object stays up; let it cross the edge and the object goes over.
Q1 · State

State what is meant by the centre of gravity of an object.

The centre of gravity is the point through which the whole weight of the object appears to act. The object's weight can be represented as a single downward force acting from this point.
Q2 · Experiment

Describe how you would find the centre of gravity of an irregularly shaped plane lamina.

Hang the lamina from a pin through a hole near its edge so it swings freely; it settles with its centre of gravity directly below the pin. Hang a plumb line from the same pin and mark the vertical line on the lamina. Repeat by hanging from a different hole and mark a second line. The centre of gravity is where the lines cross; a third line acts as a check. Try it with the plumb-line set-up in the simulator.
Q3 · Explain

Explain why a freely suspended object always comes to rest with its centre of gravity directly below the point of suspension.

If the centre of gravity were not directly below the pivot, its weight would act at a horizontal distance from the pivot, producing a resultant moment that turns the object. The object only stops turning when that distance is zero — that is, when the centre of gravity hangs directly below the pivot, so there is no resultant moment.
Q4 · Stability

A bus is more likely to topple than a sports car when going round a bend. Using the idea of centre of gravity, explain why.

A bus has a high centre of gravity (and often a relatively narrow base), so it only needs to tilt through a small angle before the vertical line of action of its weight falls outside its base — then it topples. A sports car has a low centre of gravity and a wide base, so it can tilt much further before that happens, making it more stable.
Q5 · Apply

Give two changes you could make to an object to make it more stable, and explain the effect of each.

(1) Make the base wider — the line of action of the weight can move further sideways before leaving the base, so a larger tilt is needed to topple it. (2) Lower the centre of gravity (e.g. put heavy parts at the bottom) — again a larger tilt is needed before the line of action passes the pivoting edge. Both increase the tipping angle. Confirm by changing base width and height in the stability set-up.
Q6 · Equilibrium

Name the three types of equilibrium and give an example of each.

Stable equilibrium — a small displacement raises the centre of gravity and the object returns (a cone on its base). Unstable equilibrium — a small displacement lowers the centre of gravity and the object topples (a cone balanced on its tip). Neutral equilibrium — the centre of gravity stays at the same height and the object stays put (a ball on a flat surface).
