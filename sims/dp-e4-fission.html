01Where is the energy stored? Binding energy and mass defect

Guiding question 1In which form is energy stored within the nucleus of the atom?

A nucleus holds together because the strong nuclear force between nucleons (protons and neutrons) is attractive and much stronger than the electric repulsion between protons, at distances of about 1 fm. Pulling a nucleus apart into separate nucleons needs energy. That energy is the binding energy.

DefinitionsMass defect Δm: the difference between the total mass of the separate nucleons and the mass of the nucleus.
Δm = Z m_{p} + (A - Z) m_{n} - m_{nucleus}
Binding energy EB: the energy needed to separate a nucleus completely into its individual nucleons (or the energy released when the nucleus forms from them).
E_{B} = Δm c^{2}
Answer to guiding question 1The energy is stored as nuclear potential energy of the nucleons, held together by the strong force. Because E = mc², a bound nucleus has less mass than its separate nucleons: the "missing" mass is the binding energy. When nucleons rearrange into more tightly bound nuclei (as in fission), some of this nuclear potential energy is released, and the total mass falls by Δm = E/c².
Units that save time1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 931.5 MeV c⁻². So a mass defect in u multiplied by 931.5 gives the energy directly in MeV. 1 MeV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹³ J.
Example 1.1The mass of a uranium-235 atom is 235.043930 u. The mass of a hydrogen-1 atom is 1.007825 u and of a neutron 1.008665 u. Determine the binding energy per nucleon of ^{235}_{92}U.
# Using atom masses: the 92 electrons in 92 H atoms cancel the 92 electrons of the U atom Δm = 92 × 1.007825 + 143 × 1.008665 - 235.043930 = 92.719900 + 144.239095 - 235.043930 = 1.915065 u E_{B} = 1.915065 × 931.5 = 1784 MeV => frac{E_{B}}{A} = 7.59 MeV per nucleon
Nucleus or atom?If the question gives nuclear masses, use the proton mass mp = 1.007276 u. If it gives atomic masses, use the hydrogen-atom mass 1.007825 u so the electron masses cancel. Mixing the two gives an error of about 0.5 MeV per proton.

02The binding energy per nucleon curve

Why fission releases energyA uranium nucleus has about 7.6 MeV per nucleon. The fragments it splits into (A ≈ 90–145) have about 8.3–8.6 MeV per nucleon. The products are more tightly bound, so the total binding energy increases by roughly 235 × 0.8 ≈ 200 MeV, and this energy is released. Moving towards the peak of the curve, from either side, releases energy.
Common confusion"Binding energy is released when a nucleus splits" is wrong. Binding energy is the energy you would have to supply to pull the nucleus apart. Fission releases energy because the products have more total binding energy than the original nucleus.

The curve falls for large A because the electric repulsion acts between all pairs of protons (it is long-range), while each nucleon is attracted by the strong force only to its near neighbours. Very large nuclei are therefore less tightly bound and can lower their energy by splitting.

03Spontaneous and neutron-induced fission

FissionThe splitting of a heavy nucleus into two smaller nuclei of roughly similar mass (the fission fragments), with the release of a few neutrons (usually 2 or 3, but anything from 0 to about 6; on average 2.4 for U-235) and about 200 MeV of energy.
Spontaneous fissionNeutron-induced fission
TriggerNone: the nucleus splits by itself, randomlyThe nucleus absorbs a neutron and becomes unstable
ExamplesCalifornium-252 (about 3% of its decays), uranium-238 (very rarely: about 1 in 2 million decays; it almost always α-decays)Uranium-235 and plutonium-239 with slow (thermal) neutrons; uranium-238 only with fast neutrons (above about 1 MeV)
UseCf-252 is a neutron source for starting reactors; it adds to background neutronsNuclear power and weapons

A typical neutron-induced fission of uranium-235:

^{1}_{0}n + ^{235}_{92}U → ^{236}_{92}U → ^{141}_{56}Ba + ^{92}_{36}Kr + 3 ^{1}_{0}n

The neutron is absorbed to form an excited compound nucleus, uranium-236. It vibrates, stretches into a dumbbell shape, and the electric repulsion between the two halves pushes them apart. There are many possible pairs of fragments (Ba + Kr, Xe + Sr, Cs + Rb, …); the products differ from one fission to the next.

Balancing fission equationsBoth the nucleon number A and the proton number Z are conserved. Count the neutrons released on the right!
A: 1 + 235 = 141 + 92 + 3 × 1 = 236 ✓   Z: 0 + 92 = 56 + 36 + 0 = 92 ✓
Example 3.1A uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits into xenon-140 (Z = 54) and strontium (Z = 38), releasing neutrons. The strontium nucleus has nucleon number 94. Determine the number of neutrons released.
# nucleon number before = after 1 + 235 = 140 + 94 + x => x = 2 neutrons # check Z: 92 = 54 + 38 ✓
Going further: why U-235 but not U-238?When U-235 captures a neutron, the binding energy of that neutron (about 6.5 MeV) is enough to push the U-236 nucleus over its fission barrier, so even a very slow neutron causes fission. When U-238 captures a neutron, only about 4.8 MeV is released, less than its barrier, so it needs a neutron with more than about 1 MeV of kinetic energy. See Fission → Why slow neutrons?

04Calculating the energy released

The energy released Q equals the decrease in mass multiplied by c². There are three equivalent methods; use whichever the data allow.

Method 1: from masses
Q = (m_{before} - m_{after}) c^{2}
Method 2: from binding energies
Q = (total E_{B} of products) - (total E_{B} of reactants)
Method 3: from binding energy per nucleon
Q = Σ (A × frac{E_{B}}{A})_{products} - Σ (A × frac{E_{B}}{A})_{reactants}
A free neutron has zero binding energy, so neutrons do not appear in methods 2 and 3.
Example 4.1 (from masses)Atomic masses: ^{235}U 235.043930 u, ^{141}Ba 140.914411 u, ^{92}Kr 91.926156 u, neutron 1.008665 u. Calculate the energy released when ^{235}U absorbs a neutron and splits into Ba-141, Kr-92 and 3 neutrons.
m_{before} = 235.043930 + 1.008665 = 236.052595 u m_{after} = 140.914411 + 91.926156 + 3 × 1.008665 = 235.866562 u Δm = 0.186033 u Q = 0.186033 × 931.5 => Q = 173 MeV = 2.78 × 10^{-11} J
Example 4.2 (from binding energy per nucleon)EB/A: U-235 7.59 MeV, Ba-141 8.33 MeV, Kr-92 8.51 MeV. Estimate the energy released in the same reaction.
Q = 141 × 8.33 + 92 × 8.51 - 235 × 7.59 = 1174.5 + 782.9 - 1783.7 => Q ≈ 174 MeV

The two methods agree (the small difference comes from rounding EB/A to 3 significant figures).

Example 4.3 (energy from 1 kg)On average each fission of U-235 releases 200 MeV. Calculate the energy released when 1.0 kg of U-235 undergoes fission.
N = frac{1000}{235} × 6.02 × 10^{23} = 2.56 × 10^{24} nuclei E = 2.56 × 10^{24} × 200 × 1.602 × 10^{-13} => E = 8.2 × 10^{13} J

Burning 1 kg of coal releases about 3 × 10⁷ J, so fission releases about 3 million times more energy per kilogram.

Precision mattersThe mass change is only about 0.08% of the total mass. Always keep at least 6 decimal places in the masses until the subtraction, or the answer will be meaningless.

05Where does the energy go?

Answer to the linking questionMost of the energy (over 80%) appears as the kinetic energy of the fragments. The two positively charged fragments repel each other electrically and fly apart; as they collide with surrounding atoms, this kinetic energy becomes internal energy of the fuel, which is why fuel rods get hot. The rest is carried by neutrons, γ-rays, and the β⁻ particles and antineutrinos from the radioactive products.
Decay heatAbout 7% of a reactor’s heat comes from the radioactive decay of fission products. This does not stop when the chain reaction stops, so a shut-down reactor must still be cooled for days. At Fukushima (2011) the reactors shut down correctly, but the tsunami disabled the cooling pumps and the decay heat melted the cores.

06Chain reactions

Each fission releases on average about 2.4 neutrons (the actual number varies from fission to fission, from 0 to about 6). If at least one of these causes another fission, the process continues by itself: a chain reaction.

Multiplication factor kThe average number of neutrons from one fission that go on to cause another fission.
  • k < 1 (subcritical): the reaction dies away.
  • k = 1 (critical): steady rate, as in a power reactor.
  • k > 1 (supercritical): the rate grows exponentially, as in a weapon, or briefly when a reactor’s power is raised.
After n generations the number of neutrons is N = N_{0} k^{n}.

Neutrons can be lost in three ways: they escape from the surface, they are absorbed without causing fission (by U-238, control rods, the moderator or structure), or they cause fission (useful).

Critical massThe minimum mass of fissile material needed for a sustained chain reaction (k = 1). In a small piece, too many neutrons escape through the surface. Since volume grows as r³ but surface area only as r², a bigger piece loses a smaller fraction of its neutrons. For a bare sphere of pure U-235 the critical mass is about 52 kg (about 17 cm across); for Pu-239 about 10 kg.
Example 6.1In a reactor k = 1.002 and the time between generations is 0.10 s (including delayed neutrons). Calculate the factor by which the power rises in 30 s.
n = frac{30}{0.10} = 300 generations factor = 1.002^{300} => factor ≈ 1.82
Going further: delayed neutronsAbout 0.65% of the neutrons from U-235 fission are emitted seconds later by decaying fission products. These delayed neutrons make the effective generation time about 0.1 s instead of 10⁻⁴ s, which gives control rods time to act. A reactor that is critical on prompt neutrons alone ("prompt critical") cannot be controlled: this happened at Chernobyl in 1986.
Why chain reactions do not happen on their ownNatural uranium does not undergo a chain reaction, for four connected reasons:
  • uranium-235 is only a small percentage (0.7%) of the uranium atoms;
  • neutrons are penetrating particles, and many simply pass out of the material without interacting with any nuclei;
  • to cause fission, the neutrons need to be travelling relatively slowly;
  • neutrons are uncharged, so some are absorbed (“captured”) by uranium-235 and especially uranium-238 nuclei without causing fission (the new nuclides usually emit γ-rays).
The first controlled chain reaction was achieved in 1942 at the University of Chicago (Chicago Pile-1).
Example 6.2Neutrons are produced throughout the volume of the fuel but escape through its surface. Calculate the ratio of volume to surface area for cubes of side 1.0 cm and 10 cm, and explain why a critical mass exists.
frac{V}{A} = frac{L^{3}}{6 L^{2}} = frac{L}{6} L = 1.0 cm: frac{V}{A} = 0.17 cm => L = 10 cm: frac{V}{A} = 1.7 cm

The larger cube has ten times more volume (where neutrons are made) for each square centimetre of surface (where they escape), so a smaller fraction of its neutrons is lost. Below the critical mass, k < 1. The critical mass of a bare sphere of pure U-235 is about 50 kg (a sphere about 17 cm across); it is larger for reactor fuel, which is only 3–5% U-235. See Fission → Step by step and Class activity.

07Moderators and enrichment

Neutrons are released fast, at about 2 MeV (about 2 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹). U-235 is far more likely to absorb a slow (thermal) neutron, of about 0.025 eV (about 2200 m s⁻¹, the speed of molecules at room temperature). A moderator slows the neutrons down by elastic collisions with its nuclei.

Why light nuclei make the best moderatorsIn an elastic collision, a neutron loses the most kinetic energy when it hits a nucleus of similar mass (think of snooker balls). A head-on collision with a stationary nucleus of nucleon number A leaves the neutron a fraction of its kinetic energy:
frac{E_{after}}{E_{before}} = (frac{A - 1}{A + 1})^{2}
Hydrogen (A = 1): 0 (all energy lost); deuterium (A = 2): 1/9; carbon (A = 12): 0.72.
ModeratorAverage collisions to thermaliseAbsorbs neutrons?Fuel needed
Light water H₂O≈ 18Some (H captures neutrons)Enriched (3–5% U-235)
Heavy water D₂O≈ 25Very littleNatural uranium works (CANDU, many Indian PHWRs)
Graphite C≈ 115Very littleNatural or slightly enriched (Magnox, RBMK)
Example 7.1A neutron makes a head-on elastic collision with a stationary carbon-12 nucleus. Calculate the fraction of its kinetic energy it loses. Estimate the number of such collisions needed to reduce 2.0 MeV to 0.025 eV.
frac{E_{after}}{E_{before}} = (frac{11}{13})^{2} = 0.716 => fraction lost = 0.28 0.716^{n} = frac{0.025}{2.0 × 10^{6}} = 1.25 × 10^{-8} => n = frac{ln(1.25 × 10^{-8})}{ln 0.716} ≈ 55

Most collisions are not head-on, so in practice about 115 are needed.

EnrichmentNatural uranium is 99.3% U-238 and only 0.7% U-235. U-238 absorbs many neutrons without fissioning, so most reactors use fuel enriched to 3–5% U-235. Weapons need about 90%. Neutron capture in U-238 produces plutonium-239 (via U-239 and Np-239), which is itself fissile.
Why are isotopes so hard to separate?U-235 and U-238 have the same number of protons, so their atoms have the same electrons and the same chemistry: no chemical reaction can separate them. The only difference is their mass, about 1.3% (3 nucleons in 235), and only 0.86% for the gas uranium hexafluoride (UF₆: 349 u and 352 u). Each stage of a gas centrifuge (or the older gaseous diffusion) enriches the gas only slightly, so many stages are joined in a cascade. The steps are: mining (ore usually less than 0.1% uranium) → milling to “yellowcake” (same isotope ratio) → conversion to UF₆ → enrichment → fuel pellets. The same cascade, with more stages, can reach 90% for weapons, which is why enrichment plants are inspected by the IAEA. Try Chain reaction → Fuel enrichment.
Example 7.2(Textbook questions 8 and 9.) Calculate the speed of a 1.0 MeV neutron, and of a thermal neutron in water at 300 °C, using Ek = (3/2)kBT.
v = sqrt{frac{2 E_{k}}{m}} = sqrt{frac{2 × 1.60 × 10^{-13}}{1.675 × 10^{-27}}} => v = 1.4 × 10^{7} m s^{-1} E_{k} = frac{3}{2} k_{B} T = 1.5 × 1.38 × 10^{-23} × 573 = 1.19 × 10^{-20} J => v = sqrt{frac{2 × 1.19 × 10^{-20}}{1.675 × 10^{-27}}} = 3.8 × 10^{3} m s^{-1}

The value 2200 m s⁻¹ often quoted for thermal neutrons uses E = kBT at 20 °C (the most probable speed); both describe neutrons moving at the speeds of the moderator’s molecules.

08The nuclear power plant

Guiding question 2How can the energy released from the nucleus be harnessed?
ComponentRoleTypical material
Fuel rodsContain the fissile fuel where fission takes placeUO₂ pellets (3–5% U-235) in zirconium-alloy tubes
ModeratorSlows fast neutrons to thermal speeds by elastic collisions, so they are more likely to cause fissionWater, heavy water, graphite
Control rodsAbsorb neutrons to control the rate of fission (keep k = 1). Pushed in: fewer neutrons, lower power. Fully in: emergency shutdown (a “scram”)Boron, cadmium, hafnium
CoolantCarries thermal energy away from the corePressurised water, CO₂ gas, liquid sodium
Heat exchangerTransfers thermal energy from the hot (radioactive) coolant to water in a separate circuit, which boils to make steam. The two circuits never mix, so the turbine stays non-radioactiveThousands of thin metal tubes
ShieldingAbsorbs neutrons and γ-rays so that workers and the environment are protectedThick steel vessel; concrete about 2 m thick; water
Moderator vs control rodsStudents often mix these up. The moderator slows neutrons down (making fission more likely). Control rods absorb neutrons (making fission less likely). Neither "absorbs energy" as its main job.
Built-in safety in water reactorsIf the water boils away, the moderator is lost, the neutrons stay fast, and the chain reaction slows by itself. Graphite-moderated reactors such as Chernobyl’s did not have this protection: losing cooling water there actually increased the power.
Energy transfers and the Sankey diagramThe energy changes in a nuclear power station (Figure E4.9 in the textbook) are: nuclear energy in the fuel → kinetic energy of the fission products → internal energy of the water and steam → kinetic energy of the steam → kinetic energy of the turbines and coil → electrical energy. At each stage some energy is wasted: antineutrinos escape (about 5%), some heat is lost from the reactor and pipes, by far the most is given to the condenser cooling water (about 60%), and some is lost to friction in the turbine and heating in the generator. The overall efficiency is about 33%. Draw and use the diagram in Power plant → Sankey diagram.
Safety issues and thermal meltdownWhen a reactor is shut down (SCRAM), the chain reaction stops within seconds, but the fission products keep decaying: this decay heat is about 7% of full power at first, about 1% after an hour and about 0.5% after a day. If the cooling fails, the water boils away and the core heats up. Above about 1200 °C the zirconium cladding reacts with steam to produce hydrogen; at about 2800 °C the fuel melts. This is a thermal meltdown, which can damage the reactor vessel and release radioactive material. Three Mile Island (1979), Chernobyl (1986) and Fukushima (2011, after a tsunami flooded the backup generators) are warnings, although other energy sources, such as coal mining, also have a long history of serious accidents. Try Power plant → Safety & accidents.
Nature of science: science as a shared endeavourAbout 32 countries produce roughly 10% of the world’s electricity from nuclear power. The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) in Vienna promotes the safe, peaceful use of nuclear technology, shares safety lessons after accidents, and inspects enrichment and reprocessing to check that no material is diverted to weapons.

09Rate of energy production

Linking questionHow is binding energy used to determine the rate of energy production in a nuclear power plant?

The binding energy curve tells us the energy per fission (about 200 MeV). The power of a reactor is then fixed by the number of fissions per second:

P = (fissions per second) × (energy per fission)
Example 9.1A reactor has a thermal power of 3000 MW. Each fission releases 200 MeV. Calculate (a) the number of fissions per second, (b) the mass of U-235 used per day, (c) the electrical output if the overall efficiency is 33%.
E per fission = 200 × 1.602 × 10^{-13} = 3.20 × 10^{-11} J => (a) rate = frac{3.0 × 10^{9}}{3.20 × 10^{-11}} = 9.4 × 10^{19} s^{-1} nuclei per day = 9.4 × 10^{19} × 86400 = 8.1 × 10^{24} => (b) mass = frac{8.1 × 10^{24}}{6.02 × 10^{23}} × 0.235 kg = 3.2 kg => (c) P_{elec} = 0.33 × 3000 = 990 MW

A coal station of the same output burns about 9000 tonnes of coal a day.

EfficiencyThe steam is at only about 300 °C, so the thermodynamic (Carnot) efficiency limit is low (B.4). Typical nuclear stations convert about 33% of the thermal energy to electrical energy; the rest is transferred to the cooling water or air.

Energy density of nuclear fuels

Energy density and specific energyEnergy density is the energy released per unit volume of fuel (J m⁻³). Specific energy is the energy released per unit mass (J kg⁻¹). They are linked by the density: energy density = specific energy × density. Nuclear fuels have an extremely high energy density.
Example 9.2(Worked example E4.2.) A typical fission of uranium-235 releases about 200 MeV. Determine (a) the energy that could be released by 1.00 kg of pure U-235, (b) its energy density (density 19.1 g cm⁻³), and (c) compare it with natural gas (54.0 MJ kg⁻¹).
N = frac{1000}{235} × 6.02 × 10^{23} = 2.56 × 10^{24} atoms E = 2.56 × 10^{24} × 200 × 10^{6} × 1.60 × 10^{-19} => (a) E = 8.20 × 10^{13} J => (b) energy density = 8.20 × 10^{13} × 1.91 × 10^{4} = 1.57 × 10^{18} J m^{-3} => (c) frac{8.20 × 10^{13}}{54.0 × 10^{6}} = 1.52 × 10^{6}

Per kilogram, uranium-235 releases over a million times more energy than natural gas. A 0.85 GW station with 33% efficiency uses about 1 tonne of U-235 a year (textbook question 17); compare the fuels in Fission & climate → Energy density.

Common mistakeDo not confuse energy density (energy ÷ volume, J m⁻³) with specific energy (energy ÷ mass, J kg⁻¹).

10The products of fission and their properties

A U-235 nucleus has N/Z = 143/92 ≈ 1.55. Stable nuclei with A ≈ 90–140 have N/Z of only about 1.2–1.4. The fragments keep roughly the original proportion of neutrons, so they are neutron-rich: they lie above the band of stability.

Properties of fission products
  • They are radioactive and decay by a chain of β⁻ decays (each turns a neutron into a proton), often with γ emission, until they reach a stable nucleus.
  • Many have short half-lives, so spent fuel has a very high activity and keeps producing heat.
  • Some have long half-lives: caesium-137 (30 years), strontium-90 (29 years), technetium-99 (211 000 years), iodine-129 (16 million years).
  • Neutron capture in U-238 also makes actinides such as plutonium-239 (24 100 years), which dominate the long-term hazard.
  • Some products (iodine-131, caesium-137, strontium-90) are dangerous if released, because the body absorbs them (thyroid, muscle, bone).
# Example decay chain (half-lives in brackets) ^{141}_{56}Ba (18 min) → ^{141}_{57}La (3.9 h) → ^{141}_{58}Ce (33 days) → ^{141}_{59}Pr (stable)

The products are of uneven sizes: the most likely masses are around A ≈ 95 and A ≈ 139 (a "double-humped" yield curve). Splitting into two equal halves is rare. See Products & waste → Fission products.

11Managing nuclear waste and long-term storage

TypeExamplesShare of volume / of radioactivityTreatment
Low-level (LLW)Gloves, clothing, tools, filters≈ 90% / ≈ 1%Compacted, stored in drums in shallow trenches
Intermediate-level (ILW)Fuel cladding, resins, reactor parts≈ 7% / ≈ 4%Set in concrete or bitumen, stored underground
High-level (HLW)Spent fuel, liquid from reprocessing≈ 3% / ≈ 95%Cooled in water ponds, then dry casks; vitrified (melted into glass); deep geological disposal
Stages for spent fuel
  1. Cooling ponds (about 5–10 years): water removes decay heat and shields the radiation.
  2. Dry cask storage (decades): steel and concrete containers, cooled by air.
  3. Optional reprocessing: uranium and plutonium are separated for reuse (France, India, Russia, Japan), reducing the volume of HLW.
  4. Deep geological repository: sealed in corrosion-resistant canisters, surrounded by clay, hundreds of metres down in stable rock. Finland’s Onkalo is the first of these.
Impact of long-term storage (guidance)High-level waste stays more radioactive than the uranium ore it came from for roughly 100 000 years. Issues to consider: guaranteeing containment over geological timescales (earthquakes, groundwater, climate change); warning people thousands of years in the future; security against theft of plutonium; the cost and who pays; fairness to future generations who get no benefit; and public opposition to sites ("not in my back yard"). On the other hand, the volume is small: all the spent fuel a large reactor makes in a year fits in a space about the size of a few cars.

Common exam mistakes
  • Saying binding energy is released in fission (the products have more binding energy).
  • Forgetting to count the neutrons when balancing A.
  • Rounding masses too early (keep 6 decimal places in u).
  • Mixing up the moderator (slows neutrons) and control rods (absorb neutrons).
  • Saying the moderator "slows down the reaction".
  • Thinking the heat exchanger cools the reactor down (its job is to make steam in a separate circuit).
  • Forgetting MeV → J (× 1.602 × 10⁻¹³) or u → kg.
  • Writing "the reaction becomes critical" to mean "dangerous": critical (k = 1) is the normal operating state.

Fission

  • Spontaneous (Cf-252) or neutron-induced (U-235, Pu-239)
  • n + U-235 → U-236 → 2 fragments + 2–3 n
  • Conserve A and Z
  • ≈ 200 MeV per fission
  • Mostly KE of fragments → heat

Energy released

  • Q = (mbefore − mafter)c²
  • Q = ΣEB(products) − ΣEB(reactants)
  • Keep 6 d.p. in u
  • 1 kg U-235 ≈ 8 × 10¹³ J
  • Energy density = specific energy × density (U-235 ≈ 10¹⁸ J m⁻³)

Chain reaction

  • k = neutrons going on to cause fission
  • k<1 dies, k=1 steady, k>1 grows
  • N = N0kn
  • Critical mass: surface/volume losses
  • Enrich to 3–5% U-235 (centrifuge cascade: same chemistry, tiny mass difference)

Power plant

  • Moderator: slows neutrons (elastic, light nuclei)
  • Control rods: absorb neutrons (B, Cd)
  • Coolant + heat exchanger → steam
  • Shielding: steel, concrete, water
  • P = fission rate × 3.2 × 10⁻¹¹ J
  • Sankey: about 33% electrical; most waste to the condenser
  • Decay heat: cooling needed after SCRAM (meltdown risk)

Products and waste

  • Neutron-rich → β⁻ decay chains
  • High activity, decay heat
  • Long-lived: Cs-137, Sr-90, Pu-239
  • Ponds → casks → repository
  • Hazard above ore for ~100 000 y

Top 8 mistakes to avoid

  1. Fission does not "release binding energy".
  2. Count the neutrons when balancing A.
  3. Keep 6 decimal places in u.
  4. Moderator slows, control rods absorb.
  5. Critical (k = 1) is normal, not dangerous.
  6. Heat exchanger keeps circuits separate.
  7. Use atomic masses with mH, nuclear with mp.
  8. Decay heat continues after shutdown.

What do subcritical, critical and supercritical mean?

Follow the neutrons from one fission. On average about 2.4 are released. Some escape, some are absorbed by U-238 or the control rods, and the rest cause new fissions. The multiplication factor k is the average number of neutrons from one fission that go on to cause another fission.

StatekWhat happensGraph aboveExample
Subcriticalk < 1Each generation has fewer fissions than the last, so the chain reaction dies out.falls to zeroControl rods fully in; a lump smaller than the critical mass; natural uranium with no moderator
Criticalk = 1Each fission causes exactly one more on average: a steady, self-sustaining rate.stays levelA power reactor running at constant output
Supercriticalk > 1Each generation has more fissions than the last: the rate grows exponentially.shoots upBriefly, when a reactor’s power is raised; in an uncontrolled way, a nuclear weapon
“Critical” does not mean dangerousIn everyday language “critical” means a crisis. In nuclear physics it simply means k = 1, the normal, steady working state of every power reactor. Engineers say a reactor “goes critical” when it starts up.
Critical massThe critical mass is the smallest mass of fissile material that can be critical (k = 1). A smaller piece is subcritical because too many neutrons escape through its surface. See the Critical mass sub-tab.

What is happening now?

From the mine to the fuel rod

  1. Mining: ore usually contains less than 0.1% uranium.
  2. Milling: the uranium is extracted as “yellowcake”, U₃O₈. The isotope ratio is still 99.3% : 0.7%.
  3. Conversion: into uranium hexafluoride, UF₆, which is a gas above 56 °C.
  4. Enrichment: centrifuges raise the U-235 fraction to 3–5%.
  5. Fuel fabrication: UO₂ pellets are packed into zirconium-alloy fuel rods.

Defence in depth

  • Fuel pellets (ceramic UO₂) hold most fission products.
  • Fuel cladding (zirconium alloy) seals the pellets.
  • Steel reactor vessel about 20 cm thick.
  • Concrete containment about 1 m thick: the shielding.
  • Several independent cooling systems, with backup diesel generators and batteries.
  • Negative feedback: in a water-moderated reactor, hotter water moderates less, so the power falls by itself.

UnderstandingEnergy density is the energy released per unit volume of fuel (J m⁻³). Specific energy is the energy released per unit mass (J kg⁻¹). Nuclear fuels have an extremely high energy density.

Common mistakeDo not confuse energy density (energy ÷ volume, J m⁻³) with specific energy (energy ÷ mass, J kg⁻¹). To change one into the other, multiply or divide by the density: energy density = specific energy × density.
