1 · How the marks actually work

E.5 can appear in every paper: quick ratio questions in Paper 1A, uncertainty and graph work in Paper 1B, and multi-step calculations with explanations in Paper 2. Most marks here are lost through unit conversions (pc, ly, AU, R☉, L☉), forgetting the powers in L ∝ R²T⁴, and vague explanations (“the star burns out”).

How a DP markscheme thinks

  • One mark per marking point (MP1, MP2…). “Max 4” means more points are listed than marks available.
  • Error carried forward (ECF): a wrong earlier value used correctly later still earns the later marks, so always finish the calculation.
  • “Show that”: the answer is printed, so the marks are for the steps. Give one more significant figure than the printed value.
  • OWTTE: “or words to that effect”. The idea counts, not exact wording. But the key physics term must be there.
  • Units and significant figures are only penalised where the notes say so. Give them anyway.

Command terms you will meet

  • State: a name or value, no explanation.
  • Outline: a brief account.
  • Describe: a detailed account (the stages, in order).
  • Explain: reasons or causes. Each “because” is usually a mark.
  • Calculate / Determine: working, then an answer with a unit.
  • Estimate: an approximate value; one or two significant figures.
  • Deduce: reach a conclusion from the data given, and say how.
  • Sketch: the right shape and labelled axes, not a precise plot.

Do and don't in the exam hall

✔ DO

  • Convert to SI before L = σAT⁴ and b = L/4πd²: pc → m, R☉ → m.
  • Use ratios when two stars are compared: L ∝ R²T⁴, b ∝ L/d².
  • Say “fusion”, “gravitational force” and “radiation pressure”, not “burning” and “heat pushing out”.
  • Read HR axes carefully: temperature increases to the left, both scales are logarithmic.
  • For mass defects, keep 6 decimal places of u until the last step.

✘ DON'T

  • Don't use A = πR²: the surface area of a star is 4πR².
  • Don't forget to square the distance in b = L/4πd².
  • Don't say a star’s colour tells you its size: colour gives temperature only.
  • Don't say iron fusion powers a supernova: fusing iron absorbs energy.
  • Don't treat parallax as the full angular shift: p is half of it.

1APaper 1A: multiple choice

About 2 minutes per question. E.5 multiple-choice questions are mostly ratio questions and HR-diagram questions. Never calculate an absolute value if a ratio will do.

The ratio method

Write the equation, cancel everything that is the same, then put in the factors.

L ∝ R² T⁴b ∝ L / d²R ∝ √L / T²T ∝ 1 / λmax

Example: twice the radius and half the temperature → L × 2² × (½)⁴ = L/4.

Traps the wrong options use

  • Forgetting the square (2 instead of 4) or the fourth power (2 instead of 16).
  • Using distance instead of distance² for brightness.
  • Confusing the full parallax shift with p.
  • Swapping “top left” and “top right” on the HR diagram.
  • “Iron fusion releases energy” and “heavy elements form in main-sequence stars”.

Worked example

Star X has 4 times the luminosity of star Y and the same surface temperature. Star X is 2 times as far from Earth. What is bX/bY, and how do their radii compare?

  1. b ∝ L/d², so bX/bY = 4 / 2² = 1: they look equally bright.
  2. Same T, so L ∝ R²: RX/RY = √4 = 2.
  3. Check the options for the traps: 2 (forgot d²), 16 (squared L), ½ (inverted).

1BPaper 1B: data-based questions

20 marks, about 40 minutes. You are given data from a real measurement and asked to process it: gradients, log graphs, uncertainties, and whether a result agrees with an accepted value.

Uncertainty rules for E.5

y = a b or a/b → add fractional uncertainties y = aⁿ → n × fractional uncertainty of a
  • d = 1/p: same fractional uncertainty as p.
  • L = 4πd²b: Δb/b + 2Δd/d.
  • R = √(L/4πσT⁴): ½ΔL/L + 2ΔT/T.
  • T = 2.9 × 10⁻³/λmax: same fractional uncertainty as λmax.

Log graphs

If L = kMⁿ, then lg L = n lg M + lg k. A graph of lg L against lg M is a straight line with gradient n.

  • Log values have no unit. Label axes “lg (L / L☉)”.
  • Read the gradient from the line, using a large triangle, not from two data points.
  • Logs are used because the data cover several orders of magnitude.

“Does it agree?”

  1. Calculate the value and its absolute uncertainty.
  2. Write the range: (40.0 ± 1.6) pc means 38.4 to 41.6 pc.
  3. The result agrees only if the accepted value lies inside that range. Say so explicitly.

Worked example: which measurement matters most?

L = (2.0 ± 0.2) × 10²⁸ W and T = (8000 ± 400) K. Determine the percentage uncertainty in R.

  1. ΔL/L = 10% and ΔT/T = 5%.
  2. R ∝ L½ T−2, so ΔR/R = ½(10%) + 2(5%) = 5% + 10% = 15%.
  3. The temperature contributes twice as much, even though its own uncertainty is smaller. Improve the temperature measurement first.

2Paper 2: structured and extended answers

Most E.5 marks are here. Calculations are multi-step (λmax → T → L → R → d), and explanations are marked by the number of correct physics links you make.

Weak answer vs top answer

Explain how a main-sequence star remains in equilibrium. [3]

1 / 3

The heat from burning pushes out and gravity pulls in, so it is balanced.

“Burning” is wrong (it is fusion), and it does not say what produces the outward pressure or where the balance happens.
3 / 3

Gravity pulls all the mass of the star inwards MP1. Fusion in the core releases energy, which keeps the gas hot and produces radiation, so there is an outward thermal (gas) and radiation pressure MP2. At every layer the outward pressure force balances the inward gravitational force, so the star neither collapses nor expands MP3.

Names both forces, the energy source, and the balance.

Compare the evolution of a 1 M☉ star with that of a 20 M☉ star after the main sequence. [6]

2 / 6

The small star becomes a red giant and then a white dwarf. The big star becomes a supernova and then a black hole.

Only end points. No fusion stages, no mechanism, and “black hole” is stated without the neutron-star alternative.
6 / 6

In both, the core runs out of hydrogen, contracts and heats up, hydrogen fuses in a shell and the outer layers expand MP1. The 1 M☉ star becomes a red giant and fuses helium into carbon and oxygen MP2, then ejects its outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving a white dwarf that no longer fuses and slowly cools MP3. The 20 M☉ star becomes a red supergiant MP4 and fuses successively heavier elements in shells, up to iron MP5. Iron fusion cannot release energy, so the core collapses and the star explodes as a supernova, leaving a neutron star or a black hole MP6.

Every stage, the reason the sequence stops at iron, and the comparison running throughout.

The calculation chain

  1. λmax → T with Wien’s law.
  2. T and R → L with L = σ4πR²T⁴.
  3. L and b → d with b = L/4πd².
  4. p → d with d = 1/p, then convert units.

If part (a) goes wrong, keep going: ECF gives the later marks.

Energy release in four lines

  1. Mass of reactants − mass of products = Δm (in u).
  2. Use atomic masses throughout, as given.
  3. E = Δm × 931.5 MeV.
  4. For a rate: Δm/Δt = L/c².

7 · Before the exam

Data booklet equations for E.5

d (parsec) = 1p (arc-second) L = σ A T⁴ A = 4πR² b = L4π d² λmax T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K E = m c² 1 u = 931.5 MeV c⁻²

Constants and conversions

  • σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴
  • c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹
  • L☉ = 3.83 × 10²⁶ W · R☉ = 6.96 × 10⁸ m · M☉ = 1.99 × 10³⁰ kg
  • 1 AU = 1.50 × 10¹¹ m · 1 ly = 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m
  • 1 pc = 3.09 × 10¹⁶ m = 3.26 ly ≈ 2.06 × 10⁵ AU
  • Sun’s surface temperature ≈ 5800 K (it varies a little between sources)

Final checklist

  • I can explain equilibrium with gravity and radiation pressure.
  • I can explain why fusion needs high temperature and high density.
  • I can calculate the energy released from atomic masses.
  • I can describe the evolution of low-mass and high-mass stars.
  • I can sketch an HR diagram with every region and lines of constant radius.
  • I can convert between AU, ly and pc and use d = 1/p.
  • I can find a star’s radius from L and T, with its uncertainty.
  • I can get temperature and composition from a spectrum.
