The Photoelectric Effect

Light knocks electrons out of metal

Shine light on a clean metal plate. Above a certain frequency, electrons fly off. Below it, nothing happens — no matter how bright.

What experiments actually show

Five observations — the last three are the ones classical physics could not explain.

1

Brighter light → more electrons, released more quickly. Intensity controls the number per second.

2

No time delay. Emission starts the instant the light arrives — there is no waiting for energy to build up.

3

A threshold frequency f₀. Below it, no electrons escape however bright the light. Zinc's threshold sits in the UV.

4

Metal-dependent. The same light ejects electrons from one metal but not another — each metal has its own threshold.

5

A range of kinetic energies, up to a well-defined maximum Emax that depends on frequency, not brightness.

Why the wave picture fails

Wave theory predicts

  • Any frequency works if you make it bright enough.
  • A dim source needs a delay to gather enough energy.
  • Brighter light gives faster (more energetic) electrons.

What really happens

  • Below f₀, nothing — brightness cannot help.
  • Emission is instant, even for very dim light.
  • Brightness changes the number, never the maximum energy.

Einstein's fix (1905): light arrives as photons, each carrying energy E = hf. One photon gives all its energy to one electron, instantly. That single idea explains all five observations.

Work function, Φ — the minimum energy needed to pull an electron free from the surface of a particular metal. Every metal has its own value (typically 2–5 eV). A photon must carry at least this much (so hf ≥ Φ) to release an electron; whatever energy is left over becomes the electron's kinetic energy. A larger work function means a higher threshold frequency.

Derivation · Einstein's photoelectric equation

Goal: Emax = hf − Φ, and its experimental form eVs = hf − Φ.

Step 1 · A photon of energy hf
Ephoton=hf
Light arrives as photons. A photon of frequency f carries energy hf, and it hands all of it to a single electron in one instant.
Step 2 · Conservation of energy
hf=(work to free electron)+Ek
Some energy pays to lift the electron out of the metal; the rest becomes its kinetic energy as it leaves.
Step 3 · The cheapest escape = work function
work to free electron≥Φ
Electrons sit at different depths, so the escape cost varies. The minimum is the work function Φ. Electrons that pay only Φ keep the most kinetic energy.
Step 4 · Maximum kinetic energy
Emax=hf−Φ
Take the electron that paid the least (Φ). Its kinetic energy is the largest possible: Emax = hf − Φ. This is Einstein's photoelectric equation.
Step 5 · The threshold frequency
Emax = 0⇒hf0=Φ⇒Emax=h(f−f0)
Set Emax = 0: the photon only just frees the electron. That defines the threshold frequency f₀ = Φ/h. Below f₀ there is not enough energy, so nothing is emitted.
Step 6 · The measurable form
Emax = eVs⇒eVs=hf−Φ
A reverse voltage Vs that just stops the fastest electrons measures Emax = eVs. So a graph of eVs against f is a straight line of gradient h — that is how the experiment finds Planck's constant.

How Einstein's equation rescues the failing classical picture

Each thing the wave model got wrong falls straight out of Emax = hf − Φ.

✕

Threshold frequency. Waves: any colour should work if it is bright enough.

✓

If hf < Φ then hf − Φ is negative — impossible for a kinetic energy — so no electron escapes. Emission only begins at f₀ = Φ/h, exactly the observed threshold.

✕

No time delay. Waves: dim light must take time to build up enough energy.

✓

A photon hands its whole energy hf to one electron in a single interaction. There is nothing to accumulate, so emission is instant even in very dim light.

✕

Energy vs brightness. Waves: brighter light should give faster electrons.

✓

Brightness means more photons, so more electrons — but each photon still carries only hf. The maximum KE, hf − Φ, depends on frequency alone, never on intensity.

Worked examples

Fresh problems — work each one yourself first, then reveal the full solution. Use h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00×10⁸ m s⁻¹, e = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C.

Worked example 1

Light of wavelength 4.50×10⁻⁷ m falls on a sodium surface (work function Φ = 2.28 eV). Find (a) the frequency, (b) the photon energy in eV, (c) whether the photoelectric effect occurs, (d) the maximum kinetic energy, and (e) the stopping voltage.

(a)   f=cλ=3.00×10⁸4.50×10⁻⁷=6.67×10¹⁴ Hz
(b)   E = hf = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ × 6.67×10¹⁴ = 4.42×10⁻¹⁹ J = 2.76 eV

(c) The photon carries 2.76 eV, which is more than Φ = 2.28 eV, so yes, electrons are emitted.

(d)   Emax = hf − Φ = 2.76 − 2.28 = 0.48 eV (= 7.7×10⁻²⁰ J)
(e)   Vs = Emax/e = 0.48 V
Worked example 2

The longest wavelength that ejects electrons from a certain metal is 620 nm. Find (a) its threshold frequency, (b) its work function in eV, and (c) state, with a reason, whether green light of wavelength 520 nm will cause emission.

(a)   f0 = c/λ0 = 3.00×10⁸ / 620×10⁻⁹ = 4.84×10¹⁴ Hz
(b)   Φ = hf0 = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ × 4.84×10¹⁴ = 3.21×10⁻¹⁹ J = 2.00 eV

(c) Green light has λ = 520 nm < 620 nm, so a higher frequency (5.77×10¹⁴ Hz) than the threshold. Yes — shorter wavelength means higher photon energy, so it exceeds Φ and ejects electrons.

Worked example 3

In a stopping-voltage experiment the current falls to zero when the reverse voltage reaches 1.35 V. State the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons in (a) electronvolts and (b) joules.

(a) By definition Emax = eVs, and in electronvolts this is numerically equal to the stopping voltage: 1.35 eV.

(b)   Emax = eVs = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ × 1.35 = 2.16×10⁻¹⁹ J
Worked example 4

Two frequencies are tested on the same metal. At 7.0×10¹⁴ Hz the stopping voltage is 0.62 V; at 10.0×10¹⁴ Hz it is 1.86 V. Use these to find (a) Planck's constant and (b) the work function.

Since eVs = hf − Φ, a graph of eVs against f has gradient h.

(a)   h=e(Vs2 − Vs1)f2 − f1=1.60×10⁻¹⁹ (1.86 − 0.62)(10.0 − 7.0)×10¹⁴=6.6×10⁻³⁴ J s
(b)   Φ = hf1 − eVs1 = (6.6×10⁻³⁴ × 7.0×10¹⁴) − (1.60×10⁻¹⁹ × 0.62)
     = 4.63×10⁻¹⁹ − 0.99×10⁻¹⁹ = 3.6×10⁻¹⁹ J = 2.3 eV

Common misconceptions, cleared up

The traps that cost marks in the photoelectric effect. Each card pairs the wrong idea with the fix.

✕

"Brighter light makes the photoelectrons faster."

✓

Intensity sets the number of electrons per second, not their speed. The maximum kinetic energy depends only on frequency and the metal: Emax = hf − Φ. Doubling brightness doubles the current, not the energy.

✕

"Any light will work if it is bright enough."

✓

Below the threshold frequency each photon has too little energy (hf < Φ). Because one photon interacts with one electron, piling on more weak photons never helps — nothing is emitted.

✕

"The electron absorbs several photons until it has enough energy."

✓

The transfer is a single photon to a single electron, all at once. That is exactly why emission is instantaneous — there is no build-up time.

✕

"The stopping voltage depends on the brightness."

✓

Vs measures Emax, which depends only on frequency and Φ. Brighter light of the same frequency raises the saturation current but leaves the stopping voltage unchanged.

✕

"The work function is the energy of the photon."

✓

Φ is a property of the metal — the minimum energy needed to free an electron. The photon's energy is hf. Emission requires hf ≥ Φ.

✕

"All photoelectrons come off with the same energy."

✓

Electrons start at different depths, so they lose different amounts escaping. Their energies range from nearly zero up to a maximum, Emax.

✕

"A different metal gives a different gradient on the Emax–f graph."

✓

The gradient is always Planck's constant h. A different metal shifts the line sideways (new f₀ and new −Φ intercept) but the slope never changes.

Check your understanding

Original questions — attempt each before revealing. h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J s, e = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C.

Q1 · Concept

State what determines the maximum kinetic energy of a photoelectron, and separately what determines the number emitted each second.

Maximum KE depends on the frequency of the light and the metal's work function: E_max = hf − Φ. The number per second depends on the intensity (more photons → more photoelectrons), provided f > f₀.
Q2 · Reasoning

A metal has work function 3.0 eV. Light of photon energy 2.5 eV is shone on it very brightly. State and explain what happens.

No emission. Each photon carries 2.5 eV < Φ = 3.0 eV, so no single photon can free an electron. Since one photon interacts with one electron, raising the intensity only adds more too-weak photons — nothing is ejected.
Q3 · Calculation

Photons of frequency 9.0×10¹⁴ Hz strike a metal whose threshold frequency is 5.0×10¹⁴ Hz. Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons, in eV.

E_max = h(f − f₀) = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ × (9.0 − 5.0)×10¹⁴ = 2.65×10⁻¹⁹ J. Divide by 1.60×10⁻¹⁹: 1.66 eV.
Q4 · Explanation

Explain why the existence of a threshold frequency cannot be explained by the wave model of light.

In the wave model, energy is delivered continuously and depends on intensity, so light of any frequency should eventually free electrons if it is bright enough or left long enough. The observed sharp threshold — independent of intensity — only makes sense if energy arrives in photon packets of size hf, so that below hf = Φ no single packet is large enough.
Q5 · Graphs

On an Emax vs f graph, the line for metal X lies to the left of the line for metal Y, and both have the same gradient. Compare the two metals.

Same gradient confirms both give the same h. X has the smaller x-intercept, so a lower threshold frequency f₀ and therefore a smaller work function (Φ = hf₀). X releases electrons more easily than Y.
Q6 · Experiment

Doubling the intensity of the light (same frequency) leaves the stopping voltage unchanged but doubles the saturation current. Explain both results.

Same frequency → same photon energy → same Emax → same stopping voltage (Vs = Emax/e). Double intensity → twice as many photons per second → twice as many photoelectrons → double the saturation current.
