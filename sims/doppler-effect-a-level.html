7.4 · Why pitch changes

The Doppler Effect

When a sound source moves, the wavefronts bunch up ahead of it and stretch out behind. A stationary observer therefore meets them at a different rate — hearing a higher pitch as the source approaches and a lower pitch as it recedes.

AS · Standard

Each wavefront leaves the source as a circle that then spreads at the speed of sound. Because the source has moved on before it emits the next one, the circles crowd together ahead of the motion and spread apart behind it.

Moving source, bunched wavefronts

The observer ahead (right) meets wavefronts more often → higher frequency. The observer behind (left) meets them less often → lower frequency. The source frequency itself never changes.

What the observers hear

The source emits at a steady rate, so the spacing of the circles at the moment of emission is always the same. What changes is where the centre of each circle sits, because the source keeps moving.

Ahead of the source the centres march forward, so successive wavefronts are packed into a shorter distance: the wavelength is reduced and the observed frequency rises.

Behind the source the centres fall back, so the wavefronts are spread over a longer distance: the wavelength is stretched and the observed frequency drops.

The speed of the sound in the air is unchanged throughout — it is set by the medium, not by the source's motion.

AS · Standard

Put numbers to it. In one period the source advances a distance vsT while the wavefront advances vT, so the wavelength on the approaching side is squeezed to (v − vs)T. Dividing the sound speed by that gives the observed frequency.

Squeezed and stretched wavelengths

Speed of sound taken as v = 340 m s⁻¹.

Observed frequency

fo = fs vv ± vs

Derivation · where the formula comes from

For a source moving directly towards a stationary observer. Reveal one line at a time.

Step 1 · one period of emission
Ts = 1fs
Between emitting one wavefront and the next, a time of one source period passes.
Step 2 · how far each thing moves
wavefront moves vTₛ  ·  source moves vₛTₛ
In that time the sound travels vTs while the source chases it, travelling vsTs in the same direction.
Step 3 · the squeezed wavelength
λ′ = (v − vs)Ts = v − vsfs
Ahead of the source the gap between wavefronts is the head-start of the sound minus the distance the source closed.
Step 4 · the observed frequency
fo = vλ′ = fs vv − vs
The observer still receives the wavefronts travelling at v, so fo = v/λ′. Use (v + vs) instead when the source recedes.
A car horn sounds a note of frequency 420 Hz. The car moves directly towards a stationary pedestrian at 25 m s⁻¹. Take the speed of sound as 340 m s⁻¹.
Question
(a) State whether the pedestrian hears a higher or lower frequency, and why. [2]
Higher. The source is approaching, so the wavefronts reach the pedestrian bunched into a shorter wavelength, raising the observed frequency.
(b) Calculate the frequency heard by the pedestrian. [2]
fo = fs vv − vs=420 × 340340 − 25≈453 Hz
(c) After the car passes, calculate the frequency the pedestrian now hears. [2]
fo = 420 × 340340 + 25≈391 Hz
The note drops as the car recedes.

This is the ambulance-siren experience. As the source travels along the line towards the observer the pitch is steady and high; the instant it passes, the pitch drops to a steady low value. Watch the frequency–time graph build up.

A train sounds a whistle of constant frequency as it passes through a station at constant speed. A passenger on the platform hears 660 Hz as the train approaches and 540 Hz after it has passed. (v = 340 m s⁻¹)
Question
(a) Explain why the heard frequency changes as the train passes, even though the whistle frequency is constant. [2]
While approaching, the wavefronts are compressed (shorter λ), so the observed frequency is above the source value. After passing, they are stretched (longer λ), so it falls below. The whistle itself is unchanged.
(b) Without finding fs, state why the approaching value is further from fs for a faster train. [1]
A larger vs makes the denominator (v − vs) smaller, so the ratio v/(v − vs) — and hence the shift — is greater.
(c) Challenge: estimate the whistle frequency fs. [2]
Averaging is only approximate; solving properly, 660 = fs·340/(340−vs) and 540 = fs·340/(340+vs). Dividing gives 660/540 = (340+vs)/(340−vs), so vs ≈ 34 m s⁻¹ and fs ≈ 600 Hz.
AS · Standard

Four problems using fo = fs v/(v ± vs). Take v = 340 m s⁻¹ throughout. Try each before revealing the solution.

Worked Example · Approaching source
A siren emits 256 Hz and moves towards a stationary listener at 20 m s⁻¹. What frequency is heard?
fo = 256 × 340340 − 20 = 256 × 340320=272 Hz

Approaching → subtract vs → frequency rises.

Worked Example · Receding source
The same siren (256 Hz) now moves directly away from the listener at 20 m s⁻¹. What frequency is heard?
fo = 256 × 340340 + 20 = 256 × 340360≈242 Hz

Receding → add vs → frequency falls.

Worked Example · Finding the source speed
A source of true frequency 950 Hz is heard at 1000 Hz as it approaches. Calculate its speed.

Rearrange fo = fsv/(v − vs) for the denominator:

v − vs = fs vfo = 950 × 3401000 = 323
vs = 340 − 323=17 m s⁻¹
Worked Example · Wavelength ahead and behind
A source emits 170 Hz while moving at 34 m s⁻¹. Find the wavelength of the sound in front of and behind the source.
λfront = v − vsfs = 306170=1.8 m
λbehind = v + vsfs = 374170=2.2 m

Compare with the stationary value λ = 340/170 = 2.0 m: squeezed in front, stretched behind.

AS · Standard

The traps on the Doppler effect. Each card pairs the wrong idea with the correct physics.

"The source's frequency changes."

The source emits at a constant frequency. It is only the frequency observed by the listener that changes.

fs is constant; fo changes.
"The sound travels faster when the source speeds up."

The speed of sound depends on the medium, not on the source. It stays the same; the wavelength changes instead.

v is fixed by the air; λ changes.
"Use (v − vₛ) whether approaching or receding."

Subtract vs for an approaching source (frequency up); add it for a receding source (frequency down).

Approach: v − vs. Recede: v + vs.
"The Doppler effect is the sound getting louder."

Doppler is a change of frequency (pitch) and wavelength. Loudness relates to intensity, a separate effect.

Doppler changes pitch, not loudness.
"As it passes, the listener briefly hears the true frequency."

For a source moving straight along the line of sight, the heard pitch jumps from the high (approaching) value to the low (receding) value — it is not the true fs while moving.

Pitch steps high → low on passing.
"The Doppler effect only happens with sound."

It applies to all waves. Light from receding galaxies is red-shifted — the same idea — though this syllabus formula is for a moving sound source.

All waves; e.g. red-shift of light.
