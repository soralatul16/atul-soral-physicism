What it means & why it matters

Electric potential Vₑ at a point is the potential energy per unit positive charge placed there — the work per coulomb to bring a charge in from infinity.

Physical significance: it is a property of the field alone (no test charge needed), so it maps the whole field with a single scalar "height". Differences in potential are what we call voltage and what drive charges to move — the electrical twin of height in gravity.

Potential around a point charge

Derivation · Vₑ = kQ / r

Potential is energy per unit charge, so we divide the test charge's energy by its own charge.

Step 1 · place a test charge
Ep = kQqr
A small positive test charge q sits a distance r from the source Q. From Tab 1, their potential energy is kQq/r.
Step 2 · definition of potential
Ve = Epq
Electric potential is defined as the potential energy per unit charge.
Step 3 · substitute and cancel q
Ve = 1q·kQqr
The test charge q cancels — proof that potential belongs to the source and position only.
Step 4 · result
Ve = kQr
A scalar, zero at infinity. Positive near +Q, negative near −Q. Potentials from several charges add with sign.

Question
Point charges of +4.0 µC and +4.0 µC are 0.60 m apart. Point M is midway between them.
(a) Calculate the potential at M due to one charge. [2]
Ve = kQr=(8.99×10⁹)(4.0×10⁻⁶)0.30=1.2 × 10⁵ V
(b) Hence find the total potential at M. [2]
Potential is a scalar, so add: V = 2 × 1.2 × 10⁵ = 2.4 × 10⁵ V.
(c) State the resultant electric field at M, and why it differs from the potential. [2]
The field is zero at M: the two field vectors are equal and opposite and cancel. Potential (a scalar) adds to a non-zero value, while the field (a vector) cancels.

What it means & why it matters

The potential gradient is how steeply the potential changes with position. The field strength equals its negative: E = −ΔVₑ/Δr.

Physical significance: potential (an energy picture) and field (a force picture) are two descriptions of one thing. Where the potential "hill" is steep the field is strong; the minus sign means the field points downhill — the way a positive charge is pushed.

Potential and its gradient

Upper graph: Vₑ vs r with its tangent (the gradient). Lower graph: E vs r. The tangent's steepness equals E at that r.

Derivation · E = −ΔVₑ / Δr

Move a test charge a tiny step and write the work two ways — through the field and through the potential.

Step 1 · work via the potential
ΔW = qΔVe
Moving charge q through a small step Δr changes its energy by qΔVₑ.
Step 2 · work via the field
ΔW = −F Δr = −qE Δr
The field force is F = qE. Work done against it over a step Δr along the field is −qE·Δr.
Step 3 · equate the two
qΔVe = −qE Δr
Same work, so set the expressions equal.
Step 4 · cancel q, rearrange
E = −ΔVeΔr
The field is the negative potential gradient: it points from high to low potential, and its size is the steepness of the Vₑ–r graph.

The working

E = −ΔVeΔrunit V m⁻¹ = N C⁻¹
Question
The potential 0.30 m from a point charge is 1.5 × 10⁵ V, and 0.20 m from it the potential is 2.25 × 10⁵ V.
(a) Estimate the average field strength between these two points. [2]
E = −ΔVeΔr=−(1.5−2.25)×10⁵(0.30−0.20)=7.5 × 10⁵ V m⁻¹
Pointing away from the (positive) charge.
(b) State why the field points in the direction of decreasing potential. [1]
Because E = −ΔVₑ/Δr: a positive charge is pushed from high to low potential, the direction in which its energy falls.
HL · Additional Higher Level
What it means & why it matters

Work to move a charge between two points is W = qΔVₑ — set only by the potential difference. An equipotential surface joins all points at the same potential.

Physical significance: because the electric field is conservative, only the start and end potentials matter, not the path taken. Moving along an equipotential needs no work, which is exactly why equipotentials always cross field lines at right angles.

Equipotentials and field lines

Drag the test charge. Move it along a ring (equipotential) and the work stays zero; move it across rings and work is done.

Derivation · W = qΔVₑ

This follows straight from the definition of potential difference.

Step 1 · potential difference
ΔVe = VB − VA
Take two points A and B; their potential difference is the difference of their potentials.
Step 2 · definition
ΔVe = Wq
Potential difference is the work done per unit charge moving from A to B.
Step 3 · result
W = qΔVe
Depends only on the endpoints (the field is conservative). Along an equipotential ΔVₑ = 0, so no work is done — hence equipotentials are perpendicular to field lines.

The working

W = qΔVe
Question
A charge of +2.0 nC is moved between two points whose potentials are 200 V and 500 V.
(a) Calculate the work done moving it from the 200 V point to the 500 V point. [2]
W = qΔVe = (2.0×10⁻⁹)(500−200) =6.0 × 10⁻⁷ J
Positive — work is done on the charge to raise it to higher potential.
(b) The charge is then moved along an equipotential at 500 V. State the work done. [1]
Zero — along an equipotential ΔVₑ = 0, so W = qΔVₑ = 0.
(c) State the angle between an equipotential surface and the field lines. [1]
90° — they are always perpendicular.

Two charges, +5.0 µC and −5.0 µC, are 0.40 m apart. Find the potential at the midpoint.

The subtle traps of potential and energy. Each card pairs the wrong idea with the correct physics.

"Electric potential is a vector."

Potential is a scalar — size and sign but no direction. Potentials from several charges add algebraically.

Vₑ is a scalar (volts).
"Potential and potential energy are the same thing."

Potential is energy per unit charge: Vₑ = Eₚ/q. Potential belongs to the point; energy depends on the charge placed there.

Vₑ = Eₚ / q.
"If the potential is zero, the field must be zero."

Not so: midway between +q and −q the potential is zero but the field is not. Scalar and vector are independent.

V = 0 does not mean E = 0.
"Field lines run parallel to equipotential surfaces."

They are always perpendicular; otherwise the field would have a component along the surface and moving a charge along it would do work.

Field lines ⟂ equipotentials.
"Moving a charge along an equipotential needs work."

Along an equipotential ΔVₑ = 0, so W = qΔVₑ = 0. No work is done.

No work along an equipotential.
"The potential energy of two opposite charges is positive."

It is negative: Eₚ = kq₁q₂/r with opposite signs gives Eₚ < 0. Energy must be supplied to separate them.

Opposite charges: Eₚ < 0.

HL · Additional Higher Level
What it means & why it matters

Electric potential energy Eₚ is the energy stored in an arrangement of charges — the work done to bring them from infinite separation to where they are.

Physical significance: it tells you the energy released if the charges fly apart (like charges) or the energy you must supply to pull them apart (opposite charges). Working with energy — a scalar — lets us use conservation of energy instead of tracking forces and directions.

Energy of a pair of charges

Derivation · Eₚ = k q₁q₂ / r

Watch the shaded area in the animation as you reveal each step — it is exactly the work this derivation integrates.

Step 1 · the force to overcome
F = kq1q2x2
At a separation x the charges exert a Coulomb force on each other. To assemble them we do work against this force.
Step 2 · work = area under F–x
Ep = W = ∫∞r F dx
The potential energy equals the work to bring q₂ in from infinity — the shaded area under the force–distance graph (energy is defined zero at infinity).
Step 3 · do the integral
Ep = kq1q2 ∫∞r 1x2 dx = kq1q2[−1x]∞r
The integral of 1/x² is −1/x, evaluated between x = r and x = ∞.
Step 4 · evaluate the limits
Ep = kq1q2(0 −(−1r))
At x = ∞ the term −1/x → 0; at x = r it is −1/r. Subtracting gives +1/r.
Step 5 · result
Ep = kq1q2r
Positive for like charges (energy stored pushing them together); negative for opposite charges (the field did the work; the system is bound).

The working

Ep = kq1q2runit J · k = 8.99×10⁹
Question
Two point charges, +3.0 µC and −2.0 µC, are held 0.40 m apart.
(a) Calculate the electric potential energy of the pair. [2]
Ep = kq1q2r=(8.99×10⁹)(3.0×10⁻⁶)(−2.0×10⁻⁶)0.40=−0.13 J
(b) Explain the sign of your answer. [2]
Negative because the charges are opposite and attract. The field does positive work pulling them together, so the stored energy is below the zero defined at infinity — the pair is bound.
(c) State the energy needed to separate them to infinity. [1]
+0.13 J — equal and opposite to the potential energy, since Eₚ(∞) = 0.
