01Electric charge

Guiding question 1How do charged particles flow through materials?

Atoms contain a tiny, dense nucleus of protons (positive) and neutrons (no charge), surrounded by electrons (negative). A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. Electrons in the outer parts of atoms can be removed or added: an atom that has lost electrons is a positive ion; one that has gained electrons is a negative ion.

DefinitionsCharge q is measured in coulombs (C). The elementary charge e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C is the charge on a proton (and minus the charge on an electron).
Quantisation: any charge found on an object is a whole-number multiple of e: q = Ne, where N is an integer.
Conservation of charge: charge cannot be created or destroyed. The total charge of an isolated system stays the same.
q = N e # so 1 C is the charge of N = frac{1}{1.60 × 10^{-19}} => 6.25 × 10^{18} electrons

Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. A charged object also attracts a neutral one, because it pulls the electrons in the neutral object slightly towards it or pushes them away (it induces a separation of charge).

Charging by friction (rubbing): when two different insulators are rubbed together, electrons are transferred from one to the other. Only electrons move: protons are locked in the nuclei. Polythene rubbed with wool gains electrons and becomes negative; the wool becomes equally positive. Cellulose acetate rubbed with a cloth loses electrons and becomes positive. The total charge is unchanged.

Example 1.1A polythene rod gains a charge of −4.8 × 10⁻⁹ C when rubbed with a wool cloth. Calculate the number of electrons transferred and state the charge on the cloth.
N = frac{q}{e} = frac{4.8 × 10^{-9}}{1.60 × 10^{-19}} => N = 3.0 × 10^{10} electrons # charge is conserved, so the cloth has +4.8 × 10⁻⁹ C
Common confusionA positively charged object has lost electrons. It has not gained protons. In solids, only electrons are free to be transferred.

02Charge carriers and materials: solids, liquids and gases

A charge carrier is any charged particle that is free to move through a material. A material conducts if it has a large number density of mobile charge carriers.

Number density of charge carriersn = number of mobile charge carriers per unit volume (unit m⁻³). For copper, n ≈ 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³: about one free electron per atom.

Solids

  • Metals (conductors): each atom gives one or more outer electrons to a shared “sea” of free (delocalised) electrons. The atoms become a fixed lattice of positive ions. The free electrons carry the current. n is very large (about 10²⁸–10²⁹ m⁻³) and does not change with temperature.
  • Insulators (glass, plastic, rubber, dry wood): all electrons are held tightly in their atoms or bonds. Almost no free charge carriers.
  • Semiconductors (silicon, germanium): a few electrons gain enough energy to break free, leaving behind holes (gaps that behave like positive carriers). n is small (about 10¹⁶ m⁻³ in pure silicon at room temperature) but rises steeply with temperature, because more electrons get enough energy to break free, and with light, because photons can free electrons. So a semiconductor’s resistance falls when it is heated or lit. This is how thermistors and LDRs work.

Liquids

  • Electrolytes are solutions or molten compounds containing ions: for example salt water (Na⁺ and Cl⁻) or copper sulfate solution (Cu²⁺ and SO₄²⁻). When a pd is applied, positive ions move towards the negative electrode (cathode) and negative ions move towards the positive electrode (anode). Both movements make up a current in the same direction. The ions are discharged at the electrodes, so chemical changes happen (electrolysis, electroplating).
  • Liquid metals (mercury, molten metals) conduct by free electrons, like solid metals.
  • Pure water has very few ions and is a poor conductor; dissolved salts make it conduct well. Warming an electrolyte makes the ions move more easily, so its resistance falls.

Gases

  • A gas of neutral molecules is normally an excellent insulator. A few ions are always made by cosmic rays and background radiation, so a tiny current can flow.
  • With a large enough pd, the few free electrons are accelerated enough to knock electrons out of molecules when they collide (ionisation by collision). Each collision makes more electrons: an avalanche. The gas becomes ionised and conducts, often glowing (neon signs, discharge tubes, sparks).
  • Electrons move towards the positive electrode and positive ions towards the negative electrode.
  • A gas in which a large fraction of the atoms are ionised is a plasma: the fourth state of matter (lightning, stars, plasma balls, fusion reactors).

03Random thermal motion

Even with no battery connected, the free electrons in a metal move very fast, about 10⁶ m s⁻¹, in random directions. They keep colliding with the vibrating lattice ions and change direction, following a zigzag path.

Why there is no current without a pdAt any moment, as many electrons cross a cross-section of the wire from left to right as from right to left. The net flow of charge is zero, so the current is zero. You can count the crossings in Current & drift → Random motion.

04Electric current

Connecting a cell sets up an electric field along the whole wire, so every free electron feels a force towards the positive terminal. On top of their fast random motion, the electrons now slowly drift towards the positive terminal. This net flow of charge is the current.

DefinitionElectric current is the rate of flow of charge: I = frac{Δq}{Δt}. Unit: the ampere (A), an SI base unit. 1 A = 1 C s⁻¹. A direct current (dc) flows in one direction only.
  • Conventional current flows from the positive terminal, round the circuit, to the negative terminal: the direction positive charges would move.
  • Electron flow is in the opposite direction, from − to +. This convention was fixed (by Franklin) before the electron was discovered.
Current is not used upCharge is conserved, so the current is the same at every point in a series loop: the same number of coulombs per second pass every point. Components transfer energy, not charge. At a junction, the total current in equals the total current out.
Example 4.1A charge of 180 C passes through a lamp in 2.0 minutes. Calculate the current, and the number of electrons per second.
I = frac{Δq}{Δt} = frac{180}{120} => I = 1.5 A electrons per second = frac{1.5}{1.60 × 10^{-19}} => 9.4 × 10^{18} s^{-1}
Area under a current–time graphSince Δq = IΔt, the area under an I–t graph is the charge that has flowed. The gradient of a charge–time graph is the current.

05Drift velocity: I = nAvq

# volume of the cylinder that crosses the face in Δt V = A v Δt # number of carriers in it N = n A v Δt # charge carried Δq = n A v Δt q I = frac{Δq}{Δt} => I = n A v q

Here n is the number density of carriers, A the cross-sectional area, v the drift speed and q the charge on each carrier.

Example 5.1A copper wire of cross-sectional area 1.0 mm² carries a current of 2.0 A. For copper n = 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³. Calculate the drift speed, and the time for an electron to drift 1.0 m.
v = frac{I}{n A q} = frac{2.0}{8.5 × 10^{28} × 1.0 × 10^{-6} × 1.60 × 10^{-19}} => v = 1.5 × 10^{-4} m s^{-1} t = frac{1.0}{1.5 × 10^{-4}} => t = 6.8 × 10^{3} s (about 2 hours)
How each factor affects the drift speed (same current)v = I/(nAq). Thinner wire (smaller A): carriers must drift faster. Fewer carriers (smaller n, e.g. a semiconductor): they must drift much faster, about 10⁷ times faster in silicon than in copper for the same current density. Carriers with twice the charge (Cu²⁺ ions): half the drift speed. In series, the current is the same everywhere, so the electrons drift faster in the thinner part of a wire.
Remember the unitsConvert mm² to m²: 1 mm² = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m². A wire of diameter d has A = πd²/4.

06Signal speed: why the lamp lights at once

If electrons drift at only about 0.1 mm s⁻¹, why does a lamp light the instant you press the switch? Because the wire is already full of free electrons. Closing the switch sets up the electric field in the whole circuit almost instantly: the field (the signal) travels at a large fraction of the speed of light, about 2 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. Every electron, including those already inside the lamp filament, starts drifting at the same moment.

Marbles in a tubePush one marble into one end of a tube already packed with marbles, and a marble pops out of the other end at once, even though each marble has moved only a tiny distance. In Current & drift → Signal speed you can compare the two speeds directly.

07Potential difference and emf

DefinitionsPotential difference (pd) V between two points is the work done (energy transferred) per unit charge as charge moves between them: V = frac{W}{q}. Unit: the volt, 1 V = 1 J C⁻¹.
Electromotive force (emf) ε of a source is the work done by the source per unit charge in moving charge around the complete circuit: the energy given to each coulomb by the source (for example chemical energy → electrical energy in a cell).
emf εpd V
Energy transferother forms → electrical energy (in the source)electrical energy → other forms (in a component)
Whereacross a source (cell, generator, solar cell)across a component (lamp, resistor, motor)
Unitvolt, V = J C⁻¹

By conservation of energy, around a complete loop the energy given to each coulomb by the sources equals the energy taken from it by the components: sum of emfs = sum of pds.

Example 7.1A 12 V battery drives a current of 0.50 A through a lamp for 5.0 minutes. Determine the charge that flows and the energy transferred.
q = I t = 0.50 × 300 => q = 150 C W = q V = 150 × 12 => W = 1800 J
Voltage does not “flow”Current flows through a component; pd is measured across it, between two points. A voltmeter is always connected in parallel, across the component.

Example 8.1A phone battery is rated at 3.7 V and 4000 mA h. Calculate the energy it stores.
q = 4.000 × 3600 = 1.44 × 10^{4} C E = q ε = 1.44 × 10^{4} × 3.7 => E = 5.3 × 10^{4} J

09Circuit diagrams and meters

  • An ammeter measures current. It is connected in series, so the current to be measured passes through it. An ideal ammeter has zero resistance, so it does not change the current.
  • A voltmeter measures pd. It is connected in parallel, across the component. An ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance, so no current passes through it.
  • Real (non-ideal) meters disturb the circuit a little: an ammeter adds a small resistance, and a voltmeter in parallel with a large resistor takes some current and lowers the pd it is measuring.
What goes wrongA voltmeter connected in series has a huge resistance, so the current almost stops (it reads close to the emf). An ammeter connected in parallel with a component short-circuits it and may be damaged by the large current. Try both in Circuits → Circuit builder.

10Resistance

Guiding question 3What are the consequences of resistance in conductors?
DefinitionThe resistance of a component is the ratio of the pd across it to the current through it: R = frac{V}{I}. Unit: the ohm, 1 Ω = 1 V A⁻¹.

Particle model: the drifting electrons are accelerated by the electric field, then collide with the vibrating lattice ions, losing the kinetic energy they gained. These collisions oppose the flow of charge: this is resistance. In each collision energy is passed to the lattice, which vibrates more: the wire gets hotter.

Temperature: when a metal is heated, its ions vibrate with larger amplitude, so the electrons collide with them more often. The drift speed for a given pd falls, so the resistance of a metal increases with temperature. (In a semiconductor the extra carriers matter more, so its resistance falls.)

11Resistivity

The resistance of a wire is proportional to its length L (electrons must pass more ions) and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area A (more carriers can drift side by side). The constant is the resistivity ρ of the material.

Example 11.1A nichrome wire is 2.0 m long and 0.40 mm in diameter. ρ = 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m. Calculate its resistance.
A = frac{π d^{2}}{4} = frac{π × (0.40 × 10^{-3})^{2}}{4} = 1.26 × 10^{-7} m^{2} R = frac{1.1 × 10^{-6} × 2.0}{1.26 × 10^{-7}} => R = 17.5 Ω
Diameter, not radiusDoubling the diameter makes the area 4 times bigger, so the resistance becomes ¼. Halving the length halves R; stretching a wire to twice its length (same volume) halves A as well, so R becomes 4 times bigger.

12Ohm’s law and I–V characteristics

Ohm’s lawThe current in a conductor is directly proportional to the pd across it, provided the temperature (and other physical conditions) stay constant. A conductor that obeys Ohm’s law is ohmic: its I–V graph is a straight line through the origin, and R is constant.
Resistance is V/I, not the gradientFor a non-ohmic component, find R at a point by reading V and I at that point and dividing: R = V/I (the gradient of the line from the origin to the point, on a V–I graph). The gradient of the tangent is not the resistance. For a filament lamp, V/I increases as the filament heats up.

13The heating effect of current

When current flows through a resistor, the electrons repeatedly gain kinetic energy from the field and give it to the lattice ions in collisions. The ions vibrate with greater amplitude: the internal energy of the conductor increases and its temperature rises. This is Joule (resistive) heating. It is useful in kettles, heaters, toasters and fuses, and wasteful in power cables and computer chips.

Thermal and electrical conduction are alikeMetals are good conductors of both, for the same reason: free electrons carry energy as well as charge.
Current flows because of a potential difference; thermal energy flows because of a temperature difference. The equations have the same form:
I = frac{V}{R} with R = frac{ρ L}{A} frac{ΔQ}{Δt} = k A frac{ΔT}{Δx} (thermal conduction, B.1)
Long, thin conductors resist both flows; thick, short ones let them through easily.

14Electrical power

P = frac{W}{t} = frac{q V}{t} => P = I V # substitute V = IR or I = V/R => P = I^{2} R = frac{V^{2}}{R} E = P t = V I t

Unit: the watt, 1 W = 1 J s⁻¹. The brightness of a lamp depends on the power dissipated in it. The kilowatt-hour is an energy unit: 1 kW h = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.

Example 14.1A lamp is labelled “12 V, 36 W”. Calculate the current at its working pd and its resistance at that pd.
I = frac{P}{V} = frac{36}{12} => I = 3.0 A R = frac{V^{2}}{P} = frac{144}{36} => R = 4.0 Ω
Which formula?In series (same I), P = I²R: the bigger resistor dissipates more power. In parallel (same V), P = V²/R: the smaller resistor dissipates more power.

15Series circuits

  • The current is the same through every component.
  • The supply pd is shared: V = V₁ + V₂ + …, in proportion to the resistances.
  • Total resistance: R_{total} = R_{1} + R_{2} + R_{3} + … (always larger than the largest).
# same current I through each V = V_{1} + V_{2} = I R_{1} + I R_{2} frac{V}{I} = R_{1} + R_{2} => R_{total} = R_{1} + R_{2}

Adding another lamp in series increases the total resistance, so the current falls and every lamp gets dimmer. If one lamp breaks, the circuit is broken and all go out.

16Parallel circuits

  • The pd is the same across each branch.
  • The current splits at a junction: I = I₁ + I₂ + … (charge is conserved). The smallest resistance takes the largest current.
  • Total resistance: frac{1}{R_{total}} = frac{1}{R_{1}} + frac{1}{R_{2}} + …; the total is less than the smallest branch resistance.
# same pd V across each branch I = I_{1} + I_{2} = frac{V}{R_{1}} + frac{V}{R_{2}} frac{I}{V} = frac{1}{R_{1}} + frac{1}{R_{2}} => frac{1}{R_{total}} = frac{1}{R_{1}} + frac{1}{R_{2}}
ShortcutsTwo resistors: R = R₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂). n identical resistors R in parallel: R/n. Adding a branch always lowers the total resistance and increases the current from the supply. Homes are wired in parallel so each appliance gets the full supply pd and can be switched separately.

17Combination circuits

Reduce the circuit step by step: replace each set of parallel resistors with its equivalent, then add series resistors; find the total current; then work back out to find the current and pd in each part.

Example 17.1A 12 V supply (no internal resistance) is connected to R₁ = 4.0 Ω in series with a parallel pair R₂ = 6.0 Ω and R₃ = 12 Ω. Determine the current in R₃.
R_{23} = frac{6.0 × 12}{6.0 + 12} = 4.0 Ω R_{total} = 4.0 + 4.0 = 8.0 Ω I = frac{12}{8.0} = 1.5 A V_{1} = 1.5 × 4.0 = 6.0 V V_{23} = 12 - 6.0 = 6.0 V => I_{3} = frac{6.0}{12} = 0.50 A

18Internal resistance

Real cells have an internal resistance r: the chemicals inside oppose the current. Some of the energy given to each coulomb is transferred to internal energy inside the cell (the cell warms up). These are the “lost volts” Ir.

ε = I (R + r) => V = ε - I r (terminal pd)
  • Open circuit (I = 0): V = ε. A high-resistance voltmeter across a cell on its own reads (almost) its emf.
  • Short circuit (R = 0): I = ε/r, the maximum current; V = 0 and all the energy heats the cell.
  • V–I graph: V = −rI + ε is a straight line with y-intercept ε and gradient −r. The x-intercept is the short-circuit current ε/r.
Example 18.1A cell of emf 1.5 V and internal resistance 0.50 Ω is connected to a 2.5 Ω resistor. Calculate the current, the terminal pd and the lost volts.
I = frac{ε}{R + r} = frac{1.5}{3.0} => I = 0.50 A V = I R = 0.50 × 2.5 => V = 1.25 V lost volts = I r = 0.25 V
Cells in series and parallelSeries (same way round): emfs add and internal resistances add. A cell reversed subtracts its emf. Parallel (n identical cells): the emf stays ε but the internal resistance is r/n, so more current can be delivered and the cells last longer.

19Variable resistors, sensors and potential dividers

  • NTC thermistor: a semiconductor whose resistance falls as temperature rises (negative temperature coefficient). Heating gives more electrons enough energy to break free: n increases, which outweighs the increased lattice vibration.
  • LDR (light-dependent resistor): a semiconductor whose resistance falls as light intensity increases. Photons give electrons enough energy to break free, so n increases.
  • Potentiometer: a resistor with a sliding contact (wiper). Used as a potential divider, it gives any output pd from 0 up to the supply pd.
Potential dividerTwo resistors in series across a supply Vin share the pd in the ratio of their resistances. The output across R₂ is
V_{out} = V_{in} × frac{R_{2}}{R_{1} + R_{2}}
Sensor circuitsPut the sensor in the divider. If the output is taken across a fixed resistor that is in series with an LDR, the output rises as light increases (the LDR takes a smaller share). Take the output across the LDR instead and the output rises when it gets dark: this can switch on a street light. A thermistor in the same position makes a fire or frost alarm. Design both in Sensors & dividers.
Example 19.1A 9.0 V supply is connected across a 10 kΩ resistor in series with an LDR. In daylight the LDR is 2.0 kΩ; in the dark it is 200 kΩ. Calculate the pd across the LDR in each case.
V_{day} = 9.0 × frac{2.0}{10 + 2.0} = 1.5 V V_{dark} = 9.0 × frac{200}{10 + 200} = 8.6 V

20Misconceptions

MisconceptionThe physics
A battery stores charge and pushes it out.A battery stores chemical energy. The charge is already in the wires; the battery gives each coulomb energy. A “flat” battery has as much charge as a new one.
Current is used up by the lamps.Current is the same all round a series loop (charge is conserved). Energy is transferred in the lamps.
Voltage flows through a circuit.Pd is measured between two points. Current flows; pd causes it to flow.
Electrons travel round a circuit very fast.Their drift speed is about 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹. The signal (the field) travels at nearly the speed of light.
Resistance is the gradient of an I–V graph.R = V/I at the point. For an ohmic conductor this equals 1/gradient of the I–V line; for others it does not.
Adding resistors in parallel increases the resistance.Each new branch is a new path, so the total resistance falls.
A short circuit has a high resistance.A short circuit is a path of almost zero resistance: the current becomes very large.

21Linking concepts and nature of science

Linking questionsHow does B.5 connect with the rest of the course?
  • A circuit as a system: energy is given to charge in the source and transferred in the components, and the total is conserved. Like the energy balance of the atmosphere (B.2) or a heat engine (B.4), we track inputs, transfers and outputs; the Sankey idea applies too, with useful output and wasted heating.
  • Fields: a pd creates an electric field in the wire (E = V/L for a uniform wire) that pushes the carriers. Compare with gravitational fields (D.1): a charge falling through a pd gains energy qV, as a mass falling through a height gains mgh.
  • Heating explained by other areas: resistive heating is the kinetic theory of B.1 and B.3: the electrons increase the random kinetic energy of the lattice ions, so the internal energy and temperature rise. Thermal and electrical conductivity share the same free-electron explanation.
  • Particle model (NOS): the free-electron model (Drude, 1900) is a model: it treats electrons as tiny balls bouncing off ions. It explains Ohm’s law, resistance and heating, but not everything (for example, why some metals conduct better than others, or superconductivity); quantum theory refines it. Scientists use simple models while they work, and know their limits.
  • Conventions (NOS): conventional current (+ to −) was agreed before the electron was discovered. It still works perfectly, because positive charge moving one way is equivalent to negative charge moving the other.

23One-page summary

B.5 Current and circuits (SL & HL) · one-page summary

Charge and carriers

  • q = Ne, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; charge is conserved
  • Metals: free electrons in a lattice of + ions
  • Semiconductors: few carriers; more when hot or lit
  • Electrolytes: + and − ions move opposite ways
  • Gases: ionisation by collision; plasma

Current and drift

  • I = Δq/Δt; 1 A = 1 C s⁻¹
  • Random motion ≈ 10⁶ m s⁻¹, net flow zero
  • I = nAvq; drift ≈ 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹
  • Signal at ≈ speed of light
  • Same current all round a series loop

Pd and emf

  • V = W/q; 1 V = 1 J C⁻¹
  • emf: energy given per C by the source
  • pd: energy taken per C by a component
  • Primary, secondary, solar cells
  • Ammeter in series (R ≈ 0); voltmeter in parallel (R ≈ ∞)

Resistance

  • R = V/I (at the point, not the gradient)
  • R = ρL/A; ρ in Ω m
  • Metal: R rises with T (lattice vibrations)
  • Ohmic: I ∝ V at constant T
  • Filament lamp: curve, R rises

Power and circuits

  • P = IV = I²R = V²/R; E = Pt
  • Series: R = R₁ + R₂, same I, V shared
  • Parallel: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂, same V, I splits
  • Rparallel < smallest branch

Cells and sensors

  • ε = I(R + r); V = ε − Ir
  • V–I graph: intercept ε, gradient −r
  • Short circuit I = ε/r
  • NTC thermistor and LDR: R falls
  • Vout = VinR₂/(R₁ + R₂)

Top 8 mistakes to avoid

  1. Current is not used up.
  2. A battery stores energy, not charge.
  3. Pd is across, current is through.
  4. Electrons drift slowly; the signal is fast.
  5. R = V/I, not the gradient.
  6. Use the diameter correctly: A = πd²/4.
  7. Reciprocal at the end for parallel.
  8. Terminal pd = ε − Ir, not ε.

What is potential?

Why work done per unit charge?The work W needed to bring a charge to a point depends on how big the charge is: twice the charge needs twice the work. Dividing by q removes this, leaving a number that describes the point itself, whatever charge you use to test it:
V = frac{W}{q} (unit: J C^{-1} = volt)
It is like height in a gravitational field: lifting 2 kg needs twice the work of lifting 1 kg, but the work per kilogram (gh) is the same, so it describes the place, not the object.

⚠ Misconception alert: the cell does not store charge or “send out” electrons. The charge is already in every part of the circuit. The cell gives each coulomb energy: 6 V means 6 J for every coulomb.

🔋 Primary cell

Alkaline or zinc–carbon. Chemical → electrical energy. The reaction cannot be reversed: once used up, the cell is thrown away (recycled). Cheap, long shelf life, good for low-power devices such as clocks and remote controls.

🔄 Secondary cell

Lithium-ion, lead–acid, NiMH. Chemical → electrical when discharging; electrical → chemical when recharging. Can be reused hundreds of times and deliver large currents (phones, electric cars). More expensive; capacity falls with age.

☀️ Solar cell

A semiconductor junction. Light → electrical energy: photons free electrons and the junction pushes them one way. Renewable, no fuel, no emissions while working. About 0.6 V per cell; output depends on light; efficiency about 20%.

Internal resistance

A real cell hides a resistance inside it. Change the load, take readings, plot the V–I graph and find ε and r from the intercept and gradient. Try a short circuit and an open circuit, and combine cells in series and parallel.

Follow one coulomb round the circuit: the potential at every point

1 · Where does r come from?

Charge has to pass through the cell itself: through the electrolyte (ions moving) and the electrodes. These materials resist the flow like any conductor. We model a real cell as an ideal emf ε in series with a small resistor r. You cannot take r out of the cell: it is spread through the cell’s materials.

2 · Emf and terminal pd

Emf ε: the energy given to each coulomb by the cell’s chemical reaction. Terminal pd V: the energy each coulomb delivers to the external circuit. They differ by the energy transferred inside the cell: V = ε − Ir.

3 · Lost volts, Ir

The pd across r. It is the energy per coulomb transferred inside the cell (the cell gets warm). It grows with the current, so a smaller load R means a bigger current, more lost volts and a lower terminal pd.

4 · The two extremes

Open circuit (no current): nothing is lost, so V = ε. That is how an emf is measured. Short circuit (R = 0): I = ε/r, the largest possible current, V = 0 and all the energy heats the cell.

5 · Why it matters

A car’s starter motor draws over 100 A, so the lost volts are large and the headlights dim. Lithium cells have a very small r, so a short circuit gives a huge, dangerous current. School EHT supplies are built with a very large r so that the current is always safe. As a battery ages its r increases.

6 · The equations

ε = I(R + r) · V = IR = ε − Ir
power from the cell = εI · useful power = I²R · wasted power = I²r
efficiency = R ÷ (R + r)

Common mistakes

✗ “The emf falls when a current flows.” The emf is fixed by the chemistry of the cell. What falls is the terminal pd.
✗ “A voltmeter across a cell always reads its emf.” Only when no current flows (open circuit). With a load connected it reads V = ε − Ir.
✗ “Internal resistance is a separate part inside the battery.” It is the resistance of the cell’s own materials. The resistor r is only a model.

IB practical: determining the emf and internal resistance of a cell

Method

  1. Connect the cell in series with a variable resistor, an ammeter and a switch. Connect a voltmeter across the cell’s terminals.
  2. Close the switch, record I and V, then open the switch again straight away.
  3. Change the resistance and repeat for at least 6 readings over as wide a range of current as possible.
  4. Plot V (y-axis) against I (x-axis) and draw the line of best fit.

Analysis

V = −rI + ε has the form y = mx + c.
y-intercept = ε (the pd when I = 0).
gradient = −r, so r = −gradient.
x-intercept = ε/r, the short-circuit current.
Find the gradient from a large triangle drawn on the line, not from two data points.

Errors and improvements

The cell warms up and r changes if current flows for long: open the switch between readings. A cell with a very small r gives a nearly flat line: add a small known resistor in series with the cell and subtract it from the result. Use a high-resistance (digital) voltmeter so it takes almost no current.
