1 · How the marks actually work

E.2 is examined at Higher Level only. Marks are lost by listing observations without saying what the wave model predicted, by mixing eV and J, by confusing intensity with frequency, and by using λ = h/mv for a photon.

How a DP markscheme thinks

  • One mark per marking point. “Max 4” means more points than marks.
  • “Explain why the wave model fails”: each mark pairs a wave prediction with the observation that contradicts it.
  • Error carried forward (ECF): a wrong photon energy used correctly for the stopping potential still earns the later marks.
  • “Show that”: give the steps and one more significant figure than printed.
  • Graph questions: gradient and intercepts each carry a mark, with units.

Command terms you will meet

  • State / Define: work function, threshold frequency, stopping potential.
  • Describe: the observations, or how an experiment is done.
  • Explain: link each observation to photons (one photon, one electron).
  • Calculate / Determine: working, then an answer with a unit.
  • Sketch: I–V or Ek–f graphs, with intercepts marked.
  • Compare: photoelectric effect and Compton scattering, referring to both.

Do and don’t in the exam hall

✔ DO

  • Work in eV when you can: Vs in volts equals Ek,max in eV.
  • Convert eV to J (× 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) before using h in J s.
  • Say “one photon gives all its energy to one electron”.
  • Use λ = h/p for particles and E = hc/λ for photons.
  • For an accelerated electron, use p = √(2meV) (non-relativistic).

✘ DON’T

  • Don’t say brighter light gives faster electrons: it gives more of them.
  • Don’t forget that “maximum” KE means the least tightly bound (surface) electrons.
  • Don’t say photons slow down in Compton scattering: their wavelength increases.
  • Don’t say Δλ depends on the target material or incident wavelength.
  • Don’t claim large objects have no de Broglie wavelength: it is just far too small to detect.

1APaper 1A: multiple choice

About 2 minutes per question. E.2 multiple-choice questions test what changes what (intensity or frequency), graph features and quick ratio reasoning for de Broglie and Compton.

Fast methods

  • Photon energy in eV ≈ 1240 / λ(nm).
  • Ek,max (eV) = photon energy (eV) − φ (eV), and Vs is the same number in volts.
  • Accelerated electron: λ ∝ 1/√V, so 4 × V halves λ.
  • Compton: Δλ = 2.43 pm × (1 − cos θ): 0 at 0°, 2.43 pm at 90°, 4.86 pm at 180°.

Traps the wrong options use

  • “Higher intensity → larger stopping potential.”
  • “Below the threshold, electrons are emitted after a delay.”
  • Gradient of Vs–f graph = h (it is h/e).
  • “The proton has the longer wavelength” at the same speed (it has more momentum, so shorter).
  • “Compton shift is largest at 90°” (it is largest at 180°).

Worked example

Light of wavelength 310 nm falls on a metal with work function 2.5 eV. What is the stopping potential?

  1. Photon energy ≈ 1240 / 310 = 4.0 eV.
  2. Ek,max = 4.0 − 2.5 = 1.5 eV.
  3. Vs = 1.5 V. The traps: 4.0 V (forgot φ), 6.5 V (added φ), 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁹ V (left the answer in joules).

1BPaper 1B: data-based questions

20 marks, about 40 minutes. E.2 data comes from three classic experiments: stopping potential against frequency (Millikan), electron diffraction rings against accelerating voltage, and Compton shift against angle.

Read a straight line

  • Vs against f: gradient = h/e, f-intercept = f₀, V-intercept = −φ/e.
  • Δλ against (1 − cos θ): gradient = h/(mₑc).
  • Ring radius against 1/√V: straight line through the origin, since r ∝ λ ∝ 1/√V.
  • Use a large triangle on the best-fit line, not two data points.

Uncertainty and agreement

  1. Draw steepest and shallowest lines through the error bars to get the gradient’s uncertainty.
  2. Convert the gradient to h, keeping the percentage uncertainty.
  3. The result agrees if 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s lies inside the range.

Linearising

If r ∝ 1/√V, plot r against 1/√V, or show that r√V is constant across the table. Stating “r decreases as V increases” only shows a trend, not the relationship.

Worked example: Planck’s constant from two readings

Stopping potential is 0.89 V at 6.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz and 2.13 V at 9.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz. Determine h and φ.

  1. eVs = hf − φ, so h = eΔVs/Δf = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1.24 / 3.0 × 10¹⁴ = 6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ J s.
  2. φ = hf − eVs = 6.61 × 10⁻³⁴ × 6.0 × 10¹⁴ − 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 0.89 = 2.54 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 1.6 eV.
  3. Check: the threshold f₀ = φ/h = 3.8 × 10¹⁴ Hz is below both frequencies used.

2Paper 2: structured and extended answers

E.2 Paper 2 questions combine an explanation (why the wave model fails, why Compton scattering needs photon momentum) with a short calculation chain.

Weak answer vs top answer

Explain how the photoelectric effect provides evidence for the particle nature of light. [4]

1 / 4

Light is made of photons, so electrons come out when the light is bright enough.

Wrong (brightness is not the condition), and no observation is linked to a wave prediction.
4 / 4

The wave model predicts that any frequency should eject electrons if the light is intense enough, but below a threshold frequency no electrons are emitted at all MP1. It predicts a delay while energy builds up in dim light, but emission is instantaneous MP2. It predicts brighter light gives faster electrons, but Ek,max depends only on frequency; intensity changes only the number of electrons MP3. All of this is explained if light arrives as photons of energy hf, each giving all its energy to one electron: Ek,max = hf − φ MP4.

Each mark pairs a prediction with the observation that breaks it, then the photon explanation.

Explain why Compton scattering provides evidence that photons have momentum. [3]

1 / 3

The X-rays bounce off the electrons and come out different.

No mention of wavelength, momentum conservation or the angle dependence.
3 / 3

X-rays scattered by electrons have a longer wavelength than the incident X-rays, and the shift depends on the scattering angle MP1. A classical wave would make the electrons oscillate and re-emit at the same wavelength MP2. The shift Δλ = (h/mₑc)(1 − cos θ) is exactly what conservation of energy and momentum gives for a collision between a photon of momentum h/λ and an electron, so photons carry momentum MP3.

Observation, failed classical prediction, and the particle-collision explanation.

Photoelectric chain

  1. E = hc/λ (in J), then ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ for eV.
  2. Ek,max = E − φ.
  3. Vs = Ek,max / e.
  4. vmax = √(2Ek,max / mₑ), with E in J.

Matter waves and Compton

  1. Accelerated electron: Ek = eV, p = √(2mₑeV), λ = h/p.
  2. Particle with known speed: λ = h/(mv).
  3. Compton: λ′ = λ + (h/mₑc)(1 − cos θ).
  4. Electron recoil energy = hc/λ − hc/λ′.

7 · Before the exam

Equations for E.2

E = hf = hc / λ Ek,max = hf − Φ Ek,max = eVs λ = h / p = h / mv Δλ = hmₑc(1 − cos θ)

Constants and numbers to know

  • h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s · c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹
  • e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C · mₑ = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg
  • hc = 1.99 × 10⁻²⁵ J m ≈ 1240 eV nm
  • h/(mₑc) = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m (the Compton wavelength)
  • Work functions are a few eV (about 2–5 eV for metals)
  • A 150 V electron has λ ≈ 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m, about an atomic spacing

Final checklist

  • I can describe the four photoelectric observations and why the wave model fails.
  • I can define work function, threshold frequency and stopping potential.
  • I can use Ek,max = hf − Φ and eVs = Ek,max, in J and eV.
  • I can sketch and interpret Ek–f and I–V graphs.
  • I can explain wave–particle duality and electron diffraction.
  • I can calculate de Broglie wavelengths for electrons and other particles.
  • I can explain Compton scattering and use the Compton equation.
