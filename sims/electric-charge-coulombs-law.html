D.2 · Charge is quantized

Electric Charge & Coulomb's Law

Every charged object carries a whole-number multiple of one elementary charge, and any two charges push or pull on each other with a force set by Coulomb's inverse-square law. This part is Standard Level core — needed by SL and HL students alike.

SL · Standard Level

Charge comes in indivisible packets. An object becomes charged only by gaining or losing electrons, so its net charge is always an exact multiple of the elementary charge e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

Charge an object, electron by electron

Protons are locked in the nuclei and never move. Only electrons transfer, so charge changes in steps of one e at a time — it can never be a fraction.

Net charge

Q = n·ee = 1.60 × 10−19 C

Quantization and conservation

Because n must be a whole number, Q is quantized: 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, and so on — never 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Robert Millikan's oil-drop experiment first measured this smallest step directly.

Charge is also conserved: it is never created or destroyed, only moved. When a rod gains electrons the cloth loses exactly the same number, so the total stays constant.

Question
A metal sphere is charged by adding electrons to it. Its final charge is −3.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ C. (e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)
(a) State what is meant by saying charge is quantized. [1]
Charge can only exist in whole-number multiples of the elementary charge e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C — never a fraction of it.
(b) Calculate the number of excess electrons on the sphere. [2]
n = |Q|e=3.2 × 10⁻¹⁸1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹=20 electrons
(c) The sphere is then connected to earth. State and explain its final charge. [2]
Zero (neutral). The 20 excess electrons flow to earth until the sphere again has equal numbers of protons and electrons.
SL · Standard Level

An object can be charged in three ways — by friction, by conduction (contact) and by induction. In every case no charge is created: electrons simply move. Pick a method to watch it happen.

Charging by friction

Drag the cloth back and forth across the rod — or click Rub — to transfer electrons. The more you rub, the more charge builds up.

What is happening

Rubbing two different insulators together transfers electrons from one surface to the other.

Electrons leave the material that holds them loosely and collect on the one that attracts them more strongly. Here the polythene rod gains electrons and becomes negative; the cloth loses them and becomes positive.

The charges are equal and opposite — total charge is conserved. It works only on insulators, because the charge stays where it is deposited.

Question
A polythene rod is rubbed with a dry woollen cloth. Afterwards the rod carries a charge of −6.4 nC.
(a) Explain, in terms of electrons, how the rod became negatively charged. [2]
Electrons are transferred from the cloth to the rod. The rod now has more electrons than protons, so it carries a net negative charge.
(b) State the charge on the cloth, giving a reason. [2]
+6.4 nC. Charge is conserved, so the cloth loses exactly as many electrons as the rod gains — the two charges are equal and opposite.
(c) Calculate the number of electrons transferred to the rod. [2]
n = Qe=6.4 × 10⁻⁹1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹=4.0 × 10¹⁰
About 4.0 × 10¹⁰ electrons were transferred.

Charging a sphere by induction

Step through the four stages:

What is happening

A charged object brought near a conductor pushes the conductor's free electrons to one side — it adds no charge of its own.

Earthing the conductor while the rod is in place lets those repelled electrons escape to the ground. Removing the earth, then the rod, leaves a net charge.

The induced charge is opposite in sign to the inducing charge, and the conductor is never touched by the rod.

Question
A negatively charged rod is held close to an isolated metal sphere that rests on an insulating stand.
(a) Describe how the charge is distributed on the sphere while the rod is held near it. [2]
Free electrons in the sphere are repelled to the far side, which becomes negative; the near side is left positive. The sphere's total charge is still zero.
(b) The sphere is now earthed briefly while the rod stays in place. Explain what happens to the electrons. [2]
The electrons repelled to the far side flow through the earth connection to the ground, so the sphere is left with fewer electrons than protons.
(c) The earth connection is removed, then the rod is taken away. State and explain the final charge on the sphere. [2]
Positive. The sphere is short of electrons, so it holds a net positive charge — opposite in sign to the rod and gained without any contact.

Charging by conduction (contact)

Step through contact and separation:

What is happening

A charged conductor is touched to a neutral conductor. Because the excess electrons repel one another, some flow across until both are at the same potential.

The two objects end up with charge of the same sign as the original. Two identical conductors share the total charge equally.

Here a −12 nC sphere shares with an identical neutral sphere, leaving −6 nC on each after they separate.

Question
An isolated metal sphere X carries a charge of −12 nC. An identical uncharged sphere Y is brought up, touched to X, and then separated.
(a) Explain why electrons move when the two spheres touch. [2]
The excess electrons on X repel one another, and X is at a different potential from Y, so electrons flow from X to Y until both spheres are at the same potential.
(b) Calculate the charge on each sphere after they are separated. [2]
each = −122=−6 nC
Identical spheres share the conserved charge equally, giving −6 nC on each.
(c) Sphere Y is now connected to earth. State its final charge and explain. [2]
0 nC (neutral). Its excess electrons flow to earth until Y again has equal numbers of protons and electrons.
SL · Standard Level

Two point charges exert equal and opposite forces on each other. The size of that force grows with each charge and falls off with the square of their separation — Coulomb's law.

Two charges, one force law

The force

F = kq1q2r2k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²

Reasoning · why the force is inverse-square

Reveal one idea at a time. While Step 2 is showing, the graph highlights how the force shrinks as r grows.

Step 1 · the field spreads out
one charge sends its influence out in all directions
The influence of q₁ spreads over the surface of an imaginary sphere centred on it.
Step 2 · area grows as r²
sphere area = 4πr²
Doubling r spreads the same influence over four times the area, so the force at any point is four times weaker.
Step 3 · scale with both charges
F ∝ q₁q₂
A bigger charge on either body scales the force in direct proportion.
Step 4 · Coulomb's law
F = kq1q2r2
Combining the two gives the inverse-square force law, with k the Coulomb constant.
Question
Two small spheres carry charges of +2.0 µC and +8.0 µC and are held 30 cm apart in air. (k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²)
(a) Calculate the electric force between the spheres. [3]
F = kq1q2r2=(8.99 × 10⁹)(2.0 × 10⁻⁶)(8.0 × 10⁻⁶)(0.30)2≈1.6 N
The force is repulsive, since both charges are positive.
(b) State how the force on the +2.0 µC sphere compares with the force on the +8.0 µC sphere. [1]
They are equal in size and opposite in direction — Newton's third law. The force does not depend on which charge is larger.
(c) The separation is now doubled to 60 cm. Determine the new force. [2]
F → F22=1.64=0.40 N
Doubling r quarters the force (inverse-square).
SL · Standard Level

The medium between the charges matters. Coulomb's constant hides the permittivity of free space, and filling the gap with a material weakens the force by the material's relative permittivity.

Same charges, different medium

Fixed example: +3.0 µC and +5.0 µC held 20 cm apart. Only the medium between them changes.

Force in the medium

k = 14πε0,  Fmedium = Fvacuumεr

Permittivity of free space

The Coulomb constant is k = 1 / (4πε₀), where ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C² N⁻¹ m⁻² is the permittivity of free space. A larger permittivity means the medium opposes the field more, so the force is smaller.

Relative permittivity εᵣ compares a medium to a vacuum. Water (εᵣ ≈ 80) weakens the Coulomb force dramatically, which is why many salts separate into ions when dissolved.

Question
Two charges experience a force of 0.80 N when held a fixed distance apart in a vacuum.
(a) State the force between them when they are placed in air (εᵣ ≈ 1.0). [1]
Essentially unchanged at about 0.80 N, because air has a relative permittivity very close to 1.
(b) The gap is now filled with a liquid of relative permittivity 2.5. Calculate the new force. [2]
F = Fvacuumεr=0.802.5=0.32 N
(c) Explain, in terms of permittivity, why the force changed. [2]
The liquid has a higher permittivity than a vacuum. Since k = 1/(4πε) and F = kq₁q₂/r², a larger permittivity ε gives a smaller force.
SL · Standard Level

Five original problems on quantization, conservation and Coulomb's law. Try each before revealing the worked solution.

Worked Example · Counting electrons
After rubbing, a plastic rod carries a charge of −4.8 nC. How many excess electrons has it gained?

The magnitude of the charge is a whole number of elementary charges, n = |Q| / e.

n = 4.8 × 10⁻⁹1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹=3.0 × 10¹⁰ electrons

The rod is negative, so it has gained this many electrons; the cloth lost exactly the same number.

Worked Example · Sharing by contact
Two identical metal spheres carry +8.0 nC and −2.0 nC. They are touched together and separated. What is the charge on each?

Charge is conserved, and identical spheres share the total equally.

Qtotal = +8.0 + (−2.0) = +6.0 nC ⟹ each = +6.02=+3.0 nC

Both spheres end up positive because the net charge of the system is positive.

Worked Example · Coulomb's law
Point charges of +3.0 µC and +5.0 µC sit 20 cm apart in air. Find the force between them.
F = kq1q2r2=(8.99 × 10⁹)(3.0 × 10⁻⁶)(5.0 × 10⁻⁶)(0.20)2
F = (8.99 × 10⁹)(1.5 × 10⁻¹¹)0.040≈3.4 N, repulsive

Both charges are positive, so the force pushes them apart.

Worked Example · Effect of a medium
The two charges above are now immersed in water, for which εᵣ ≈ 80. What is the new force?
Fwater = Fairεr=3.480≈4.2 × 10⁻² N

Water's high relative permittivity cuts the force to less than a hundredth of its value in air.

Worked Example · Inverse-square reasoning
Without any numbers: if the separation between two fixed charges is tripled, what happens to the Coulomb force?

Force depends on 1/r². Tripling r multiplies r² by 9.

F → F32=F9

The force drops to one-ninth, not one-third — the inverse-square law is steep.

SL · Standard Level

The classic traps on charge and Coulomb's law. Each card pairs the wrong idea with the correct physics.

"An object can carry any amount of charge."

Charge is quantized. Every net charge is a whole-number multiple of e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

Q = n e, with n a whole number.
"Rubbing creates charge from nothing."

Friction only moves electrons from one surface to the other. The two objects gain equal and opposite charge; the total is unchanged.

Charge is conserved, never created.
"Positive charging means protons were added."

Protons stay bound in nuclei. An object turns positive by losing electrons, not by gaining protons.

Only electrons move.
"Two charges must touch to push on each other."

The Coulomb force acts at a distance through the electric field. No contact is needed.

Force acts across the gap via the field.
"Doubling the distance halves the force."

Force follows 1/r². Doubling r makes the force four times weaker, not two.

Double r → force ÷ 4.
"A denser medium increases the Coulomb force."

A higher relative permittivity reduces the force, because F ∝ 1/ε.

Higher εᵣ → smaller force.
