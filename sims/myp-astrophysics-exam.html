1 · How the marks actually work

Each criterion is scored from 0 to 8, strand by strand. In astrophysics most marks are lost through vague words (“the galaxy goes red”), mixing up similar ideas (meteor and meteorite, mass and weight, a light-year as a time), and standard-form mistakes in calculations.

Command terms in this unit

  • Define: the exact meaning. “A light-year is the distance light travels in one year.”
  • Distinguish: say how each one differs (star against planet, emission against absorption).
  • Describe: the stages in order (the life cycle of a star, the Big Bang).
  • Explain: cause → physics → effect (tilt → angle of sunlight → seasons).
  • Analyse: match spectral lines and state which elements are present and which are not.
  • Calculate: equation → standard form → substitution → answer with unit.
  • Discuss / Evaluate: several implications, both sides, then a justified conclusion.

What a top-band (7–8) answer looks like

A 7–8
Explain and apply ideas to unfamiliar situations (a new exoplanet, a mystery spectrum, a satellite design) and solve multi-step problems (red shift → speed → distance → age).
B 7–8
Plan an investigation using a model (orbit on a string, craters in flour, lamp spectra): a testable hypothesis with a scientific reason; how to vary, measure and control; sufficient, repeated data; safety.
C 7–8
Process data correctly (means, gradients such as H₀ or T²/r³), interpret trends using physics, spot anomalies and evaluate the method and the limitations of the model.
D 7–8
Explain how astronomy solves a problem, then discuss and evaluate implications from several angles (economic, environmental, ethical, cultural, political) with precise terms and full references.

Do and don't in the exam hall

✔ DO

  • Write numbers in standard form: 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s, 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m.
  • Convert time to seconds before using v = 2πr/T or d = ct.
  • Say “wavelength increases” for red shift and link it to moving away.
  • Name the force: gravity provides the centripetal force in an orbit.
  • For seasons, mention tilt, angle of sunlight and day length.
  • In D answers, use real examples: JWST, Chandrayaan-3, GPS, space debris.

✘ DON'T

  • Don't use the light-year as a unit of time.
  • Don't say seasons happen because Earth gets closer to the Sun.
  • Don't say there is no gravity in space: astronauts are in free fall.
  • Don't say stars “burn”: they release energy by nuclear fusion.
  • Don't say the Big Bang was an explosion at a centre: space itself expands.
  • Don't say red-shifted light becomes red: the whole spectrum shifts.

AKnowing & Understanding

Everything examiners can ask in an MYP Earth-and-space unit, not just one school’s checklist: the Solar System, celestial bodies, gravity and orbits, the Earth–Moon–Sun system, the night sky, distances, stars, spectra, the Doppler effect, the Big Bang and telescopes.

The Solar System

  • The Sun (a star) and everything held in orbit by its gravity.
  • Inner planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars. Small, rocky, few moons.
  • Outer planets: Jupiter, Saturn (gas giants), Uranus, Neptune (ice giants). Large, many moons and rings.
  • The asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter; the Kuiper belt lies beyond Neptune (Pluto, a dwarf planet).
  • Mnemonic: My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Noodles.

Gravity, orbits & satellites

W = m gmass never changes
v = 2πrTorbital speed
  • Gravity provides the centripetal force that keeps a moon, planet or satellite in orbit.
  • Closer orbit → stronger gravity → faster speed and shorter period.
  • Geostationary: period 24 h, above the equator, for TV and communications. Low Earth orbit (~90 min): imaging, ISS, weather.
  • Kepler’s third law: T² ∝ r³ (T in years, r in AU: T² = r³).

Day, seasons, Moon & eclipses

  • Day and night: Earth spins once every 24 h (west to east).
  • Year: one orbit of the Sun, 365.25 days.
  • Seasons: the 23.5° tilt changes the angle of sunlight and the day length. Not distance!
  • Phases: half the Moon is always lit; over 29.5 days we see different amounts of the lit half.
  • Solar eclipse: Moon between Sun and Earth (new moon). Lunar eclipse: Earth between Sun and Moon (full moon).
  • We always see the same face because the Moon rotates once per orbit.

Constellations & the night sky

  • A constellation is a pattern of stars seen from Earth; its stars are often at very different distances.
  • Stars rise in the east and set in the west, turning 15° per hour around the celestial pole, because Earth rotates.
  • Polaris (Dhruva tara) sits above the North Pole, so it barely moves. Its height above the horizon equals your latitude.
  • Different constellations appear in different seasons because Earth orbits the Sun.
  • Cultures read the sky differently: nakshatras, Matariki, the Emu in the Sky.

Distances in space

d = c tc = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s
  • Astronomical unit: 1 AU = 1.5 × 10¹¹ m (Earth to Sun, light takes about 8.3 min).
  • Light-year: the distance light travels in a year, 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m. It is a distance.
  • Look-back time: we see a galaxy 2 million ly away as it was 2 million years ago.
  • Parallax (extension): d (parsec) = 1 / p (arcsec); 1 pc = 3.26 ly.

Stars and their life cycle

  • A nebula collapses under gravity; the core heats up until hydrogen fuses into helium.
  • Main sequence: gravity inwards is balanced by gas and radiation pressure outwards.
  • Colour shows temperature: red ≈ 3000 K, yellow (Sun) ≈ 5800 K, blue > 10 000 K.
  • Massive stars live shorter lives and end in a supernova, which makes heavy elements.

Spectra

  • Continuous: all colours (a hot, dense object). Emission: bright lines (a hot gas). Absorption: dark lines on a continuous spectrum (light through cooler gas).
  • Each element has its own line pattern, a fingerprint. Matching a star’s dark lines with laboratory spectra shows which elements it contains.

Doppler effect: red & blue shift

Δλλ = vcfor v much less than c
  • Source moving away: waves stretched, longer λ, red shift.
  • Source moving towards us: waves squashed, shorter λ, blue shift.
  • The bigger the shift, the faster the motion. Same physics as a passing siren.

The Big Bang & the expanding universe

v = H₀ dH₀ ≈ 70 km/s per Mpc
age ≈ 1H₀ ≈ 13.8 billion years
  • The universe began ~13.8 billion years ago, extremely hot and dense; space has been expanding and cooling ever since.
  • Evidence 1: galaxies are red-shifted, and more distant ones recede faster (Hubble’s law).
  • Evidence 2: the cosmic microwave background, leftover radiation from the hot early universe, detected in every direction.
  • Evidence 3: the amounts of hydrogen and helium (about 3:1 by mass) match predictions.

Telescopes & the EM spectrum

  • Astronomers observe the whole EM spectrum: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, UV, X-ray, gamma.
  • The atmosphere absorbs UV, X-rays and much IR, so those telescopes go into space (Hubble, JWST, Chandra).
  • Ground telescopes are built on mountains: less air, less turbulence and less light pollution.
  • Infrared sees through dust and catches red-shifted light from the earliest galaxies.

Weak answer vs top-band answer

Question: Explain how the spectra of distant galaxies support the Big Bang theory. [4]

1 / 4

The galaxies go red because they are moving, which shows the Big Bang happened.

“Go red” is vague; no mention of wavelength, direction of motion, distance or expansion.
4 / 4

The absorption lines in galaxy spectra are shifted to longer wavelengths than in the laboratory: red shift ✓. By the Doppler effect, this means the galaxies are moving away from us ✓. More distant galaxies have bigger red shifts, so they recede faster (Hubble’s law) ✓. So the universe is expanding; tracing it back, everything began from a hot, dense state, as the Big Bang predicts ✓.

Observation, Doppler, the distance link and the conclusion.

BInquiring & Designing

We can't experiment on stars, so Criterion B questions in astrophysics use models and observations. Be ready to plan any of these:

Investigations you could be asked to design

  • Orbit model: whirling bung on a string. How does the radius (or force) affect the period?
  • Impact craters: marbles into flour. How do drop height, mass or angle affect crater diameter?
  • Star colour and temperature: a filament lamp at different voltages, viewed with a diffraction grating.
  • Inverse-square law: how light intensity falls with distance from a bulb (why distant stars look dim).
  • Seasons: a torch at different angles on squared paper (area lit) or on solar cells.
  • Light pollution: star counts at different distances from a city.
  • Expanding universe: dots on a balloon or an elastic band.

B i–ii · Research question & hypothesis

RQ

How does the angle of a torch beam from the vertical (0°, 15°, 30°, 45°, 60°) affect the light intensity measured by a light sensor at a fixed distance of 30 cm?

Hypothesis

If the angle increases, then the measured intensity will decrease, because the same light energy is spread over a larger area. This is why winter sunlight, arriving at a low angle, heats the ground less.

B iv · A full, safe method (orbit model)

  1. Thread a string through a glass tube; tie a rubber bung to the top and a 100 g mass hanger to the bottom.
  2. Measure the radius from the tube to the bung; mark the string with a paper clip just below the tube.
  3. Whirl the bung horizontally so the clip stays just below the tube (constant radius).
  4. Time 10 revolutions; divide by 10. Repeat 3 times; find the mean.
  5. Repeat for 5 radii (0.20–0.80 m); plot T² against r.

Safety

  • Wear goggles; tie the bung on tightly.
  • Keep a clear space of 2 m; whirl above head height.
  • Never look at the Sun, even through a telescope or filter you made yourself.

Weak plan vs top-band plan

Task: Formulate a hypothesis for the crater investigation. [3]

1 / 3

The higher you drop the marble, the bigger the crater.

A trend, but no scientific reason and no measurable detail.
3 / 3

If the drop height increases from 20 to 100 cm, then the crater diameter will increase ✓ because more gravitational potential energy is converted to kinetic energy (mgh = ½mv²), so the marble hits faster ✓ and transfers more energy, pushing aside more flour, just as faster meteorites make larger craters ✓.

A measurable prediction, the physics and a link to the real world.

CProcessing & Evaluating

Astronomy data comes as tables of distances, periods, speeds and wavelengths. Show one full sample calculation, choose axes that give a straight line, and connect the gradient to the physics.

C i · Present & process

  • Units in every column header; consistent significant figures.
  • Processed columns: mean period, T², r³, v from Δλ/λ, H₀ = v/d.
  • Graph: title, labelled axes with units, sensible scales, line of best fit through the origin if the physics predicts it.
  • Straight-line tricks: T² against r³ (Kepler), v against d (Hubble), intensity against 1/d² (inverse square).

C ii–iii · Interpret & discuss the hypothesis

Top band

The speed of the galaxies rose from 1500 km/s at 20 Mpc to 18 000 km/s at 260 Mpc. The points lie close to a straight line through the origin, so v ∝ d. The gradient, H₀ = 69 km/s per Mpc, gives an age of about 978 / 69 = 14 billion years, close to the accepted 13.8 billion years, which supports the hypothesis that the universe is expanding uniformly.

DReflecting on the impacts of science

Criterion D asks how astronomy and space science are applied, what their implications are, and how they have shaped people’s ideas and identity. Use precise terms and reference your sources.

The four strands

  • D i: explain how the science solves a problem (GPS uses d = ct; spectra reveal what stars are made of; weather satellites warn of cyclones).
  • D ii: discuss and evaluate implications: economic, environmental, social, ethical, cultural, political.
  • D iii: precise terminology: celestial body, spectrum, absorption line, Doppler effect, red shift, geostationary.
  • D iv: document sources (author, year, title, URL, date accessed) with in-text citations.

Good D topics for this unit

  • Is space exploration worth the cost? (ISRO’s low-cost missions)
  • Space debris and the Kessler syndrome
  • Satellite mega-constellations against dark skies
  • Light pollution and dark-sky reserves (Hanle, Ladakh)
  • Satellites for climate monitoring and disaster warning
  • Mining the Moon and asteroids: who owns space?
  • Astronaut health on a mission to Mars
  • How Copernicus, Galileo and Hubble changed our view of ourselves
  • Indigenous and cultural astronomy

A structure for “Discuss / Evaluate”

  1. Explain the science in one or two sentences.
  2. Benefit with a real example and data.
  3. Limitation or risk with an example.
  4. A different angle: ethical, cultural, political or environmental.
  5. A conclusion that weighs them up: “On balance… because…”.

Use linking phrases: however, on the other hand, consequently, this suggests that, overall.

Weak answer vs top-band answer

Question: Discuss the implications of launching thousands of internet satellites. [5]

2 / 5

They give everyone internet, which is good, but they might crash into each other.

Two points, but undeveloped; no example, no other angle and no conclusion.
5 / 5

Satellites in low Earth orbit relay signals to ground terminals, bringing broadband to remote villages and disaster zones where cables are impossible ✓. However, their reflected sunlight leaves bright streaks in telescope images, harming astronomy and hiding the night sky that many cultures value ✓. Thousands of extra objects also raise the risk of collisions and space debris (Kessler syndrome) ✓. Ethically, a few private companies are taking up shared orbits with little international regulation ✓. On balance, the social benefits are large, but they should come with darker satellite coatings, de-orbit rules and fair global regulation ✓.

The science, a benefit, two risks, an ethical angle and a justified conclusion.
