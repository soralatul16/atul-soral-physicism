A.4 · Higher Level

Rotational mass and τ = Iα

Mass resists a push; the moment of inertia I resists a twist. Build I from scratch as Σmr², meet the standard-shape formulas, and watch a resultant torque spin a body up through Newton's second law for rotation, τ = Iα.

HL · Higher Level
Rotational inertia from the ground up

How hard is it to start something spinning? Not just how much mass it has — but where that mass sits relative to the axis. A single particle of mass m a distance r from the axis contributes m r² to the body's reluctance to spin. Add that up over every particle and you have the moment of inertia, I = Σ m r².

The r² is everything. Move a chunk of mass twice as far from the axis and it resists turning four times as much. That is why a figure skater, a flywheel and a diver all control their spin by moving mass toward or away from the axis — and why I, not mass alone, is the quantity that matters once things rotate.

Build I from two masses

A light rod carries two point masses, spinning about the central axis. Each bar in the readout is that mass's m r² share; together they make I = Σ m r². Slide a mass outward and watch its contribution grow with the square of distance.

Derivation · I = Σ m r²

Follow one particle through Newton's law, then add up the whole body.

Step 1 · one particle, tangential push
F = m a = m (r α)
A particle of mass m sits a distance r from the axis. A tangential force gives it a tangential acceleration a = rα (from Part 2).
Step 2 · torque on that particle
τ = F r = (m r α) r = m r² α
Multiply the force by its distance r to get the torque. The particle's "resistance to angular acceleration" is the factor m r².
Step 3 · add every particle
τtotal = (Σ mi ri²) α
A rigid body shares one α. Sum the torques and the bracket is a single number for the whole body.
Step 4 · define the moment of inertia
I = Σ m r²unit kg m²
That bracket is the moment of inertia. It plays the role mass plays in straight-line motion — and it is set by the mass distribution.

The working

I = Σ m r² = m₁r₁² + m₂r₂²
Question
Two point masses are fixed to a light rod that rotates about its centre: 3.0 kg at 0.20 m and 2.0 kg at 0.50 m.
(a) Calculate the moment of inertia of the system. [2]
I = Σmr² = (3.0)(0.20²) + (2.0)(0.50²) = 0.12 + 0.50 =0.62 kg m²
(b) The 2.0 kg mass is moved out to 1.0 m. State the new moment of inertia. [2]
I = 0.12 + (2.0)(1.0²) = 0.12 + 2.0 =2.12 kg m²
Doubling that mass's distance quadrupled its contribution (0.50 → 2.0 kg m²).
(c) Explain why distance matters more than mass for the moment of inertia. [1]
Because each contribution is m r²: mass enters linearly, but distance is squared, so moving mass outward raises I far faster than adding mass does.
HL · Higher Level
The booklet formulas

Real bodies are not two point masses — they are continuous. Summing m r² over a continuous shape is a job for calculus, so the IB provides each formula when you need it. Every one has the same form, I = (coefficient) × M R², where the coefficient captures how the mass is spread about the axis.

The coefficient tells the whole story. A ring keeps all its mass at the rim, so its coefficient is 1 — the largest possible. A disc drops to ½, a sphere to ⅖, because more and more mass sits close to the axis where r² is small. Compare them below at equal mass and size.

Compare the shapes

Pick a shape to spin it about its axis; the size slider grows or shrinks it. The bars compare I for every shape at the same M and size — so the only thing changing between bars is the coefficient, i.e. the mass distribution.

Reading the formulas · I = c M R²

You are given these — here is how to read and trust them.

Step 1 · the ring is exact from Σmr²
Iring = Σ m r² = (Σm) R² = M R²
Every scrap of a thin ring sits at the same radius R, so r² = R² factors out of the sum — no calculus needed. Coefficient = 1.
Step 2 · solid disc
Idisc = ½ M R²
A disc has mass spread from the centre out to R. Averaging r² over that area gives ½R², so the coefficient drops to ½.
Step 3 · solid sphere & rods
sphere ⅖MR²,rod-centre 1⁄12 ML²,rod-end ⅓ML²
The sphere bunches mass near the centre (⅖). A rod about its end has mass farther out than about its centre, so ⅓ > 1⁄12.
Step 4 · what the coefficient means
I = c M R²0 < c ≤ 1
The coefficient c is just the mass-weighted average of (r/R)². Mass at the rim pushes c toward 1; mass near the axis pulls it down.

The working

I = c M R²unit kg m²
Question
A solid disc and a thin ring each have mass 1.5 kg and radius 0.20 m.
(a) Calculate the moment of inertia of each about its central axis. [2]
disc: ½MR² = ½(1.5)(0.20²) =0.030 kg m²
ring: MR² = (1.5)(0.20²) =0.060 kg m²
(b) The ring's I is exactly double the disc's. Explain why. [2]
The ring holds all its mass at R, so every particle contributes the maximum r² = R². The disc spreads mass inward, where r² is smaller, averaging to half — hence ½MR² against MR².
(c) A 0.80 kg rod of length 1.2 m is spun about one end. Find its moment of inertia. [2]
I = ⅓ML² = ⅓(0.80)(1.2²) = ⅓(0.80)(1.44) =0.38 kg m²
HL · Higher Level
The rotational F = ma

A resultant force gives a mass a linear acceleration: F = ma. A resultant torque gives a body an angular acceleration — and the quantity that resists is the moment of inertia. Put them together and you get Newton's second law for rotation: τ = Iα, where τ is the resultant (average) torque.

Rearranged, α = τ / I. The same torque spins up a low-I body briskly but a high-I body sluggishly — exactly as the same force accelerates a light object faster than a heavy one. Set a torque and a moment of inertia below and watch the body respond.

Torque in, spin-up out

The flywheel is driven by the torque you set and resists with its moment of inertia. The gauge shows the angular acceleration α = τ / I; the wheel then spins up at that rate. Raise I and the same torque produces a gentler α.

Derivation · τ = Iα

Pick up exactly where Tab 1 left off and name the result.

Step 1 · one particle
τi = mi ri² α
From Tab 1: each particle needs a torque m r² α to share the body's angular acceleration α.
Step 2 · sum over the body
τnet = Σ mi ri² α = (Σ mi ri²) α
Every particle turns with the same α (rigid body), so α comes outside the sum.
Step 3 · recognise I
Σ mi ri² = I
That sum is just the moment of inertia from Tabs 1–2.
Step 4 · Newton's second law for rotation
τnet = I ατ = average torque
Compare F = ma. Torque replaces force, I replaces mass, α replaces a. Rearrange for α = τ / I.

The working

τ = Iα ⟹ α = τI
Question
A wheel of moment of inertia 0.25 kg m² is acted on by a resultant torque of 0.60 N m.
(a) Calculate its angular acceleration. [2]
α = τI = 0.600.25 =2.4 rad s⁻²
(b) Starting from rest, how fast is it spinning after 5.0 s? [2]
ω = ωi + αt = 0 + (2.4)(5.0) =12 rad s⁻¹
(using the Part 2 equation ω = ωi + αt).
(c) The torque is produced by a force of 8.0 N on the rim. Find the wheel's radius. [2]
τ = Fr ⟹ r = τF = 0.608.0 =0.075 m

Two laws, one shape

Newton's second law comes in two matching flavours. In a straight line, F = ma: force drives, mass resists, acceleration results. Around an axis, τ = Iα: torque drives, moment of inertia resists, angular acceleration results. The equations are identical in form — only the symbols change.

Push the cart and twist the wheel below with their own "effort" and "reluctance". The cart accelerates at a = F/m; the wheel at α = τ/I. Line them up and the analogy stops being a slogan and becomes something you can see.

Side by side

Left: a block on a track, accelerating at a = F/m. Right: a wheel, spinning up at α = τ/I. Match F to τ and m to I and the two systems march in perfect step — that is the analogy made literal.

The dynamics dictionary

Each rotational quantity is the straight-line one with mass replaced by I.

Inertia
mass m ↔ moment of inertia I
What resists a change in motion. For rotation it depends on where the mass is, not just how much.
Cause of acceleration
force F ↔ torque τ
A force changes linear motion; a torque — a force times its lever arm — changes rotation.
The second law
F = m a ↔ τ = I α
Same structure: (cause) = (inertia)(acceleration). Divide to get a = F/m and α = τ/I.
What comes next
p = m v ↔ L = I ω,½mv² ↔ ½Iω²
Momentum and kinetic energy carry over too — the subject of Part 4.

The working

a = Fm,α = τI
Question
A 4.0 kg block feels a 12 N force; a wheel of I = 4.0 kg m² feels a 12 N m torque.
(a) Find the linear acceleration of the block and the angular acceleration of the wheel. [2]
a = F/m = 12/4.0 = 3.0 m s⁻²,  α = τ/I = 12/4.0 = 3.0 rad s⁻²
Numerically equal because F:m matches τ:I.
(b) State which rotational quantity plays the role of mass, and why it is not simply the mass. [2]
The moment of inertia I. Unlike mass, it also depends on how that mass is distributed about the axis (I = Σmr²), so the same object can have different I about different axes.
(c) The wheel's mass is pushed outward so I doubles. What happens to α for the same torque? [1]
α halves (to 1.5 rad s⁻²), since α = τ/I and I has doubled.
HL · Higher Level
Reshaping the resistance

Because I = Σ m r² depends on where the mass sits, you can change a body's moment of inertia without adding or removing a single gram — just move the mass. Slide two equal masses along a rod and the body's I rises with the square of their distance from the axis.

That matters because α = τ / I. Hold the driving torque fixed and the same twist produces a smaller angular acceleration as the masses move out. And when two bodies are joined on one shaft, their moments of inertia simply add: Itotal = I₁ + I₂. Explore both effects below.

Move the mass, change α

Two 1.0 kg masses sit a distance d either side of the axis, so I = 2 m d². Slide them outward and watch I climb while the same torque spins the rod up ever more slowly — a live reading of α = τ / I.

Derivation · how I changes

Two equal masses on a light rod, then a note on joining bodies.

Step 1 · the moment of inertia
I = m d² + m d² = 2 m d²
Each mass contributes m d² (Tab 1). With both at distance d, the total is 2 m d².
Step 2 · it scales with d²
d → 2d ⟹ I → 4 I
Double the distance and I quadruples — the square again. Mass position dominates.
Step 3 · the effect on α
α = τI ⟹ α falls as I rises
For a fixed torque, pushing the masses out makes the rod harder to spin up: α drops.
Step 4 · coupled bodies add
Itotal = I₁ + I₂ + …
Rigidly join bodies on one axis and their moments of inertia simply add — because Σmr² runs over all the particles at once.

The working

I = 2 m d²,α = τI
Question
Two 0.50 kg masses sit 0.30 m either side of an axis on a light rod. A constant torque of 1.2 N m is applied.
(a) Find the moment of inertia and the angular acceleration. [2]
I = 2md² = 2(0.50)(0.30²) = 0.090 kg m²
α = τ/I = 1.2 / 0.090 =13 rad s⁻²
(b) The masses are slid out to 0.60 m. Find the new angular acceleration for the same torque. [2]
I = 2(0.50)(0.60²) = 0.36 kg m² (×4),  α = 1.2 / 0.36 =3.3 rad s⁻²
Four times the I, one quarter the α.
(c) A disc of I = 0.10 kg m² is fixed to the same shaft. State the combined moment of inertia (masses at 0.60 m). [1]
Moments of inertia add: Itotal = 0.36 + 0.10 = 0.46 kg m².

Five problems that run from building I out of point masses to driving a coupled system with torque. Attempt each before the full solution.

Worked Example 1 · point masses
Three 0.40 kg masses sit on a light rod at 0.10 m, 0.30 m and 0.50 m from one end, which is the axis. Find the moment of inertia about that end.
I = Σmr² = 0.40(0.10² + 0.30² + 0.50²)
= 0.40(0.01 + 0.09 + 0.25) = 0.40(0.35) =0.14 kg m²

Common mass factors out; the farthest particle (0.50 m) dominates thanks to r².

Worked Example 2 · a flywheel
A solid disc flywheel has mass 12 kg and radius 0.25 m. Find its moment of inertia about its central axis.
I = ½MR² = ½(12)(0.25²) = ½(12)(0.0625) =0.38 kg m²

Use the provided disc coefficient ½. If it were a ring of the same M and R it would be 0.75 kg m² — double.

Worked Example 3 · spinning it up
A torque of 2.5 N m acts on a wheel of moment of inertia 0.50 kg m², initially at rest. (a) Find α. (b) Find ω after 3.0 s. (c) Find the angle turned.
α = τ/I = 2.5 / 0.50 = 5.0 rad s⁻²
ω = ωi + αt = 0 + (5.0)(3.0) = 15 rad s⁻¹
Δθ = ½αt² = ½(5.0)(3.0²) = 22.5 rad

τ = Iα gives α; the Part 2 equations then handle the motion.

Worked Example 4 · which axis?
A uniform rod has mass 0.60 kg and length 0.90 m. Find its moment of inertia (a) about its centre, (b) about one end, and comment.
centre: I = 1⁄12 ML² = (0.60)(0.90²)/12 =0.0405 kg m²
end: I = ⅓ ML² = (0.60)(0.90²)/3 =0.162 kg m²

The end value is four times the centre value: shifting the axis to the end puts far more of the rod's mass at large r.

Worked Example 5 · a coupled system
A disc (I = 0.20 kg m²) and a ring (I = 0.30 kg m²) are fixed on the same axle. A 1.0 N m torque drives them. Find the angular acceleration.
Itotal = I₁ + I₂ = 0.20 + 0.30 = 0.50 kg m²
α = τItotal = 1.00.50 =2.0 rad s⁻²

Joined bodies share one α, so add the moments of inertia first, then apply τ = Iα once.

HL · Higher Level

The traps that cost marks on moment of inertia and τ = Iα. Each is a small habit — fix it once and it sticks.

"Moment of inertia is just the mass of the object."

I depends on how the mass is distributed about the axis, not only how much there is. I = Σmr².

Two objects of equal mass can have very different I if their mass sits at different radii.
"A body has one fixed moment of inertia."

I is defined about a particular axis. Change the axis and r changes for every particle, so I changes.

A rod has I = 1⁄12 ML² about its centre but ⅓ ML² about its end — same rod, different axis.
"Doubling the radius doubles the moment of inertia."

Each contribution is m r², so doubling r multiplies that term by four, not two.

For I = cMR², doubling R (at fixed M) makes I four times larger.
"Newton's law for rotation is τ = mα."

Rotation resists with the moment of inertia, not plain mass: τ = Iα. Mass alone can't tell you the twisting response.

Always use I = Σmr² (or the provided shape formula) in τ = Iα.
"The units of moment of inertia are kilograms."

From I = Σmr², the units are mass × distance² = kg m².

If an answer comes out in kg or kg m, a factor of r has been dropped.
"A hollow and a solid cylinder of equal M and R spin up the same."

The hollow one keeps mass at the rim (≈ MR²); the solid one carries mass inward (½MR²), so it has less I and spins up faster for the same torque.

Distribution decides I — and therefore α = τ/I — even at identical mass and radius.
