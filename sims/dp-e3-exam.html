1 · How the marks actually work

E.3 marks are lost through forgetting to subtract background, unbalanced decay equations (missing the antineutrino or neutrino), confusing activity with count rate, and, at HL, unit mismatches in λ (s⁻¹ against years). How a DP markscheme thinks

  • One mark per marking point (MP1, MP2…). “Max 3” means more points are listed than marks available.
  • Error carried forward (ECF): a wrong λ used correctly in A = λN still earns the later marks.
  • “Show that”: give the steps and one more significant figure than the printed answer.
  • OWTTE: the idea counts, but key words (“random”, “spontaneous”, “ionises”) must be there.
  • Decay equations need both A and Z correct, and the (anti)neutrino, for full marks.

Command terms you will meet

  • State: a name or value (the type of decay, the half-life).
  • Define: the precise meaning (half-life, activity, isotope).
  • Identify: pick from the data (which radiation the source emits).
  • Explain: reasons. Each “because” is usually a mark.
  • Calculate / Determine: working, then an answer with a unit.
  • Deduce: reach a conclusion from the data, and say how.
  • Outline: a brief account (how the neutrino was predicted).
  • Suggest: a reasoned proposal (which isotope for a tracer).

Do and don’t in the exam hall

✔ DO

  • Subtract background from every count rate before finding a half-life.
  • Balance A and Z, and write ν̄ for β⁻ and ν for β⁺.
  • Count whole half-lives when the time is a multiple of T½; otherwise use N = N₀e−λt (HL).
  • Use the same time unit throughout: λ in s⁻¹ with activity in Bq.
  • Say decay is random (cannot predict which nucleus) and spontaneous (not affected by conditions).

✘ DON’T

  • Don’t say a sample is “gone” after two half-lives: a quarter remains.
  • Don’t confuse count rate (what the detector records) with activity (decays per second in the source).
  • Don’t say γ emission changes A or Z.
  • Don’t say α is the most dangerous outside the body: it is stopped by skin.
  • Don’t use binding energy per nucleon where total binding energy is asked.

1APaper 1A: multiple choice

About 2 minutes per question. E.3 multiple-choice questions test decay equations, half-life fractions, the properties of α, β and γ and reading the binding-energy curve.

Fast methods

  • α: A − 4, Z − 2. β⁻: Z + 1. β⁺: Z − 1. γ: no change.
  • For a chain, count the α decays from the change in A, then use Z to find the β decays.
  • Half-lives: fraction left = (½)ⁿ; 1/8 left means 3 half-lives.
  • Fraction decayed = 1 − fraction left.

Traps the wrong options use

  • Fraction remaining instead of fraction decayed (or the reverse).
  • Swapping β⁻ and β⁺ changes in Z.
  • α as “most penetrating” and γ as “most ionising”.
  • “Half-life depends on the mass of the sample” or on temperature.
  • Total binding energy (largest for uranium) vs per nucleon (largest near iron).

Worked example

²³⁸₉₂U decays to ²²⁶₈₈Ra. How many α and β⁻ decays happen?

  1. A falls by 12, so there are 12 / 4 = 3 α decays.
  2. 3 α decays alone would give Z = 92 − 6 = 86, but Z = 88, so there are 2 β⁻ decays (each raises Z by 1).
  3. The traps: 3 α and 0 β (forgot to check Z), or 2 β⁺ (wrong sign).

1BPaper 1B: data-based questions

20 marks, about 40 minutes. E.3 data: count rates against time or absorber, with background; for HL, ln graphs and long half-lives from activity and mass.

Counting uncertainty

Decay is random. A count of N has an uncertainty of about ±√N.

  • Fractional uncertainty = 1/√N, so a count of 100 is ±10%, and 10 000 is ±1%.
  • Count for longer or use a stronger source to reduce it.
  • Background matters most when the corrected count is small.

Half-life from data

  1. Subtract background from every reading.
  2. Read the time for the corrected count to halve, from several starting points, and average.
  3. HL Or plot ln(corrected count) against t: gradient = −λ, then T½ = ln 2 / λ.

Identifying radiation from absorbers

  • Paper stops it → α is present.
  • A few mm of aluminium stops it → β.
  • Still above background after aluminium → γ (reduced by cm of lead).
  • Always compare with the background count, not with zero.

Worked example HL: a half-life too long to watch

1.00 g of uranium-238 has an activity of (1.24 ± 0.03) × 10⁴ Bq. Determine its half-life.

  1. N = (1.00 / 238) × 6.02 × 10²³ = 2.53 × 10²¹ nuclei.
  2. λ = A / N = 4.90 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹, so T½ = ln 2 / λ = 1.41 × 10¹⁷ s = 4.48 × 10⁹ years.
  3. The 2.4% uncertainty in A gives (4.5 ± 0.1) × 10⁹ years. The accepted 4.47 × 10⁹ years is inside the range, so it agrees.

2Paper 2: structured and extended answers

Typical chains: decay equation → energy released → which radiation for an application → half-life or (HL) activity calculation. Explanations are marked on the number of correct physics links.

Weak answer vs top answer

Explain why a β-emitter, rather than an α- or γ-emitter, is used to monitor the thickness of paper in a factory. [3]

1 / 3

Because β is in the middle, so it is the best one to use.

No reason linked to thickness, and nothing about why α and γ fail.
3 / 3

α particles would be completely absorbed by the paper, so no count would get through MP1. γ rays would pass through almost unaffected, so small changes in thickness would not change the count MP2. β particles are partly absorbed, so the count rate changes noticeably with thickness and can be used to adjust the rollers MP3.

Each radiation is judged against what the detector must notice.

HL Outline how the energy spectrum of β⁻ particles provided evidence for the neutrino. [3]

1 / 3

The electrons have lots of different energies, so another particle was discovered.

The observation is there, but not why it was a problem or what the new particle does.
3 / 3

If β⁻ decay produced only the daughter nucleus and an electron, conservation of energy and momentum would give every electron the same energy, as in α decay MP1. Instead the electrons have a continuous range of energies, from zero up to a maximum equal to the energy released MP2. So a third, neutral, almost massless particle (the antineutrino) must carry away the rest of the energy and momentum MP3.

Prediction, observation, conclusion.

Energy released in decay

  1. Δm = mass of parent − total mass of products (use atomic masses; for β⁻ the electron masses cancel).
  2. E = Δm × 931.5 MeV.
  3. In α decay almost all of E goes to the α particle, because it has far less mass than the daughter nucleus.

Decay law HL

N = N₀ e−λtA = λN = A₀ e−λtT½ = ln 2 / λ

t = (1/λ) ln(A₀/A). Check: is the answer between the right whole numbers of half-lives?

7 · Before the exam

Equations for E.3

E = m c² 1 u = 931.5 MeV c⁻² EB = Δm c² Δm = Zmp + Nmn − mnucleus fraction left = (½)t / T½ N = N₀ e−λt HL A = λN HL T½ = ln 2 / λ HL

Facts to know

  • α: ⁴₂He, stopped by paper or a few cm of air, strongly ionising.
  • β⁻: electron (plus ν̄), stopped by a few mm of aluminium, moderately ionising.
  • β⁺: positron (plus ν), annihilates with an electron.
  • γ: photon, reduced by several cm of lead, weakly ionising.
  • Strong force: attractive to about 3 × 10⁻¹⁵ m, repulsive below about 0.5 × 10⁻¹⁵ m, same for p and n.
  • 1 Bq = 1 decay s⁻¹ · NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ · 1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s
  • mp = 1.007276 u · mn = 1.008665 u · m(¹H atom) = 1.007825 u

Final checklist

  • I can write α, β⁻, β⁺ and γ equations, with neutrinos.
  • I can calculate mass defect, binding energy and energy released.
  • I can sketch the EB/A curve and explain its shape.
  • I can describe the strong force and why large nuclei are unstable.
  • I can compare the penetration and ionisation of α, β and γ.
  • I can correct for background and find a half-life from data.
  • HL I can use the N–Z graph to predict the type of decay.
  • HL I can explain discrete α and γ energies and the continuous β spectrum.
  • HL I can use N = N₀e−λt and A = λN, and measure long and short half-lives.
