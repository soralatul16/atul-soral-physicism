A.4 · Higher Level

Describing how things spin

Straight-line motion has displacement, velocity and acceleration. Spinning motion has exact partners: angular displacement, angular velocity and angular acceleration. Meet all three, then watch them obey the same equations of motion you already know.

HL · Higher Level
Measuring a turn

To describe spinning, we first need a clean way to say how far something has turned. Degrees work, but physics prefers the radian — because it ties the angle directly to the arc the body sweeps out. One radian is the angle at which the arc length equals the radius, so a full turn (circumference 2πr) is exactly 2π radians.

That single choice makes everything downstream simple: arc length is just s = rθ, and the whole machinery of rotational motion falls out without stray conversion factors. The quantity θ here is the angular displacement — the rotational twin of straight-line displacement — and like all our quantities it carries a sense: counter-clockwise or clockwise.

Sweeping out an angle

Drag the moving spoke or use the slider to sweep the angle. The highlighted arc is the distance a point on the rim travels — watch s = rθ update. The faint gold tick marks exactly one radian (≈ 57.3°), where the arc first equals the radius.

Derivation · the radian & s = rθ

Define the angle from the arc it subtends, then read off the arc-length rule.

Step 1 · define the radian
θ =srθ in radians
The angle in radians is the arc length divided by the radius. Both are lengths, so a radian is a pure number — it has no unit.
Step 2 · one full turn
θfull =2πrr=2π rad
Go all the way round and the arc is the whole circumference 2πr. So 360° = 2π rad, which gives the conversion 1 rad ≈ 57.3°.
Step 3 · rearrange for arc length
s = r θ
Multiply through by r. The distance a rim point travels is simply radius × angle (in radians) — the bridge between turning and travelling.
Step 4 · angular displacement Δθ
Δθ = θf − θi
A change in angle is the angular displacement — the rotational partner of Δx. Counter-clockwise is taken as positive, clockwise as negative.

The working

s = r θθ in rad
Question
A wheel of radius 0.40 m is rotated so that a point on its rim travels an arc of 0.50 m.
(a) Calculate the angular displacement in radians. [2]
θ = sr = 0.500.40 =1.25 rad
(b) Convert this angle to degrees. [1]
1.25 × 180π =71.6°
(c) The wheel now completes 3 full revolutions. State its angular displacement in radians. [1]
Each revolution is 2π rad, so Δθ = 3 × 2π = 6π ≈ 18.8 rad.
HL · Higher Level
How fast it turns

Angular velocity ω is how quickly the angular displacement changes — radians swept per second. It is the rotational twin of ordinary velocity: where v tells you metres per second along a line, ω tells you radians per second around an axis. One trip round takes a time called the period T, so ω = 2π/T = 2πf.

Here is the elegant part. Every point on a rigid body shares the same ω — the whole thing turns together. But a point far from the axis still has to cover a longer arc in that same time, so its linear speed is larger: v = rω. That is why the rim of a wheel whizzes while the hub barely moves.

One ω, many speeds

The disc spins at the rate you set. The red dot sits a distance r from the axis; its green arrow is the linear velocity v = rω, always tangent to the circle. Slide r outward and the arrow grows — same ω, faster point.

Derivation · ω = 2π/T and v = rω

Start from the definition of angular velocity, then connect it to linear speed through s = rθ.

Step 1 · define ω
ω =ΔθΔtrad s⁻¹
Angular velocity is angular displacement per unit time — exactly how fast the angle is growing.
Step 2 · one whole revolution
ω =2πT=2πf
In one period T the body turns 2π rad. Since f = 1/T, this also gives ω = 2πf.
Step 3 · bring in arc length
v =st=r ΔθΔt
The point's linear speed is arc travelled per time. Substitute s = rΔθ and r stays constant for a rigid body.
Step 4 · the link
v = r ω
Pull Δθ/Δt out as ω. Linear speed equals radius × angular velocity — bigger r, faster point, for the same ω.

The working

ω = 2πT,v = rω
Question
A fan blade completes 1 revolution every 0.25 s. A point sits 0.18 m from the axis.
(a) Calculate the angular velocity of the blade. [2]
ω = 2πT = 2π0.25 =25.1 rad s⁻¹
(b) Calculate the linear speed of that point. [2]
v = rω = (0.18)(25.1) =4.5 m s⁻¹
(c) A second point sits twice as far out. State its angular velocity and its linear speed relative to the first. [2]
Angular velocity is identical (25.1 rad s⁻¹) — the whole blade turns together. But its linear speed is double (9.0 m s⁻¹), since v = rω and r has doubled.
HL · Higher Level
Speeding up the spin

If ω itself is changing, the body has an angular acceleration α — radians per second, per second. It is the rotational twin of linear acceleration: a resultant force gives a, a resultant torque gives α. Spin a wheel up from rest and ω climbs steadily; brake it and α points the other way, bleeding ω back down.

And just as one ω gives every point its own v, one α gives every point its own tangential acceleration a = rα — the part of the acceleration that speeds the point along its arc. Farther from the axis means a larger a for the very same α.

Watch ω climb

The wheel starts at ωi and gains speed at the rate α you set. The lower graph plots ω against t — a straight line whose slope is α. A negative α tips the line downward: the wheel slows, stops, then spins the other way.

Derivation · α and a = rα

Define α from the change in ω, then differentiate v = rω to find the tangential acceleration.

Step 1 · define α
α =ΔωΔtrad s⁻²
Angular acceleration is the rate of change of angular velocity — how fast the spin rate itself is changing.
Step 2 · start from v = rω
v = r ω
A rim point's linear speed is tied to ω through the fixed radius r.
Step 3 · change over time
a =ΔvΔt=rΔωΔt
Divide the change in v by time. Because r is constant it comes straight out of the fraction.
Step 4 · tangential acceleration
a = r α
This is the tangential acceleration — it speeds the point along its path. (A spinning point also needs a separate centripetal acceleration v²/r just to keep curving.)

The working

α = ΔωΔt,a = rα
Question
A turntable speeds up uniformly from rest to 33.0 rad s⁻¹ in 4.0 s.
(a) Calculate its angular acceleration. [2]
α = ΔωΔt = 33.0 − 04.0 =8.25 rad s⁻²
(b) A speck of dust sits 0.12 m from the centre. Find its tangential acceleration. [2]
a = rα = (0.12)(8.25) =0.99 m s⁻²
(c) The power is cut and the turntable coasts to rest in 12 s. State the sign of α now and why. [1]
α is negative (a deceleration): ω is decreasing, so Δω is negative over the time interval. Its magnitude is 33.0/12 = 2.75 rad s⁻².
HL · Higher Level
The rotational equations of motion

When α is constant, the four straight-line "suvat" equations carry over wholesale — just swap each linear symbol for its angular partner. Displacement s becomes angular displacement Δθ, initial and final velocities u, v become ωi, ωf, and acceleration a becomes α. The algebra is identical because the definitions are identical.

Set the initial spin, the angular acceleration and a time below, and the predictor runs all four equations at once — showing the final angular velocity, the angle turned, and the two graphs that go with them: a straight ω–t line and a curved θ–t parabola.

Predict the motion

Scrub the time slider to move the marker along both graphs. The wheel above shows the angle turned so far; the left graph is ω against t (slope α), the right graph is θ against t (a parabola when α ≠ 0).

Deriving the four equations

Everything follows from the definition of α and the average angular velocity.

Equation 1 · from the definition of α
α = ωf − ωit ⟹ωf = ωi + αt
Rearrange the definition of angular acceleration. The final spin rate is the initial one plus the gain αt.
Equation 2 · average angular velocity
Δθ = ωi + ωf2 t
With uniform α the average of ω is just (ωi+ωf)/2. Angle turned = average angular velocity × time.
Equation 3 · substitute Eq 1 into Eq 2
Δθ = ωi + (ωi+αt)2 t ⟹Δθ = ωit + ½αt²
Replace ωf with ωi+αt and simplify. This gives the angle directly from the initial spin and α.
Equation 4 · eliminate t
t = ωf−ωiα into Eq 2 ⟹ωf² = ωi² + 2αΔθ
Substitute t from Eq 1 into Eq 2 and tidy. The time-free equation — perfect when t is neither given nor wanted.

The working

ωf = ωi + αt
Δθ = ωit + ½αt²
Question
A flywheel starts at 2.0 rad s⁻¹ and accelerates uniformly at 3.0 rad s⁻² for 4.0 s.
(a) Find its final angular velocity. [2]
ωf = ωi + αt = 2.0 + (3.0)(4.0) =14 rad s⁻¹
(b) Find the angle it turns through in that time. [2]
Δθ = ωit + ½αt² = (2.0)(4.0) + ½(3.0)(4.0²) =32 rad
(c) Check part (b) using the time-free equation. [2]
ωf² = ωi² + 2αΔθ ⟹ 14² = 2.0² + 2(3.0)Δθ
196 − 4 = 6Δθ, so Δθ = 192/6 = 32 rad ✓ — the two methods agree.
HL · Higher Level
The same physics, two languages

Rotational kinematics isn't a new subject — it's linear kinematics wearing different symbols. Every linear quantity has an angular partner, and the bridge between them is the radius: s = rθ, v = rω, a = rα. Learn the dictionary once and every suvat result you already trust becomes a rotational result for free.

A wheel rolling without slipping shows the whole dictionary at once. In one full turn (θ = 2π) it lays down an arc equal to its circumference and so advances a distance 2πr — meaning the distance rolled equals rθ, and the centre moves at exactly v = rω. Watch it below.

Rolling without slipping

The wheel turns at ω and rolls without slipping, so its centre glides at v = rω. The gold track is the distance travelled; the readouts confirm it stays equal to the arc rθ as the wheel turns.

The linear ↔ angular dictionary

Each line differentiates the one above — the same step that turns s into v into a.

Displacement
s = r θ
Distance along the arc = radius × angle. The anchor of the whole dictionary.
Velocity
v = r ω
Rate of change of each side. For a rolling wheel this is the speed of the centre.
Acceleration
a = r α
Differentiate once more. The tangential (and here, forward) acceleration.
The suvat map
x↔θ   u↔ωi   v↔ωf   a↔α   t↔t
Swap symbols and every linear equation of motion becomes its rotational twin — same algebra, different letters.

The working

s = rθ,v = rω,a = rα
Question
A bicycle wheel of radius 0.33 m rolls without slipping. Its centre moves at 6.0 m s⁻¹.
(a) Find the wheel's angular velocity. [2]
ω = vr = 6.00.33 =18.2 rad s⁻¹
(b) The bike travels 50 m. Through what angle has the wheel turned? [2]
θ = sr = 500.33 =152 rad
(about 24 revolutions, since 152 ÷ 2π ≈ 24.)
(c) The brakes give the wheel an angular deceleration of 9.0 rad s⁻². How long to stop? [2]
ωf = ωi + αt ⟹ 0 = 18.2 + (−9.0)t
t = 18.2 / 9.0 = 2.0 s.
HL · Higher Level

Five exam-style problems that stitch the whole chapter together — from radians through the equations of motion to the rolling link. Try each before revealing the full solution.

Worked Example 1 · angular velocity
A compact disc spins at 540 revolutions per minute. Find its angular velocity in rad s⁻¹ and the period of rotation.

Convert revolutions per minute to revolutions per second: 540 / 60 = 9.0 rev s⁻¹. Each revolution is 2π rad.

ω = 9.0 × 2π =56.5 rad s⁻¹
T = 1f = 19.0 =0.11 s

Always drop to rev s⁻¹ (= frequency f) first — then ω = 2πf is one clean step.

Worked Example 2 · equations of motion
A drill bit accelerates uniformly from rest to 1200 rad s⁻¹ in 2.5 s. (a) Find the angular acceleration. (b) Find the number of revolutions made during this time.
α = ωf − ωit = 1200 − 02.5 =480 rad s⁻²

For the angle, use the average angular velocity (clean since α is uniform):

Δθ = ωi+ωf2t = 0+12002(2.5) =1500 rad
revolutions = 15002π ≈239 rev
Worked Example 3 · time-free equation
A grinding wheel spinning at 60 rad s⁻¹ is switched off and decelerates uniformly at 4.0 rad s⁻². Through what angle does it turn before stopping?

Time isn't asked for, so use the time-free equation with ωf = 0 and α = −4.0 rad s⁻².

ωf² = ωi² + 2αΔθ ⟹ 0 = 60² + 2(−4.0)Δθ
Δθ = 60²8.0 = 36008.0 =450 rad

Keeping α negative (it opposes the motion) is what makes the arithmetic come out positive.

Worked Example 4 · rolling link
A car tyre of radius 0.31 m rolls without slipping. The car accelerates from 10 m s⁻¹ to 25 m s⁻¹ over 300 m. Find the tyre's angular acceleration.

Translate every linear quantity to angular using the radius:

ωi = 100.31 = 32.3,  ωf = 250.31 = 80.6 rad s⁻¹
Δθ = sr = 3000.31 = 968 rad
ωf² = ωi² + 2αΔθ ⟹ α = 80.6² − 32.3²2(968) =2.8 rad s⁻²

Equivalently you could find the linear a first and use α = a/r — same answer, since the dictionary is self-consistent.

Worked Example 5 · two-phase motion
A centrifuge accelerates from rest at 50 rad s⁻² for 6.0 s, then holds that speed for 10 s. Find the total angle turned.

Phase 1 (accelerating): use Δθ = ωit + ½αt² with ωi = 0.

Δθ₁ = ½(50)(6.0²) = 900 rad,  ωf = 50 × 6.0 = 300 rad s⁻¹

Phase 2 (constant ω): α = 0, so Δθ = ωt.

Δθ₂ = (300)(10) = 3000 rad
Δθtotal = 900 + 3000 =3900 rad

Split the motion wherever α changes, then add the angles. The handover speed from Phase 1 becomes the constant speed of Phase 2.

HL · Higher Level

The traps that cost marks in rotational kinematics. Each one is a small habit — fix it once and it stays fixed.

"Points far from the axis turn through a bigger angle."

Every point of a rigid body sweeps the same angle in the same time — that's what "rigid" means. They share one θ, one ω and one α.

Far-out points travel a longer arc (s = rθ) and move faster (v = rω), but the angle is identical.
"I can use the suvat equations with ω in rev/s."

The equations of motion only work in consistent units, and they're built on the radian. Mixing in revolutions or degrees breaks them.

Convert everything to radians and seconds first: ω in rad s⁻¹, α in rad s⁻², θ in rad.
"Angular acceleration means the wheel is speeding up."

α is just the rate of change of ω. If α opposes the spin, the wheel slows down — α is negative, a deceleration.

Pick a positive sense (usually counter-clockwise) and let the signs of ω and α tell the story.
"a = rα is the whole acceleration of a rim point."

a = rα is only the tangential part — the bit that changes the speed. A point going in a circle also needs a centripetal acceleration v²/r pointing inward.

Tangential rα speeds it up along the arc; centripetal v²/r keeps it curving. They're perpendicular.
"The radian is a unit like the metre, so it carries through formulas."

A radian is a ratio of two lengths (arc ÷ radius), so it's dimensionless. That's exactly why s = rθ gives a length in metres, not "metre-radians".

Treat "rad" as a bookkeeping label: keep it on ω and α, but it quietly disappears in s = rθ and v = rω.
"Δθ = ½(ωi+ωf)t works for any motion."

The average-velocity shortcut assumes uniform α, so ω changes linearly. If α varies (a non-constant torque), the average isn't the midpoint.

Only reach for the four equations when the angular acceleration is constant.
