02A charged particle in a uniform electric field

The key ideaIn a uniform electric field every charge feels the same constant force F = qE, in the direction of the field for a positive charge and opposite to it for a negative charge. A constant force means a constant acceleration a = frac{qE}{m}, so all the equations of uniformly accelerated motion (A.1) apply.

Case 1: moving along the field lines (accelerating a charge)

The field does work on the charge. For a charge accelerated from rest through a potential difference V:

work done by the field = gain in kinetic energy qV = frac{1}{2} m v^{2} => v = sqrt{frac{2qV}{m}}
Example 2.1An electron is accelerated from rest through 2000 V. Calculate its final speed.
v = sqrt{frac{2eV}{m_{e}}} = sqrt{frac{2 × 1.60 × 10^{-19} × 2000}{9.11 × 10^{-31}}} => 2.65 × 10^{7} m s⁻¹
The electronvolt1 eV is the energy gained by one electron accelerated through 1 V: 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. An electron accelerated through 2000 V gains 2000 eV = 2 keV, whatever its mass.

Case 2: entering at right angles to the field (deflection)

# horizontal: no force, constant velocity x = v_{x} t # vertical: constant acceleration from rest a = frac{qE}{m} = frac{qV}{md} y = frac{1}{2} a t^{2} # time in the plates, and angle on leaving t = frac{L}{v_{x}} tan θ = frac{v_{y}}{v_{x}} = frac{a t}{v_{x}}
Example 2.2The electron from Example 2.1 enters the gap between plates 5.0 cm long and 2.0 cm apart, with 200 V across them. Determine its vertical deflection as it leaves the plates.
E = frac{V}{d} = frac{200}{0.020} = 1.0 × 10^{4} V m⁻¹ a = frac{eE}{m_{e}} = frac{1.60 × 10^{-19} × 1.0 × 10^{4}}{9.11 × 10^{-31}} = 1.76 × 10^{15} m s⁻² t = frac{L}{v} = frac{0.050}{2.65 × 10^{7}} = 1.89 × 10^{-9} s y = frac{1}{2} a t^{2} = 0.5 × 1.76 × 10^{15} × (1.89 × 10^{-9})^{2} => 3.1 × 10^{-3} m (3.1 mm)
Gravity?The weight of an electron is about 9 × 10⁻³⁰ N. The electric force here is 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ N, about 10¹⁴ times bigger. For particles in these fields you can always ignore gravity, unless a question says otherwise.

Case 3: fired against the field (stopping a charge)

If a charge moves in the direction in which the field pushes it backwards, the force does negative work: the charge slows down, stops for an instant and is then accelerated back the way it came. Energy conservation decides what happens:

# work done against the field over a distance s q E s = E_{k} s = frac{E_{k}}{qE} = d × frac{E_{k}}{qV} # if qV < E_k the particle reaches the far plate with E_k − qV left # if qV > E_k it stops at s, then returns with its original speed
Example 2.3A proton with kinetic energy 2.0 keV passes through a hole into the gap between two plates 5.0 cm apart, with 3.0 kV across them opposing its motion. Determine how far it travels before stopping.
# the proton would need 3.0 keV to cross, but has only 2.0 keV: it stops s = d × frac{E_{k}}{qV} = 5.0 × frac{2.0 keV}{3.0 keV} => s = 3.3 cm
NoticeThe stopping distance depends on Ek and q, but not on the mass: an electron and a proton with the same kinetic energy stop at the same point. Try it in Electric fields → Firing against the field.

03A charged particle in a uniform magnetic field

The magnetic force on a moving charge
F = q v B sin θ
θ is the angle between the velocity and the field. The force is largest when the charge moves at 90° to the field and zero when it moves along the field (θ = 0°). A charge at rest feels no magnetic force.
Direction: always perpendicular to both v and BUse Fleming’s left-hand rule: First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current (the direction a positive charge moves), thuMb = Motion (force). For a negative charge (an electron), the conventional current is opposite to its velocity, so the force is reversed. Many textbooks use the equivalent right-hand “slap” rule: fingers along B, thumb along v of a positive charge, palm pushes in the direction of F. Practise in the Direction trainer sub-tab.

Why the path is a circle

# the magnetic force provides the centripetal force q v B = frac{m v^{2}}{r} => r = frac{m v}{q B} = frac{p}{q B} # time for one orbit T = frac{2π r}{v} = frac{2π m}{q B}
Kinetic energy stays constant (syllabus guidance)Work done = F s cos θ. The force is perpendicular to the displacement at every instant (cos 90° = 0), so the magnetic force does no work. The kinetic energy, and so the speed, stay constant. A magnetic field can steer a charge but can never speed it up or slow it down.
  • A faster or heavier particle (bigger momentum) makes a bigger circle.
  • A bigger charge or a stronger field makes a smaller circle.
  • The period T does not depend on the speed: a faster particle goes round a bigger circle in the same time. (This is how a cyclotron works.)
Example 3.1A proton moving at 2.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ enters a 0.50 T field at right angles. Calculate the radius of its path and its period.
r = frac{mv}{qB} = frac{1.67 × 10^{-27} × 2.0 × 10^{6}}{1.60 × 10^{-19} × 0.50} => 0.042 m (4.2 cm) T = frac{2π m}{qB} = frac{2π × 1.67 × 10^{-27}}{1.60 × 10^{-19} × 0.50} => 1.3 × 10^{-7} s
Example 3.2An electron (accelerated through 2000 V, so v = 2.65 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹) moves perpendicular to a field of 1.0 mT. Calculate the radius of its path.
r = frac{m_{e} v}{e B} = frac{9.11 × 10^{-31} × 2.65 × 10^{7}}{1.60 × 10^{-19} × 1.0 × 10^{-3}} => 0.15 m

Same speed, same momentum or same kinetic energy?

Exam questions compare particles in different ways. Always rewrite the radius in terms of the quantity that is the same for both particles:

r = frac{mv}{qB} = frac{p}{qB} = frac{sqrt{2 m E_{k}}}{qB}
Same for both particlesr is proportional toProton vs alpha (m = 4u, q = 2e)
speed vm/qrα = 2 rp
momentum p1/qrα = ½ rp
kinetic energy Ek√m / qrα = rp (√4 / 2 = 1)
accelerating p.d. V (Ek = qV)√(m/q)rα = √2 rp
Example 3.3An electron and a proton have the same kinetic energy and move at right angles to the same field. Determine the ratio rp / re.
frac{r_{p}}{r_{e}} = sqrt{frac{m_{p}}{m_{e}}} (same q and E_k) = sqrt{frac{1.67 × 10^{-27}}{9.11 × 10^{-31}}} => 43

Entering and leaving a field region

# if r < W: a semicircle, leaving back through the entry edge distance from entry point = 2r time in the field = frac{T}{2} = frac{π m}{qB} # if r > W: it crosses the region and is deflected by an angle φ sin φ = frac{W}{r} time in the field = frac{φ}{ω} = frac{φ r}{v} (φ in radians)
Example 3.4A proton moving at 2.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ enters, at right angles, a region 6.0 cm wide where B = 0.20 T. Determine the angle through which it is deflected and the time it spends in the field.
r = frac{mv}{qB} = frac{1.67 × 10^{-27} × 2.0 × 10^{6}}{1.60 × 10^{-19} × 0.20} = 0.104 m sin φ = frac{W}{r} = frac{0.060}{0.104} = 0.574 φ = 35.0° = 0.611 rad t = frac{φ r}{v} = frac{0.611 × 0.104}{2.0 × 10^{6}} => 3.2 × 10^{-8} s
Extension · not at 90°: a helixSplit the velocity into a part along B (v cos θ) and a part across B (v sin θ). The part along B feels no force and stays constant; the part across B makes a circle of radius frac{m v sin θ}{qB}. Together they make a helix (a spiral). Charged particles from the Sun spiral along the Earth’s field lines towards the poles, causing the aurora.

05Perpendicular (crossed) electric and magnetic fields

# undeflected when the forces balance q E = q v B => v = frac{E}{B} # the charge q cancels: it works for any charge and any mass
  • Particles faster than E/B: magnetic force wins, they bend one way.
  • Particles slower than E/B: electric force wins, they bend the other way.
  • Only particles with exactly v = E/B pass straight through a narrow exit slit.
Example 5.1A velocity selector has E = 2.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹ and B = 0.10 T. Calculate the speed of the ions that pass through undeflected. State what happens to ions with twice this speed.
v = frac{E}{B} = frac{2.0 × 10^{4}}{0.10} => 2.0 × 10^{5} m s⁻¹ # at 2v the magnetic force is doubled, so it is bigger than qE: they are deflected in the direction of the magnetic force
Mass spectrometer: what is the particle?Ions first pass through a velocity selector, so they all have the same speed v. They then enter a magnetic field alone and follow semicircles of radius r = frac{mv}{qB}. With v, q and B the same, r ∝ m: heavier isotopes make bigger semicircles. Measuring r gives the mass: m = frac{qBr}{v}. Explore this in Crossed fields → Mass spectrometer.

06Force on a current-carrying conductor

F = B I L sin θ

L is the length of wire in the field and θ is the angle between the current and the field. The force is perpendicular to both; its direction comes from the same left-hand rule (the second finger is the conventional current).

Where it comes fromA current is moving charge. If a length L of wire contains N charge carriers, each of charge q moving with drift speed v, the force on each is qvB, so the total force is F = N q v B. The current is I = frac{Nq}{t}, where t = L/v is the time for the carriers to travel the length L, so Nqv = IL, giving F = BIL. The force on a wire is the sum of the forces on its moving charges.
Example 6.1A 0.20 m wire carries 3.0 A at 30° to a uniform field of 0.050 T. Calculate the force on it.
F = BIL sin θ = 0.050 × 3.0 × 0.20 × sin 30° => 0.015 N
Measuring B with a current balanceA wire or a loop on a top-pan balance sits in a magnetic field. Switch on the current: the force BIL changes the balance reading by Δm. Then BIL = Δm g, so a graph of Δm g against I is a straight line with gradient BL. Try it in Wires → Force on a wire.

07The force between parallel current-carrying wires

# field of wire 1 at the position of wire 2 B_{1} = frac{μ_{0} I_{1}}{2π r} # force on a length L of wire 2 F = B_{1} I_{2} L => frac{F}{L} = frac{μ_{0} I_{1} I_{2}}{2π r}
Direction (syllabus guidance)Currents in the same direction attract; currents in opposite directions repel. Check it: use the right-hand grip rule to find the field of wire 1 at wire 2, then the left-hand rule for the force on wire 2.
Example 7.1Two long parallel wires 5.0 cm apart carry currents of 10 A and 20 A in the same direction. Calculate the force per metre on each wire and state its direction.
frac{F}{L} = frac{μ_{0} I_{1} I_{2}}{2π r} = frac{4π × 10^{-7} × 10 × 20}{2π × 0.050} => 8.0 × 10^{-4} N m⁻¹ # the same on both wires (Newton’s third law), and attractive
Nature of science · defining the ampereFrom 1948 to 2019 the ampere was defined by this force: the current that, flowing in two infinitely long parallel wires 1 m apart in a vacuum, gives a force of 2 × 10⁻⁷ N per metre. Since 2019 the SI defines the ampere by fixing the value of the elementary charge e instead, because charge can be counted more precisely than forces can be measured.

08Measuring the charge-to-mass ratio e/m (required practical)

The syllabus requires you to determine the charge-to-mass ratio of a charged particle from its path in a uniform magnetic field. The classic method uses a fine-beam tube:

  • An electron gun accelerates electrons through a p.d. V, so eV = frac{1}{2} m v^{2}.
  • A pair of Helmholtz coils gives a uniform field B at right angles to the beam. The tube contains low-pressure gas that glows where the electrons pass, so the circular path is visible and its radius r can be measured.
  • The magnetic force gives the centripetal force: evB = frac{m v^{2}}{r}.
# from the magnetic field v = frac{eBr}{m} # substitute into eV = ½mv² eV = frac{1}{2} m frac{e^{2} B^{2} r^{2}}{m^{2}} => frac{e}{m} = frac{2V}{B^{2} r^{2}}
Example 8.1With V = 250 V and B = 0.80 mT, the beam radius is 6.6 cm. Determine e/m.
frac{e}{m} = frac{2V}{B^{2} r^{2}} = frac{2 × 250}{(8.0 × 10^{-4})^{2} × (0.066)^{2}} => 1.8 × 10^{11} C kg⁻¹ # accepted value 1.76 × 10¹¹ C kg⁻¹
Making it an IA-quality experimentRearrange to a straight line: r^{2} = frac{2}{(e/m) B^{2}} V. Keep B fixed, vary V, and plot r² against V: the gradient is frac{2}{(e/m)B^{2}}. A graph uses all the data and shows up anomalies. Biggest uncertainty: reading the radius (parallax, the beam has a width). Do it in the e/m experiment tab.
Nature of science · J. J. Thomson, 1897Thomson measured e/m for “cathode rays” using crossed electric and magnetic fields. It was the same for every metal he used and about 1800 times bigger than for hydrogen ions. He concluded that cathode rays are tiny, universal, negatively charged particles: the electron, the first subatomic particle discovered.

Thomson’s crossed-field method

Thomson did not know the speed of his electrons, so he measured it with a velocity selector first:

  • Step 1. Apply E and B together and adjust B until the beam is undeflected: v = frac{E}{B}.
  • Step 2. Switch off E. In B alone the beam bends into an arc of radius r.
  • Step 3. Combine: frac{e}{m} = frac{v}{Br} = frac{E}{B^{2} r}.
Finding r from where the beam hits a plateIf the beam enters midway between plates a distance d apart and hits a plate a horizontal distance x from the entrance, Pythagoras gives r² = x² + (r − d/2)², so r = frac{x^{2} + (d/2)^{2}}{d}. Try it in Crossed fields → Thomson’s e/m method.

09Energy and motion in fields

  • Electric field: work done on a charge moving through a p.d. V is W = qV, which becomes kinetic energy (or comes from it). This is conservation of energy.
  • Magnetic field: the force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so W = 0 and the kinetic energy is constant.
  • In crossed fields, only the electric field can change the particle’s energy.
Using energy to find the size of a nucleusAn alpha particle fired straight at a nucleus slows down as it gets closer, because the electric repulsion does negative work. At the distance of closest approach d all its kinetic energy has become electric potential energy:
E_{k} = frac{k Q q}{d} d = frac{k Q q}{E_{k}} # 5.0 MeV alpha, gold nucleus (Z = 79): d = frac{8.99 × 10^{9} × 79e × 2e}{5.0 × 1.60 × 10^{-13}} ≈ 4.5 × 10^{-14} m
This gives an upper limit for the radius of the gold nucleus (E.1 Rutherford scattering).

10Linking questions

These come from the syllabus. Open each one to see how D.3 connects to other topics.

A.2 Circular motionWhat causes circular motion of charged particles in a field?

The magnetic force F = qvB is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it acts as a centripetal force, exactly like the tension in a string for a whirled ball or gravity for a satellite. Setting qvB = mv²/r gives r = mv/qB. (An electric field alone cannot cause uniform circular motion in a uniform field, because its force has a fixed direction.)

E.1 · E.3 · ParticlesHow can the orbital radius of a charged particle moving in a field be used to determine the nature of the particle?

r = mv/qB = p/qB. If v (or the energy) and B are known, measuring r gives the ratio q/m, and the direction of curvature gives the sign of the charge. Electrons and positrons curl tightly and in opposite senses; protons and alpha particles make bigger circles. This is how particle detectors and mass spectrometers identify particles, and how Thomson identified the electron. Try Magnetic fields → Identify the particle.

A.3 EnergyHow can conservation of energy be applied to motion in electromagnetic fields?

In an electric field, the work done qV changes the kinetic energy: qV = ΔEk (electron guns, accelerators). In a magnetic field no work is done, so Ek is constant. Combining them gives e/m = 2V/(B²r²).

E.1 AtomHow are the concepts of energy, forces and fields used to determine the size of an atom?

In Rutherford scattering, alpha particles are repelled by the electric field of the nucleus. Using energy conservation at the closest approach, Ek = kQq/d, gives the nucleus size (about 10⁻¹⁴ m), showing that the atom (about 10⁻¹⁰ m) is mostly empty space. Electric forces also hold electrons in orbit, which sets the size of the atom.

Nature of scienceHow are the properties of electric and magnetic fields represented?

With field lines (direction of the force on a positive charge or N pole; closer lines mean a stronger field), with the ⊙/⊗ notation for fields perpendicular to the page, with vectors and with equations (E = F/q, F = qvB sin θ). These are models: field lines are not real objects, but they make invisible fields easier to think about and predict.

11Nature of science and theory of knowledge

Observing the invisibleNobody has seen an electron. We infer its existence and properties (charge, mass, e/m) from the paths it makes in fields: a glowing beam, a track in a cloud chamber. How certain can knowledge be when it comes from indirect observation and models?
Models and analogiesThe motion of a charge in a uniform electric field is mathematically identical to a projectile in a gravitational field. How useful are analogies between different areas of physics, and when do they break down? (For example, there is no magnetic “projectile” analogy: magnetic force depends on velocity.)

Common exam mistakes
  • Using the left-hand rule for an electron without reversing the direction.
  • Saying the magnetic force “speeds up” or “slows down” the particle: it never does work.
  • Forgetting sin θ, or using θ measured from the wrong line (θ is between v and B, or I and B).
  • Writing r = mv/qB as a given: in “show that” or “derive” questions you must start from qvB = mv²/r.
  • Using the wrong charge or mass: an alpha particle has q = 2e and m ≈ 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.
  • Forgetting that both wires feel the same force, even with different currents.
  • Unit slips: mT, μT and cm must be converted to T and m.

15One-page summary

D.3 Motion in electromagnetic fields · one-page summary

Electric field (uniform)

  • F = qE, E = V/d, a = qE/m
  • Along the field: qV = ½mv², v = √(2qV/m)
  • Across the field: parabola. x = vt, y = ½at²
  • Against the field: stops after s = Ek/(qE)
  • The field does work: Ek changes

Magnetic field (uniform)

  • F = qvB sin θ, perpendicular to v and B
  • No work done: speed and Ek constant
  • ⟂ entry: circle, r = mv/qB = p/qB = √(2mEk)/qB
  • T = 2πm/qB (independent of v)
  • At angle θ: helix
  • Region of width W: sin φ = W/r, t = φr/v

Crossed fields

  • Undeflected when qE = qvB: v = E/B (any q, m)
  • Faster: bends towards qvB. Slower: towards qE
  • Mass spectrometer: r = mv/qB ∝ m/q
  • Thomson: e/m = E/(B²r)

Wires

  • F = BIL sin θ (θ between I and B)
  • B = μ₀I/(2πr) around a long wire
  • F/L = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πr)
  • Same direction: attract. Opposite: repel
  • Equal and opposite forces (Newton’s 3rd law)

Direction rules

  • Left hand: First finger Field, seCond Current (+ve charge), thuMb Motion (force)
  • Electron: reverse the current finger
  • Right-hand grip: thumb = current, fingers = field
  • ⊙ out of page, ⊗ into page

e/m experiment

  • eV = ½mv² and evB = mv²/r
  • e/m = 2V/(B²r²)
  • Plot r² against 2V/B²: gradient = m/e
  • %unc(e/m) includes 2 × %unc(r)
  • Accepted: 1.76 × 10¹¹ C kg⁻¹

Top 10 mistakes to avoid

  1. Using the left-hand rule for an electron without reversing.
  2. Saying a magnetic field changes the speed.
  3. Forgetting sin θ, or measuring θ from the wrong line.
  4. Using q = e for an alpha particle (it is 2e).
  5. Not converting mT, cm, keV.
  6. Thinking a faster particle takes longer per orbit.
  7. Thinking a bigger current gets a smaller force (forces on both wires are equal).
  8. Writing “parallel currents repel”.
  9. Including gravity for electrons and ions.
  10. In “show that” questions, starting from r = mv/qB instead of qvB = mv²/r.

Fleming’s left-hand rule (force)Hold the first finger, second finger and thumb of your left hand at right angles. First finger: Field (N → S). SeCond finger: Current, the direction a positive charge moves. ThuMb: Motion, the force.

Negative chargesAn electron moving to the right is a conventional current to the left. Reverse the second finger, or find the force for a positive charge and then flip it.

Right-hand grip rule (field of a wire)Right thumb along the conventional current; curled fingers give the direction of the circular field lines.

Currents and magnetic fields

A current is a stream of moving charges, so a wire carrying current in a magnetic field feels a force F = BIL sin θ. Two wires carrying currents push or pull on each other.

The physics

Helmholtz coils (N = 130 turns each, radius R = 15.0 cm) give a nearly uniform field B = (4/5)3/2 μ₀NI/R = 0.779 mT per amp.
eV = ½mv² and evB = mv²/r give r² = (m/e) × 2V/B², so a graph of r² against 2V/B² is a straight line through the origin with gradient m/e.

Safety and technique

  • The tube uses a high-voltage supply: connect everything with the supply off, and never touch terminals while it is on.
  • Darken the room so the faint beam is visible.
  • Rotate the tube so the beam is exactly perpendicular to B. If not, the beam forms a helix and the measured “radius” is wrong.
  • Read the scale with your eye in line with the beam and its reflection (mirror scale) to avoid parallax.
  • The beam has a width: read the same edge (e.g. the outside) on both sides, or read the centre of the beam.
  • Do not leave the coil current high for long: the coils heat up, their resistance rises and the current drifts.
