7.3 · Measuring sound

Frequency & Wavelength of Sound

Two measurements combine to give the speed of sound: read its frequency from a calibrated oscilloscope, find its wavelength from the nodes of a stationary wave, then multiply with v = fλ.

AS · Standard

Feed a microphone's signal into a calibrated oscilloscope — one whose time-base (time per division) is known. Measure how many divisions one complete cycle spans, convert to a period, and the frequency is simply 1/T.

Microphone → calibrated CRO

The frequency slider is what you are really trying to measure — in a real experiment you read it off the screen using the time-base, not from a dial.

From trace to frequency

T = ndiv × tbase,  f = 1T
A microphone detects a steady note on a CRO whose time-base is set to 0.50 ms per division. One complete cycle of the trace occupies 4.0 divisions.
Question
(a) Determine the period of the sound. [2]
T = 4.0 × 0.50 ms=2.0 ms = 2.0 × 10⁻³ s
(b) Calculate the frequency of the note. [2]
f = 1T=12.0 × 10⁻³=500 Hz
(c) Explain why the oscilloscope must be calibrated for this measurement to work. [1]
Because the period is found from (divisions × time-base), the time represented by each division must be known accurately — that is what calibration provides.
AS · Standard

Point a loudspeaker at a hard reflector and the outgoing and reflected waves superpose into a stationary wave. Slide a microphone along the line between them: it finds alternating loud points (antinodes) and quiet points (nodes). Adjacent nodes are exactly half a wavelength apart.

Find the nodes, find λ

Nodes (N) stay permanently still and quiet; antinodes (A) oscillate with the largest amplitude and are loudest. The microphone meter on the right responds to whatever point it sits over.

Reading off the wavelength

node spacing = λ2⇒λ = 2 × (node spacing)
A microphone moved between a loudspeaker and a reflecting board detects minima (quiet points) in the sound. The distance from the first minimum to the sixth minimum is measured as 42.5 cm.
Question
(a) State what each minimum of sound corresponds to on the stationary wave. [1]
A node — a point of permanently zero (or minimum) amplitude where the two waves cancel.
(b) Calculate the wavelength of the sound. [2]
From the 1st to the 6th minimum is 5 node spacings = 5 × (λ/2).
λ = 2 × 42.55=17.0 cm = 0.170 m
(c) State one advantage of measuring across five spacings rather than one. [1]
It reduces the percentage (fractional) uncertainty in the measured distance, giving a more precise wavelength.
AS · Standard

With the frequency from the CRO and the wavelength from the node spacing, the speed of sound follows at once from v = fλ. Combine your two measurements below.

Combine the two measurements

λ = 2 × (node spacing),  v = fλ

A real experiment would read f from the CRO trace and the node spacing from a metre rule — the sliders stand in for those readings.

The speed of sound

The experiment, and how to make it accurate

  1. Connect a signal generator to a loudspeaker and place a flat reflecting board facing it; feed the microphone into a calibrated CRO.
  2. Read the frequency from the trace (period = divisions × time-base, then f = 1/T).
  3. Move the microphone slowly along the line and mark the positions of successive nodes (minima).
  4. Measure the distance across several node spacings and divide, so one spacing = λ/2; hence λ.
  5. Calculate v = fλ; a typical value in air at room temperature is about 340 m s⁻¹.

Reducing uncertainty: measure across many spacings not one; locate each node by finding the centre of the quiet region; repeat and average; keep the microphone on the line between speaker and reflector.

In an experiment the frequency is found to be 1.1 kHz and adjacent nodes are 0.15 m apart.
Question
(a) Determine the wavelength of the sound. [1]
λ = 2 × 0.15=0.30 m
(b) Calculate the speed of sound. [2]
v = fλ = (1.1 × 10³)(0.30)=330 m s⁻¹
(c) The frequency is quoted to 2 significant figures. State the speed to an appropriate number of significant figures. [1]
To 2 s.f., matching the least precise data: 330 m s⁻¹ (i.e. 3.3 × 10² m s⁻¹).
AS · Standard

Four problems on the two measurement methods and the speed they combine to give. Try each before revealing the solution.

Worked Example · Frequency from the CRO
A CRO has a time-base of 0.20 ms per division. One complete cycle of a note spans 5.0 divisions. Find the frequency.
T = 5.0 × 0.20 ms = 1.0 ms⇒f = 11.0 × 10⁻³=1000 Hz

Read the width in divisions, multiply by the time-base for the period, then invert.

Worked Example · Wavelength from nodes
A microphone finds that the 1st and the 9th nodes of a stationary sound wave are 1.00 m apart. Find the wavelength.

From the 1st to the 9th node is 8 node spacings, each λ/2.

8 × λ2 = 1.00⇒λ = 2 × 1.008=0.25 m
Worked Example · Speed of sound
A sound of frequency 850 Hz is found to have a wavelength of 0.40 m. Calculate the speed of sound.
v = fλ = 850 × 0.40=340 m s⁻¹

A reassuringly typical value for air at room temperature.

Worked Example · Why nodes, not antinodes
Explain why the positions of the nodes, rather than the antinodes, are used to find the wavelength.

A node is a sharp minimum of sound — the loudness changes quickly on either side, so its position can be located precisely. An antinode is a broad maximum where the loudness barely changes nearby, making its centre hard to pin down. Precise node positions give a more accurate node spacing and hence wavelength.

AS · Standard

The traps on measuring sound. Each card pairs the wrong idea with the correct physics.

"The spacing between nodes is one wavelength."

Adjacent nodes are only half a wavelength apart. You must double the node spacing to get λ.

node spacing = λ/2.
"Any oscilloscope can give the frequency."

Only a calibrated CRO with a known time-base lets you convert the trace width into a period and hence a frequency.

You need the time-base (s per division).
"Antinodes are the quiet points."

Antinodes oscillate with the greatest amplitude — they are the loudest. Nodes are the quiet points.

Antinode = loudest; node = quietest.
"A stationary wave carries energy along, like a progressive wave."

In a pure stationary wave there is no net transfer of energy along it; energy stays stored between the nodes.

No net energy transfer along a stationary wave.
"The microphone measures the wavelength directly."

The microphone only detects loud and quiet points. You find the wavelength from the measured spacing of the nodes.

λ comes from node spacing, not a direct reading.
"Measuring one node spacing is just as good as measuring many."

A single small spacing has a large percentage uncertainty. Measuring across many spacings and dividing reduces it.

Measure across many spacings for accuracy.
