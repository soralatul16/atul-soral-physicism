Theme E.3 · Probability per second

The Decay Law

Half-lives only let you find the activity at whole multiples of the half-life. This part introduces the decay constant λ and the exponential law N = N₀e−λt, which give the number of nuclei — and the activity — at any time.

The decay constant λ is the probability that any one nucleus decays in the next unit of time. It never changes — but because the whole sample decays at a rate proportional to how many nuclei are left, the number falls away exponentially.

What λ means

Each square is a nucleus. The red fraction is the share expected to decay in the next unit of time — that fraction is λ, the same for every nucleus no matter how long it has already survived.

Rate of decay

ΔN/Δt = −λN

Definition and the small-λt caveat

Formally the decay constant is λ = −(ΔN / N) / Δt, with SI unit s⁻¹. It equals the probability of a nucleus decaying per unit time only in the limit of a small λt — over a long interval the number present drops so much that the simple fraction λΔt would overcount the decays.

Because the rate of decay is proportional to the number present (ΔN/Δt = −λN), the quantity falls fastest at the start and ever more slowly after — the signature of exponential decay.

The solution of ΔN/Δt = −λN is the radioactive decay law, N = N₀e−λt. It gives the number of undecayed nuclei at any time, not just at whole half-lives.

N = N₀e−λt

Reading the law

N = N₀e−λt,  λ = ln2 / T½

Derivation · N = N₀e−λt

Reveal one line at a time. While the first step is showing, the graph draws the tangent so you can see that the slope — the rate of decay — is set by how many nuclei remain.

Step 1 · start
dN/dt = −λN
The rate of decay is proportional to the number present. The minus sign says N is falling; the tangent on the graph is steepest when N is large.
Step 2 · separate
(1/N) dN = −λ dt
Gather all the N terms on one side and all the t terms on the other, ready to integrate each side independently.
Step 3 · integrate
∫ (1/N) dN = −λ ∫ dt  ⟹  ln N = −λt + C
The integral of 1/N is ln N; the right side gives −λt plus a constant of integration C.
Step 4 · fix C
at t = 0, N = N₀  ⟹  C = ln N₀
Use the initial condition: at the start there are N₀ nuclei, which pins down the constant.
Step 5 · combine
ln N − ln N₀ = −λt  ⟹  ln(N/N₀) = −λt
Subtracting ln N₀ from both sides turns the two logs into a single log of the ratio.
Step 6 · result
N/N₀ = e−λt  ⟹  N = N₀e−λt
Exponentiating both sides removes the logarithm and leaves the radioactive decay law.

Taking logarithms

Dividing by N₀ gives N/N₀ = e−λt. Taking natural logs of both sides turns the exponential into a straight line: ln(N/N₀) = −λt, or ln N = ln N₀ − λt. That is the trick behind every half-life measurement in Tab 4.

Unlike the whole-number half-life method of Part 2, this works for any time at all — including a single year of a source whose half-life is 5.3 years.

Activity is the rate of decay, A = λN. Since N decays exponentially, so does the activity — and because A is proportional to N, a measured count rate traces the same curve as the number of nuclei.

A = λN = λN₀e−λt

The activity

A = λN,  unit becquerel (Bq)

Derivation · A = λN₀e−λt

Four short lines take you from the definition of activity to its exponential form — and show why the activity curve is just the number curve scaled by λ.

Step 1 · define
A = −dN/dt
Activity is the number of decays per second — the rate at which N falls. The minus sign makes A a positive quantity.
Step 2 · substitute rate
dN/dt = −λN  ⟹  A = −(−λN) = λN
Replace the rate with −λN from the decay law. The two minus signs cancel, giving A = λN.
Step 3 · insert N(t)
N = N₀e−λt  ⟹  A = λN₀e−λt
Substitute the number of nuclei at time t. Activity decays with the same exponential as N.
Step 4 · initial activity
at t = 0, A₀ = λN₀  ⟹  A = A₀e−λt
So A and N share the same half-life; the activity is simply the number of nuclei scaled by the constant λ.

Why activity and number share the same curve

At every instant A = λN, and λ is constant, so activity is just the number of nuclei scaled by λ. Both halve over exactly the same half-life, and both obey A = A₀e−λt with A₀ = λN₀.

This is why you can measure a half-life from a count-rate graph without ever counting the nuclei directly — the shape is identical.

A curve is hard to measure a half-life from. Plot ln N against t instead and the exponential becomes a straight line whose gradient is −λ — the standard way to find a half-life in the lab.

ln N against time

From gradient to half-life

T½ = ln2 / λ

Derivation · T½ = ln2 / λ

The same reveal first straightens the curve into the line you plot, then finds the half-life from it. From Step 3 the graph shades the drop to half so you can see where T½ sits.

Step 1 · start
N = N₀e−λt
Begin from the decay law derived in Tab 2.
Step 2 · linearise
ln N = ln N₀ − λt
Taking logs gives a straight line of gradient −λ — exactly the line on the graph. This is what you plot in the lab.
Step 3 · half remain
at t = T½, N = N₀/2
By definition the half-life is the time for N to fall to half of N₀ — the shaded drop on the graph.
Step 4 · substitute
N₀/2 = N₀e−λT½  ⟹  1/2 = e−λT½
Put N = N₀/2 into the decay law and divide through by N₀.
Step 5 · take logs
ln(1/2) = −λT½  ⟹  −ln2 = −λT½
Since ln(1/2) = −ln2, both minus signs match up.
Step 6 · result
T½ = ln2 / λ
The half-life and decay constant are inversely linked through ln2 ≈ 0.693 — never dependent on N₀.

Measuring a half-life

Record the count rate (proportional to N) at many times, plot ln(count rate) against time, and draw the best straight line. Its gradient is −λ, so λ = −gradient and the half-life follows from T½ = ln2 / λ.

A large λ gives a steep line and a short half-life; a small λ gives a shallow line and a long half-life. The two are inversely linked through ln2 ≈ 0.693.

Four original problems on the decay constant, the decay law and the half-life relationship. Try each before revealing the solution.

Worked Example · Decay constant from activity
A sample contains 5.0 × 10¹² undecayed nuclei and has an activity of 2.0 × 10⁹ Bq. Calculate the decay constant.

Activity and number are linked by A = λN, so λ = A / N.

λ = (2.0 × 10⁹) / (5.0 × 10¹²) = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹

The unit is s⁻¹ because activity is in decays per second and N is a pure number.

Worked Example · Fraction remaining
A radioisotope has a decay constant of 0.030 day⁻¹. What fraction of the original nuclei remains after 50 days?

Use N/N₀ = e−λt with λt = 0.030 × 50 = 1.5.

N/N₀ = e−1.5 = 0.223 ≈ 22%

Note 50 days is not a whole number of half-lives (T½ = ln2/0.030 ≈ 23 days), so only the exponential law gives the answer.

Worked Example · Half-life from λ
A nuclide has a decay constant of 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹. Find its half-life, in seconds and in minutes.
T½ = ln2 / λ = 0.693 / (4.0 × 10⁻⁴) ≈ 1733 s

Dividing by 60: 1733 / 60 ≈ 29 minutes. A larger λ would give a shorter half-life, and vice versa.

Worked Example · Activity at an arbitrary time
A source has an initial activity of 800 Bq and a half-life of 6.0 hours. What is its activity after 10 hours?

First the decay constant: λ = ln2 / T½ = 0.693 / 6.0 = 0.1155 h⁻¹.

A = A₀e−λt = 800 × e−0.1155 × 10
A = 800 × e−1.155 = 800 × 0.315 ≈ 252 Bq

10 hours is 1.67 half-lives — not a whole number — so the halving shortcut cannot be used; the exponential law is essential here.

The traps that catch students on the decay law. Each card pairs the wrong idea with the correct physics.

"A bigger decay constant means a longer half-life."
Larger λ → shorter half-life.
"After a time t = 1/λ, all the nuclei have decayed."

1/λ is the mean lifetime. After it, N has fallen to N₀/e ≈ 37% — about a third still remain, not zero.

At t = 1/λ, about 37% is still left.
"A graph of N against t is a straight line."

N against t is a curve (exponential). It is ln N against t that is a straight line, with gradient −λ.

Only ln N vs t is straight; its slope is −λ.
"λ is just the fraction that decays each second, exactly."

λΔt only approximates the fraction decaying when λt is small. Over longer times the number present changes too much for the simple fraction to hold.

λ ≈ fraction per unit time only for small λt.
"The half-life depends on how many nuclei you start with."

Half-life is fixed by λ alone. Whether you start with a million nuclei or a trillion, each still halves in the same time.

T½ is independent of N₀.
"Activity and number of nuclei follow different curves."

Because A = λN with λ constant, activity is just N scaled by λ. Both follow the same exponential and share the same half-life.

A and N share one decay curve.
