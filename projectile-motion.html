Analysis
Discussion

Why do launch angles of 30° and 60° give the same range? What happens to the time of flight and the greatest height as the angle increases, and why does the horizontal velocity stay constant throughout?

Conclusion

Throw a ball and it does two things at once: it travels forwards at a steady rate, and it rises and falls under gravity. Neither motion cares about the other. That single idea — independence of the horizontal and vertical — explains the whole parabola.

Splitting the launch velocity

A projectile launched at speed u and angle θ has a velocity with two components at right angles to each other:

→

Horizontal, uₓ = u cosθ

There is no horizontal force (ignoring air resistance), so this component never changes. The projectile covers equal horizontal distances in equal times.

↑

Vertical, u_y = u sinθ

Gravity pulls down at g, so this component falls steadily: v_y = u sinθ − g t. It reaches zero at the top, then grows downward.

Time, height and range

T=2usin θg
Time of flight — up and back down again.
H=u2sin2θ2g
Greatest height, reached when v_y = 0.
R=u2sin 2θg
Horizontal range on level ground.

What the angle does

45°

Maximum range

Range depends on sin 2θ, which is largest when 2θ = 90°, i.e. θ = 45°. On level ground, 45° throws the projectile furthest for a given speed.

30°/60°

Complementary angles match

Because sin 2θ = sin(180° − 2θ), any pair of angles adding to 90° (like 30° and 60°) give the same range — one is a low fast throw, the other a high lob.

0°/90°

No range

At 0° there is no upward velocity to keep it in the air; at 90° it goes straight up and lands where it started. Either way sin 2θ = 0, so R = 0.

Launching from a height (off a cliff)

If the projectile leaves from a height h above the ground — thrown horizontally, or angled up or down off an edge — it no longer lands at its launch height, so the simple R = u² sin 2θ / g no longer applies. The two motions are still independent; only the vertical equation gains the starting height:

y(t)=h+usin θ·t−12gt2
Set y = 0 (ground) and solve the quadratic for the flight time T.
R=ux·T
Horizontal range is still just the constant uₓ multiplied by the time in the air.
→

Thrown horizontally (θ = 0)

u_y starts at 0, so T = √(2h/g) — exactly the time to fall height h — and R = u·√(2h/g). Set the angle to 0 and raise the cliff to see it.

↘

Angled below the horizontal (θ < 0)

The projectile starts moving downward, so it lands sooner and the range is shorter than a horizontal throw at the same speed.

↗

Angled above the horizontal (θ > 0)

It first rises to H = h + (u sinθ)²/2g, then falls all the way to the ground. Because of the extra drop h, the angle for maximum range is now less than 45°.

Everyday rule. For the longest throw on flat ground, launch at about 45°. Aim flatter and it lands short; lob it steeper and it hangs in the air but does not travel far. A shot-putter or long-jumper leaves the ground at close to this angle.
Q1 · Define

Write the equations for the horizontal and vertical components of a projectile's launch velocity, and state which one changes during flight.

Horizontal: uₓ = u cosθ; vertical: u_y = u sinθ. The horizontal component stays constant (no horizontal force); the vertical component changes because gravity acts on it: v_y = u sinθ − g t.
Q2 · Independence

Explain what is meant by saying the horizontal and vertical motions of a projectile are independent.

The horizontal motion (constant velocity) and the vertical motion (uniform acceleration g) happen at the same time but neither affects the other. They share only the time t. This is why a ball fired horizontally and one dropped from the same height hit the ground together.
Q3 · Calculate

A ball is launched at 20 m/s at 30° to the horizontal (g = 9.81 m/s²). Find the range on level ground.

R = u² sin 2θ / g = 20² × sin 60° / 9.81 = 400 × 0.866 / 9.81 ≈ 35.3 m.
Q4 · Angle

For the same 20 m/s launch, find the angle that gives the maximum range, and state that range.

Range is greatest at θ = 45° (sin 2θ = 1). Then R = 20² × 1 / 9.81 ≈ 40.8 m. Any other angle gives less.
Q5 · Height & time

For a launch speed of 15 m/s at 50° (g = 9.81 m/s²), find the time of flight and the greatest height.

T = 2u sinθ / g = 2 × 15 × sin 50° / 9.81 ≈ 2.34 s. H = u² sin²θ / (2g) = 225 × sin²50° / 19.62 ≈ 6.73 m.
Q6 · Explain

Why do launch angles of 25° and 65° produce the same range on level ground?

They add to 90°, so their doubled angles (50° and 130°) have equal sines: sin 50° = sin 130°. Since R depends only on sin 2θ (for fixed u and g), both give the same range — the 25° throw is fast and flat, the 65° throw is slow and high. Try both in the simulator.
Q7 · Cliff

A ball is thrown horizontally at 15 m/s from a cliff 20 m high (g = 9.81 m/s²). Find the time to land and the horizontal range.

Horizontally uₓ = 15 m/s, and u_y = 0. Time to fall: T = √(2h/g) = √(2 × 20 / 9.81) ≈ 2.02 s. Range: R = uₓ · T = 15 × 2.02 ≈ 30.3 m. The formula R = u² sin 2θ / g does not apply here because it lands lower than it started — set angle 0 and h = 20 in the simulator to check.
